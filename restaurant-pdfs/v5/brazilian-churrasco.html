Method

1
Combine pão de queijo mix, salt, egg, milk, and oil in a bowl and mix until a smooth, slightly sticky dough forms. The tapioca starch base is why pão de queijo has its unique chew — tapioca starch creates a gelatinous, stretchy crumb that wheat flour cannot replicate. This is a gluten-free product by nature, which is a genuine menu selling point. The dough will be looser than bread dough — this is correct.
2
Fold in grated parmesan first, then mozzarella. Two cheeses serve two purposes: parmesan delivers deep, salty umami flavour through its aged proteins; mozzarella provides the elastic pull and golden surface browning. The interior stays chewy and slightly hollow; this is correct texture.
4
Serve immediately — pão de queijo is best within 5 minutes of leaving the oven. They deflate as they cool and the chew becomes rubbery. For service management, batch-prep raw balls and freeze on trays; transfer to freezer bags. Bake from frozen at 200°C / 392°F for 22 minutes. Frozen-to-oven is the only reliable way to serve pão de queijo at consistent quality during a rush.
Chef's Tip: Pão de queijo is your table greeting — every table gets 4 rolls while they look at the menu. This is not a complimentary item; it is a paid entrée that you upsell at the table. Method

1
Make the dough: simmer chicken stock with butter and salt, bring to a boil, add sifted wheat flour all at once and stir vigorously over heat until the dough pulls cleanly from the pan sides — this is choux-adjacent technique called "cozimento da massa." This cooking-out of the flour gelatinizes the starch and creates a workable, non-sticky dough that holds its teardrop shape during frying. Undercooking the dough means it tears during shaping.
2
Make the filling: sauté onion and garlic until translucent. Add shredded poached chicken thigh, season with salt, pepper, and chopped parsley. Method

1
Desalting bacalhau is a 24–48 hour process: soak in cold water, changing water 3–4 times. The desalting time determines the final salt level of the dish — you cannot season out excess salt after the fritter is made. Taste the soaked cod before using. It should taste pleasantly salty, not overwhelming. The unique flavour of bacalhau comes from the fermentation-like curing process; this funky, oceanic depth is what makes it irreplaceable in this dish.
2
Poach desalted cod in unsalted water with a bay leaf for 8–10 minutes. Remove bones and skin meticulously — any remaining bones are a safety hazard and a guest complaint. Flake into medium shreds (not minced). The potato must be dry: mash while hot, spread on a tray to steam-dry before mixing. Wet potato = fritters that collapse or fall apart in the oil.
3
Combine cod, mashed potato, egg yolk, grated onion, parsley, chives, pepper. Do not add salt — bacalhau provides all necessary salt. Mix by hand until just combined — overworking makes a dense, gluey fritter. The mixture should hold a shape when pressed but still feel light. Portion with two spoons into oval quenelles (about 50g each), which is the traditional shape for bolinho de bacalhau.
4
Deep fry at 180°C / 356°F for 3–4 minutes, turning once, until deep golden brown. The high frying temperature is deliberate — it creates a rapid surface crust that locks in steam and moisture, giving a crispy exterior and a hot, creamy interior. Too low a temperature and the fritters absorb oil and become greasy. Internal: 70°C / 158°F. Drain briefly and serve immediately with herb aioli and lemon.
Chef's Tip: Bacalhau is a prestige ingredient with deep Iberian and Brazilian cultural weight — guests who recognize it will often order it specifically and are typically high-value regulars. Brief your servers: "Bacalhau is salt cod that's been cured and dried — it has a unique concentrated flavour you can't get from fresh fish. It's one of the most important ingredients in Brazilian cuisine." This context elevates the perceived value of a $14 entrée.

Method

1
Render diced bacon in a dry, cold pan — starting in a cold pan renders more fat out slowly, giving crispier bacon bits with less shrinkage. Once crispy, remove bacon and reserve. In the bacon fat + butter, sauté diced onion until translucent and just beginning to brown at the edges (about 4 minutes over medium-high). The Maillard reaction on the onion adds sweetness that balances the saltiness of bacon and butter.
2
Add cassava flour to the pan and toss constantly over medium heat for 3–4 minutes. This toasting step is the heart of farofa — raw cassava flour has a chalky, slightly bitter taste. Toasting in hot fat transforms it: the starch granules toast and develop a nutty, golden flavour, and the texture changes from powder to a coarse, crumbly sand-like consistency. Never skip or rush this step.
3
Push the farofa to one side of the pan. Crack the egg into the cleared space and scramble softly — you want small, moist curds, not dry scramble. Fold the egg into the farofa along with the reserved bacon. The egg adds richness and protein, binding the mixture slightly without making it heavy. Season with salt (carefully — bacon is salty), pepper, and parsley.
4
Serve immediately in a warm bowl. Farofa absorbs moisture quickly and becomes clumpy if it sits. At service, this can be pre-batched up to the toasting stage and finished à la minute with egg and bacon in 3 minutes. For table-service context, farofa is traditionally a side/accompaniment to grilled meats — selling it as an entrée to share adds a cultural story: "This is what every Brazilian home serves alongside their churrasco."
Chef's Tip: Farofa is your highest-margin, most versatile item — it appears as an entrée, a side for mains, and is included in the Feijoada set. The cassava flour has a 6-month shelf life; order in 10lb bags from Latin grocery suppliers for best pricing. The dish tells a story: cassava (mandioca) is the indigenous South American staple that shaped Brazilian cooking. Guests who ask "what IS this?" are guests you can educate and retain.

Method

1
Vinagrete is not a European vinaigrette — it is a Brazilian salsa-salad hybrid. All vegetables must be cut to a uniform small dice (5mm cubes). Uniform dice means uniform marinating and a consistent bite. Large or uneven cuts result in some pieces being under-seasoned while others are over-acidic. The visual precision of a well-cut vinagrete signals kitchen quality to guests; it is one of those dishes where technique is visible.
2
Salt the diced onion, let stand 5 minutes, then rinse under cold water. This step (called "degorging" or "drawing out") removes the harsh, eye-watering raw allium compounds from white onion without cooking it. The result is a milder, cleaner onion flavour that does not overpower the tomato and pepper. Skipping this step is the most common amateur mistake with vinagrete.
3
Combine all diced vegetables in a bowl. Dress with white wine vinegar and extra-virgin olive oil. The ratio is 3:1 vinegar to oil — the opposite of European vinaigrette. Brazilian vinagrete is meant to be sharp and acidic, which is why it works as a counterpoint to rich, fatty grilled meats. Add parsley, season with salt and pepper, toss gently to avoid bruising tomatoes.
4
Marinate for 15–20 minutes before serving — the acid draws out juices from the tomato and pepper, creating a flavourful dressing that enriches the whole dish. Do not marinate more than 2 hours as the vegetables begin to break down and become soggy. Batch-prep diced vegetables in advance; dress per-serve at service. The individual dressed salad should be slightly wet at the base — this is correct and expected.
Chef's Tip: Vinagrete is the churrasco palate cleanser — its sharpness cuts through the fat of picanha and linguiça and refreshes the mouth between bites of grilled meat. Encourage servers to describe it this way: "This is the Brazilian answer to a salsa — it's designed to go alongside the grilled meats, not as a standalone salad." This framing turns a $8 salad into an essential companion item and increases table attachment rate.

Method

1
Clean chicken hearts by trimming any visible fat from the exterior and cutting off the top ~3mm where the aorta attaches. Split each heart in half vertically (butterfly cut) to expose the interior chambers — this serves two functions: it allows any clotted blood to be rinsed out (food safety) and creates a larger flat surface that caramelises better on the grill. Rinse under cold water, pat dry. This step cannot be rushed.

Marinate with olive oil, crushed garlic, lemon juice, black pepper, and a light sprinkle of coarse salt for minimum 30 minutes, maximum 2 hours. The lemon juice tenderises the muscle fibres slightly — chicken hearts are dense and lean, and over-marinating makes them mushy. The oil prevents the lean hearts from drying out on the grill. 3

4
Season with additional coarse sea salt the moment they come off the grill — this is the classic churrasco finishing move. The residual heat dissolves the salt crystals into the surface, creating an intense savoury crust. Serve immediately on the skewer (the visual drama is part of the dish) alongside chimichurri. The skewer presentation is central to churrascaria culture and should never be plated off the skewer for tableside service.

Price confidence is key — $12 for a skewer of chicken hearts is justified by the fire, technique, and experience, not the ingredient cost.

Method

1
Understanding the picanha cut is the foundation of this entire menu. Picanha is the top sirloin cap (biceps femoris), a triangular muscle that sits above the rump, covered by a thick fat cap of 1–2cm. This fat cap is non-negotiable — buying trimmed picanha defeats the purpose. The fat renders during grilling and bastes the meat from above, keeping the interior moist while the exterior chars. Source from Brazilian specialty butchers or ask your supplier for "coulotte" (the French name). USDA Choice or Prime only — the fat marbling is the quality indicator.
2
Season only with coarse sea salt — no pepper, no garlic, no marinades. This is not simplicity by accident; it is deliberate philosophy. The picanha's flavour is in the fat and the fire. Adding other seasonings competes with the meat's inherent sweetness and the Maillard reaction on the fat cap. Score the fat cap in a crosshatch pattern (5mm deep cuts) to prevent curling and allow the salt to penetrate. Apply salt 30 minutes before grilling — the salt draws out surface moisture, which then reabsorbs with the salt dissolved (dry brining).
3
For churrasco style: fold a thick slice (3–4cm) of picanha into a "C" shape with the fat cap on the outside, and skewer through so the skewer holds the fold. This exposes maximum fat cap to the fire while protecting the lean interior. Grill fat-side-down first over intense heat (280°C / 536°F) for 5 minutes to render and char the fat. Then sear the lean sides — 3 minutes each. The goal is a deeply charred fat cap and a medium-rare interior. Internal target: 54–57°C / 129–135°F for medium-rare.
4
Rest for 5 minutes minimum — the muscle fibres need time to relax and redistribute juices. Slicing immediately loses 30–40% of the moisture as runoff. Slice against the grain in 1cm slices at the table if you have a carving setup, or in the kitchen against the grain. Serve fat-side-up so the guest sees the rendered, charred fat cap — this is the visual signature of authentic picanha. Plate with chimichurri, vinagrete, and a small portion of farofa.
Chef's Tip: The single biggest mistake new operators make with picanha is trimming the fat cap. Never trim it. The fat cap IS the product — it protects, bastes, flavours, and provides the visual identity. A trimmed picanha is just a sirloin steak. If your supplier delivers pre-trimmed, find a new supplier. Source whole picanha weighing 1–1.5kg; portion at 280–300g per serve after trimming any silver skin from the lean side only. Yield is approximately 85% from a good-quality whole picanha.

Method

1
Flank steak has a very pronounced longitudinal grain — the muscle fibres run clearly in one direction along the length of the steak. Identifying and understanding this grain before cooking is essential, because slicing direction determines whether the steak is tender or impossibly chewy. Long muscle fibres = tough; cutting those fibres short = tender. Before marinating, feel the steak and identify which direction the grain runs — you will be cutting perpendicular to this at service.
2
Marinate with olive oil, minced garlic, lemon juice, cumin, salt, and pepper for 2–4 hours. Flank is a lean, flavourful cut with minimal intramuscular fat, so it benefits from a marinade in ways that fattier cuts like picanha do not. The acid (lemon) gently denatures surface proteins for better sear; the oil prevents sticking and carries oil-soluble aromatics into the surface. Do not marinate more than 6 hours — the lemon will begin to "cook" the surface proteins and cause a mushy exterior texture.
3
Grill on maximum heat (260°C / 500°F) — do not move during searing. Flank steak needs direct, intense heat to create a fast crust before the interior overcooks (it is a thin cut, typically 2–3cm). 4–5 minutes per side for medium-rare to medium. Internal: 55–60°C / 131–140°F. The steak should have deep char marks and a juicy interior. Avoid medium-well or well-done — fraldinha becomes dry and tough above 65°C.
4
Rest 4 minutes, then slice thinly (<5mm) on a 45° angle, strictly against the grain. The diagonal angle gives wider slices with shorter grain length — this is both visually appealing and maximally tender. Arrange overlapping slices across the plate, exposing the pink interior. The visual contrast of the charred exterior and pink interior signals correct cooking and drives satisfaction. Serve with chimichurri and a scoop of arroz e feijão.
Chef's Tip: Fraldinha is your entry-level steak for guests who are not yet ready to commit to the premium picanha price. It is also your best value proposition — a $27 grilled steak that competes on experience with a $50+ steakhouse item when cooked correctly. Cross-sell at the table: "The fraldinha is our everyday steak — fantastic flavour at a great price point. If you'd like the signature experience, the picanha is our showpiece cut." This creates an upgrade path, not a downgrade.

Method

1
Beef short rib (costela) is a collagen-rich cut with significant connective tissue between and around the meat. This connective tissue (primarily collagen) is what makes it tough when undercooked, but the transformation is remarkable: at sustained low heat, collagen hydrolyzes into gelatin over 5–7 hours, creating the silky, richly unctuous texture that makes costela one of the most desirable dishes in Brazilian cuisine. You are essentially cooking away toughness and replacing it with luxury. This transformation cannot be rushed — high heat simply dries out the muscle fibres before the collagen breaks down.
2
Apply rub (coarse salt + cracked black pepper + garlic powder) the night before and refrigerate uncovered. The overnight dry rub does two things: the salt draws out surface moisture which then reabsorbs with dissolved seasonings (dry brine effect), and the open-air refrigeration dries the surface, which dramatically improves bark formation during the cook. Bark (the dark, crusty exterior) is the hallmark of properly smoked costela and is where 80% of the flavour complexity lives.
3
Cook in your smoker or low oven at 120°C / 248°F for 6–7 hours. Add hardwood chunks (cherry, hickory, or oak) for smoke. The 120°C target is critical — it is above the temperature where collagen converts (70°C+) but below the temperature where the muscle fibres contract and dry out sharply (above 145°C sustained). Monitor with a probe thermometer. The ribs are done when the internal temperature reaches 93–97°C / 200–205°F AND when a skewer slides through the meat with zero resistance. Temperature alone is insufficient — trust the texture test.
4
Rest wrapped in butcher paper for 45 minutes — the carry-over cooking completes, and the juices redistribute. For service, flash on a very hot grill (280°C / 536°F) for 2–3 minutes to re-crisp the bark and add fresh grill marks. This "finish grill" is what separates a restaurant costela from home cooking — the contrast of crackling bark and molten interior is what guests will talk about. Slice or pull at the table if possible — the theatre is powerful.
Chef's Tip: Costela is a high-ticket, high-margin showpiece — but it requires 7am prep for lunch service. Your Head Cook starts the ribs first thing at 7am and the smoking is largely passive. Factor this into your prep flow: the ribs run in the background while everything else is being prepared. Batch cook 8–10 portions at once; leftovers can become the Feijoada filling or a next-day special. Never sell costela unless it has had full cook time — a 4-hour rib is tough and will damage your reputation.

Sourcing linguiça: authentic Brazilian linguiça (pronounced "lin-GWEE-sah") is distinctly different from Portuguese linguiça or Spanish chorizo. Serve whole links on a wooden board — never cut in the kitchen. The guest should hear and see the sausage arrive sizzling. Cutting in the kitchen loses steam and visual drama. Method

1
Quindim has African-Brazilian-Portuguese origins and is one of the most technically interesting desserts in the Brazilian canon. The bright yellow colour comes entirely from egg yolks — no colouring. The density comes from the high yolk-to-liquid ratio. The distinct two-layer structure (clear coconut jelly base with a dense custard top when unmolded) happens naturally during baking, as the shredded coconut floats to the bottom of the mold (which becomes the top when inverted). Understanding this physics means you will not be confused by the baking process.
2
Beat egg yolks with caster sugar until the mixture is pale and thickened slightly — this is called "ribbon stage" and takes 3–4 minutes. The purpose: beating air into the yolks while dissolving the sugar creates a structure that prevents the quindim from becoming grainy or separated. Add coconut milk and shredded coconut and stir gently to combine. The shredded coconut is left whole (not blended in) — it will separate during baking to create the characteristic base layer.
3
Butter individual molds (small ramekins or mini bundt molds) and coat with sugar — this sugar coating prevents sticking and creates a slight caramelised surface on the unmolded quindim. Fill each mold to 80% capacity. Bake bain-marie at 160°C / 320°F for 25–30 minutes. The quindim is set when the edges are firm and the centre has the slightest wobble. The surface should be matte yellow, not golden — browning indicates overbaking and a rubbery texture.
4
Cool to room temperature, then refrigerate overnight. Unmold by running a thin blade around the edge and inverting onto a small plate. The quindim will release with a slight resistance and land with the glossy clear coconut layer on top — this is correct. Garnish with a pinch of toasted coconut shreds. Serve cold — the texture at room temperature becomes too soft. The quindim should feel dense and almost sticky to the touch of a spoon, cutting cleanly without crumbling.
Chef's Tip: Quindim is your most visually distinctive dessert — the bright chrome-yellow dome with a glossy surface looks unlike anything in any other cuisine. Photograph it well for your menu and social media. When guests ask "what is that?" — and they will — the answer is your brand story: "It's a quindim — it comes from West African custard recipes brought to Brazil centuries ago, made with coconut and egg yolks from the colonial Portuguese kitchen. There's nothing else like it." This history converts curious guests into customers.

5 Kitchen Prep Flow — 7am to 3pm

The Head Cook's first act every morning is lighting the charcoal or hardwood — it takes 45–60 minutes to reach cooking temperature, and the costela goes on within the first 30 minutes of opening prep. The grill never goes cold during service.

When to use: When guests sit down and look confused by the menu format, or ask "is this a rodízio?"

Server script: "Great question — we do à la carte here rather than the traditional rodízio, which means you choose exactly what you'd like rather than the continuous all-you-can-eat service. [Point to menu.] Most guests start with the pão de queijo from the oven and one of the entrées, then one of the grill mains. Does that sound right for you?"

Why this works: It pre-emptively handles the "this isn't a churrascaria rodízio" objection, positions your format as premium rather than lesser, and immediately moves toward an order.

Script 2: The Picanha Story (Why It's the King Cut)

When to use: When a guest is deciding between mains, or asks "what's the picanha?"

Server script: "The picanha is the most beloved cut in Brazilian churrasco — it's the top sirloin cap, which sits just above the rump on the back of the animal. What makes it special is this fat cap: [draw a finger across the top of the menu to show a cap] — it stays on during grilling and basically self-bastes the steak as it renders over the fire. Brazilians have been cooking it over hardwood since the 1970s, and once you've had it, it's hard to go back to a regular steak. It's $32.99 and absolutely worth it."

Why this works: The visual gesture (miming the fat cap), the heritage reference, and the confident price anchoring all prime the guest to say yes.

Script 3: The Caipirinha Making Theatre
