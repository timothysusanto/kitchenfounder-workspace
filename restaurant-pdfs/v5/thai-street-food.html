About This Guide: Thai cuisine is one of the most commercially successful food categories in the US market. The flavour profile — simultaneously salty (fish sauce), sweet (palm sugar), sour (lime/tamarind), spicy (fresh chili) — is uniquely addictive and difficult to replicate at home without the right ingredients and technique. This creates genuine repeat business. The key differentiator for a serious Thai café is house-made curry paste (not jarred), fish sauce from quality producers, and fresh aromatics (lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaves) used daily. Method

1
Mince fish in a food processor to a smooth paste — not chunky. The smooth paste is what gives tod mun its characteristic bouncy, dense texture (called Q texture in Thai food culture). Add curry paste, kaffir lime leaves, fish sauce, egg. Process briefly to combine. Fold in sliced long beans by hand — they provide crunch and visual texture contrast.
2
The mixture should be sticky and hold its shape when pressed. Form into flat discs (30g each, 1cm thick). Wet hands prevent sticking. The flat disc shape ensures even cooking — a ball-shaped fish cake has a raw centre while the exterior is overcooked.
3
Shallow fry in oil at 175°C / 347°F for 2–3 minutes per side until deep golden. Internal temp: 70°C / 158°F. Drain briefly. The cakes should have a crispy exterior and a springy, dense interior — not soft or mushy.
4
Cucumber relish (ajat): rice vinegar + sugar + salt + water, warm to dissolve. Add diced cucumber, shallot, fresh chili, cilantro. The relish must be sharp and cold to contrast with the hot, oily fish cakes. Never serve warm relish with tod mun.
Chef's Tip: Kaffir lime leaves are irreplaceable in this dish — their distinctive floral, citrusy intensity is what makes tod mun smell and taste Thai. No other ingredient substitutes. Buy fresh or frozen from Asian grocery suppliers. Dried kaffir lime leaves have almost no flavour. If you cannot get fresh or frozen, do not make tod mun.

Method

1
Som tum is made in a mortar and pestle — this is non-negotiable for authentic texture and flavour. A food processor creates a wet, uniform mash. The mortar bruises and crushes the ingredients, releasing their flavours while maintaining textural integrity. Every Thai street food vendor uses a specific clay mortar (khrok din) for this purpose.
2
Pound garlic and chilies first until roughly broken. Add palm sugar, pound to dissolve. Add long beans and tomatoes — pound gently (you want them bruised and partially crushed, not destroyed). Add shredded papaya. Pound and mix with a spoon simultaneously — this is the technique. Fish sauce and lime juice added last.
3
The flavour balance is the entire skill of som tum: spicy (chili) + sweet (palm sugar) + sour (lime) + salty (fish sauce) must all be present and in balance. Taste after every addition. Adjust with individual components. A som tum that is too sweet is flat; too sour is harsh; too salty is unpleasant; not spicy enough is not Thai.
4
Serve immediately — the papaya releases liquid quickly and the salad becomes soggy within 20 minutes. Never make ahead. Transfer from mortar directly to the plate. Scatter crushed peanuts at service — not in the mortar (they soften).
Chef's Tip: Spice level is the most important guest interaction for this dish. Ask every table: "How spicy? Thai-spicy means 5–7 bird's eye chilies. Mild means 1–2." Never assume. Thai bird's eye chilies are among the hottest commercially available chilies — a guest who expected mild and received Thai-spicy will not come back.

Method

1
Slice chicken thigh thinly — 5mm strips — and thread onto bamboo skewers in a ribbon/wave pattern (thread in and out multiple times, not just once). The wave threading keeps the meat flat against the grill for maximum contact and caramelisation. Soak skewers 30 min before use to prevent burning.
2
Marinade: lemongrass + turmeric + coriander + cumin + coconut milk + palm sugar + fish sauce. Marinate minimum 2 hours. The turmeric gives the characteristic golden colour; the lemongrass gives the aromatic identity. Satay without lemongrass is just grilled chicken with spices.
3
Grill on a very hot charcoal or gas grill — 220°C / 428°F. Turn once after 3 minutes. 2–3 minutes more. Internal: 74°C / 165°F. Brush with remaining coconut milk marinade during grilling for gloss and flavour.
4
Peanut sauce (batch): fry red curry paste in oil 2 minutes. Add coconut milk, simmer. Add peanut butter (or ground roasted peanuts), palm sugar, fish sauce, tamarind. Simmer until thick and glossy. The sauce should be rich, nutty, slightly sweet-spicy, and thick enough to coat a spoon.
Chef's Tip: Satay is your entry-level item — familiar to guests who've had minimal Thai food, beloved by everyone who knows it. It is also your most versatile cross-sell: "Start with the satay while you wait for your pad thai." This framing turns satay into a table-management tool that keeps guests happy during longer cooking items and increases average spend.

Method

1
Tom yum broth (large batch — make daily): simmer prawn heads/shells in water 20 minutes to make prawn stock. Add bruised lemongrass (3 stalks, cut in thirds), sliced galangal (not ginger — galangal has a sharper, more piney flavour; ginger is a different ingredient entirely), kaffir lime leaves, Thai chilies. Simmer 15 minutes. Add fish sauce, lime juice, and a small amount of nam prik pao (roasted chili paste) — this gives the soup its characteristic red-orange colour and smoky depth.
2
Service: bring broth to a boil. Add mushrooms, cook 2 minutes. Add prawns — cook just until pink and curled, 2–3 minutes maximum. Overcooked prawns in a hot soup become rubbery within minutes.
3
Final seasoning: add fresh lime juice and fish sauce at the end, after removing from heat. The fresh lime must be added off the heat — boiled lime juice loses its bright, volatile citrus character and becomes flat and slightly bitter.
Chef's Tip: The galangal and lemongrass in tom yum are aromatics, not food — they are there to flavour the broth and should not be eaten. Brief FOH on this: "The lemongrass pieces and the round ginger-like pieces are for flavour only — just move them to the side." This simple instruction prevents the complaint "I bit into something hard and inedible."

Method

Miang kham is one of the great Thai one-bite experiences — a leaf wrap containing a burst of multiple flavours simultaneously. Method

1
Marinate diced chicken in oyster sauce, soy, sesame oil, pounded garlic and coriander root. Rest 2 hours. The coriander root (not the leaves) is a Thai pantry essential — its flavour is earthier and more intense than the leaf. Pound it to a paste with the garlic.
2
Wrap each chicken piece in a pandan leaf — fold into a neat package and secure with a toothpick if needed. The pandan leaf does not impart intense flavour during shallow frying (its full flavour requires longer steaming or direct contact), but it perfumes the chicken subtly, creates a dramatic presentation, and protects the chicken from direct oil contact.
3
Deep fry at 175°C / 347°F for 5–6 minutes until the pandan leaf is dark green and slightly charred at the edges and the chicken is cooked through. The visual — glossy green parcels on a plate — is immediately striking and distinctive.
Chef's Tip: Pandan chicken is one of the most visually distinctive Thai appetisers. The deep green, glossy parcels look elegant and unusual — guests invariably ask what they are when they see them on a neighbouring table. This is your silent marketing item. Always run it and place it at visible tables.

Method

1
Pad thai sauce (batch): tamarind paste + fish sauce + palm sugar (3:2:2 ratio). Simmer until palm sugar dissolves. Taste — should be simultaneously sour (tamarind), salty (fish sauce), sweet (palm sugar). Balance these three. This sauce is the DNA of the dish — jarred "pad thai sauce" is an inferior shortcut that produces a sweeter, less complex result.
2
Wok hei is the goal. Use the hottest burner you have at maximum heat. Wok must be ripping hot before oil goes in. Stir-fry shallots, dried shrimp, preserved radish briefly. Push to the side. Add shrimp, cook 60 seconds. Push to side. Add noodles and sauce — toss rapidly. Push everything to the side, crack eggs into the empty pan centre, scramble partially, fold through the noodles.
3
Add bean sprouts at the last 30 seconds — they should still have crunch when the dish is served. Overcooked bean sprouts are limp and watery. Toss once more. Plate immediately. The entire cook from a hot wok takes 4–5 minutes maximum.
4
Serve with condiment tray: chili flakes, extra fish sauce, white sugar, white vinegar. This is standard Thai restaurant service — guests season to their own taste. The four condiments represent the four Thai flavours and are a cultural touchstone that communicates authenticity.
Chef's Tip: One wok, one portion at a time. Pad thai made in batches or for multiple portions simultaneously has no wok hei — the temperature drops and everything steams. This is why restaurant pad thai tastes different from home pad thai: the single-portion, screaming-hot wok. This is non-negotiable. Your wok station should be able to produce one pad thai every 5 minutes during peak service.

Method

1
House green curry paste (batch — makes 10+ portions): pound in a mortar: green bird's eye chilies, lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime zest, coriander root, garlic, shallot, shrimp paste, cumin, coriander seeds, white pepper. The mortar produces a paste with more texture and aroma than a blended paste — the pounding ruptures the cell walls and releases volatile oils that blending does not.
2
Fry curry paste in the coconut cream (top layer of unshaken coconut milk — thick, fatty) over medium heat until the paste is fragrant and the oil separates from the paste (3–4 minutes). This step — "cracking the coconut cream" — is essential. An un-fried paste produces a flat curry; a properly fried paste produces a rich, aromatic curry.
3
Add remaining coconut milk, bring to simmer. Add chicken and eggplant. Cook 8–10 minutes until chicken is cooked through (74°C / 165°F) and eggplant is tender but not falling apart. Season with fish sauce and palm sugar. Add torn kaffir lime leaves and Thai basil at the end — never cook basil; add off heat.
Chef's Tip: The colour of a house green curry paste is the most immediate visual indicator of quality. A paste made with fresh green chilies, fresh lemongrass, and fresh herbs should be vivid emerald green. Jarred paste is olive drab. The colour difference between a house-made paste and a commercial one is visible from across the room. This visual alone justifies the price premium to any guest who notices.

Method

1
Massaman is a slow-cook curry — it cannot be made to order. Braise beef chuck (2cm cubes) in massaman paste + coconut milk + water at 150°C / 302°F for 2.5–3 hours until the beef is completely tender and the sauce is thick and rich. The extended braising time allows the connective tissue to dissolve into gelatin, creating the silky, clinging sauce that defines massaman.
2
Massaman paste: dried red chilies, roasted shallots, roasted garlic, lemongrass, galangal, coriander seeds, cumin, cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg, shrimp paste. The warm spice profile (cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg) distinguishes massaman from other Thai curries — it reflects the Muslim-influenced Southern Thai heritage of the dish.
3
Add potatoes for the final 30 minutes — they absorb the curry sauce and become deeply flavoured. Add peanuts. Adjust seasoning: tamarind for acidity, palm sugar for sweetness, fish sauce for salt. Massaman should be rich, slightly sweet, gently spiced (not fiercely hot), and deeply savoury.
Chef's Tip: Massaman is famously described as one of the world's best foods — it was ranked number one by CNN Travel in 2011 and the discussion never really ended. Mention this context: "This is the curry that CNN called the best food in the world." It is not an overstatement, it is a genuine reference, and it immediately frames the dish as worth ordering.

Method

1
Pound garlic and bird's eye chilies coarsely in a mortar. The pounding releases more volatile oils than mincing — pad kra pao from pounded aromatics is more fragrant than from knife-cut aromatics. Heat wok to maximum. Oil. Add garlic-chili paste — cook 30 seconds, stirring rapidly (do not burn the garlic).
2
Add minced pork — stir-fry on maximum heat, breaking up any clumps. Cook until pork is fully cooked and beginning to caramelise at the edges. Add oyster sauce, soy sauce, fish sauce, dark soy, sugar. Stir to coat.
3
Add holy basil at the very last moment — toss 3–4 times and immediately plate. Holy basil cooks extremely fast and loses its distinctive peppery, anise-like flavour within 20 seconds of heat exposure. It is added last and the dish goes to the plate immediately after.
4
The fried egg: deep fry in about 1cm of hot oil — pour oil over the top while frying to cook the white while the edges crisp and blister. This Thai-style fried egg (blistered, lacey edges, runny yolk) is completely different from a Western fried egg and is an essential visual and textural element of the dish. When the runny yolk breaks over the basil pork, it creates a sauce.
Chef's Tip: Pad kra pao is the most-eaten dish in Thailand — it is what Thais eat when they want something fast, satisfying, and uncomplicated. It is the national comfort food. Holy basil (kra pao) is not the same as sweet Italian basil — holy basil has a peppery, slightly clove-like character that is completely different. This dish made with sweet basil is not pad kra pao. Source holy basil from Asian grocers or grow it yourself.

Method

1
Poach whole chicken breasts in aromatic stock (ginger, garlic, lemongrass, salt) at 82°C / 180°F — below a boil. Boiling causes the proteins to contract violently, producing tough, stringy chicken. Sub-boil poaching (called gentle poaching) produces silky, juicy chicken with a smooth texture that slides easily off the bone.
2
Chicken fat rice: render chicken fat (from the poaching liquid surface) with garlic and ginger. Toast jasmine rice in this fat 2 minutes. Cook in the poaching stock 1:1.5 ratio. The rice absorbs the poaching liquid's flavour — it is savoury, silky, and has a depth that plain steamed rice cannot achieve.
3
Slice chicken cold (against the grain for tenderness). Arrange on rice. Serve with a small bowl of clear broth, ginger-garlic sauce (ginger + garlic + sugar + soy + vinegar + sesame oil), fermented soybean sauce, cucumber slices, cilantro, fried garlic. The multiple dipping sauces are part of the eating ritual.
Chef's Tip: Khao man gai is the most underrepresented great dish in Thai restaurants outside Thailand. In Bangkok, entire restaurants serve only this dish, all day, every day. It is clean, satisfying, and technically demanding in a subtle way. Guests who have eaten it in Thailand will specifically seek it out. Guests who haven't will be surprised by how compelling simplicity can be.

Method

1
Pad see ew requires the hottest possible wok heat for the characteristic charred soy flavour (wok hei). Separate fresh noodles gently by hand before cooking — fresh wide rice noodles clump together in the package and will tear if not separated carefully. Never use dried noodles for this dish — the texture is completely different.
2

3

Chef's Tip: The dark caramelised colour of well-made pad see ew — almost black in spots, deep amber through the noodles — is the visual signal of quality. A pale pad see ew was not cooked on a hot enough wok. The dark soy and the wok heat together are responsible for both colour and flavour. Get the heat right and the colour follows.

Method

1
Thai fried rice is simpler and less soy-forward than Chinese fried rice — the seasonings are fish sauce + a little soy + a touch of sugar. The character is lighter and more fragrant. 7 Service Training Module

Script 1 — The Tea Opening

"Can I get you a Thai iced tea while you look at the menu? We brew it strong with the traditional Thai tea leaves — it's sweet, slightly spiced, orange. Or the butterfly pea flower tea — it's blue until you put lime in it and it turns purple. $5.99 each."

Why it works: The butterfly pea tea colour change is the most reliably effective drink sell in Thai food. The chemistry demo converts almost every table that hears it.

Script 2 — The Spice Conversation

"Before I take your order — how spicy do you like things? Thai spicy means bird's eye chilies, which are legitimately hot. Medium means 2–3. Thai means 5–7. I always ask because the gap between medium and Thai is significant."

Why it works: Proactive spice management prevents complaints, creates a collaborative dynamic, and demonstrates that you know your product. Guests who feel informed don't complain even if it's spicier than expected.

Script 3 — The Som Tum Mortar

"The som tum is made in the mortar — we make each one fresh to order. 6 minutes. The heat level is completely adjustable. $12.99 and it's probably the most flavourful $13 you'll spend today."

Why it works: The mortar detail communicates authenticity immediately. "Made fresh to order" is both honest and a quality signal. "Most flavourful $13 you'll spend today" is a confident, memorable line.

Script 4 — The Massaman Story

"The massaman — CNN called it the best food in the world a few years back. It's a slow-braised beef curry, Southern Thai, Muslim-influenced, so it has warm spices — cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg — rather than just chili heat. It's not aggressively spicy. $19.99 with jasmine rice."

Why it works: The CNN reference is a genuine, memorable fact. Explaining the Muslim Southern Thai influence adds cultural depth. "Not aggressively spicy" removes the barrier for spice-uncertain guests.

Script 5 — The Fried Banana Pre-Sell

"One thing worth knowing — the fried banana with coconut ice cream is 6 minutes if you want it at the end. I'll put it on when I bring your mains so it times right. $8.99. The batter has coconut and sesame in it — it's not like a funfair doughnut."

Why it works: Pre-ordering removes the "too full" barrier. The coconut-sesame batter detail distinguishes it from cheap fried desserts. Timing it with the mains is a genuine service act that guests appreciate.

8 Customer Retention System

The Four Pillars Principle: Every dish on this menu must deliver all four Thai flavours simultaneously — salty, sweet, sour, spicy. When all four are present and balanced, the palate experiences a satisfaction that is unique to Thai cuisine and is literally addictive at a neurological level. This is why people return to Thai restaurants more frequently than almost any other cuisine category. Your retention strategy is built on this flavour addiction — ensure every dish is perfectly balanced, every time.
The Discovery Ladder: Most non-Thai guests start with pad thai (safest) and progress over visits to green curry → massaman → pad kra pao → khao man gai → som tum. Each step up the ladder deepens their connection to the cuisine. Staff should actively guide this progression: "You loved the green curry last time — have you tried the massaman? It's completely different." Track what regulars have tried and what they haven't.

5 VIP Micro-Moments

1. The Prawn Cracker Welcome. Prawn crackers with sweet chili sauce on the table within 60 seconds of seating. Cost: $0.15. Impact: every Thai restaurant in Bangkok does this. It is the universal signal that the meal has started and the hospitality is genuine. Guests who haven't been to a proper Thai restaurant will not expect it — it immediately exceeds their baseline.

2. The Butterfly Pea Colour Change. When the tea arrives, say: "Squeeze the lime in — it turns purple." Watch them do it. Watch their face. This is a 5-second moment that generates a genuine emotional response. Take a photo of their reaction if they're comfortable with it. This is your social media content and your retention anchor in one moment.

3. The Mortar Presence. If you have counter or pass visibility, keep the mortar in sight. The sound of pounding, the visual of the cook working the mortar for each som tum order — this communicates authenticity more effectively than any menu description. Sound and sight together are the two most powerful restaurant marketing tools and they are completely free.

4. The Regional Story. When a regular has tried the core menu items, introduce them to regional Thai cuisine: "Have you had Isaan food? It's from Northeast Thailand — more sour, more fermented fish. Very different from Central Thai." This conversation establishes you as a knowledgeable guide to a deep subject, not just a pad thai dispensary. Depth creates loyalty.

5. The Spice Progression. For guests who always order mild: "Last time you had medium on the green curry — want to try medium-hot this time? I promise I won't push past your limit." This gentle progression toward authentic spice levels creates a shared journey. Guests who successfully eat spicier food than they thought they could feel accomplished and will return to push further. You are their guide.
