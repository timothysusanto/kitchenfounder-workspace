🇫🇷 The Story Behind This Guide
"A brasserie is not a bistro and not a restaurant gastronomique. It is something specific: generous, confident, democratic. Getting that positioning right is the whole business."

Claire had spent three years at culinary school in Lyon and four years working in Parisian kitchens before she moved to Chicago at 30 to join her partner. She arrived with a deep technical foundation — classical French sauces, proper brunoise, the hierarchy of stocks — and a clear vision: not a fine dining room, not a crepe stand. A brasserie. The Parisian institution that is somehow both everyday and excellent, the place where a worker eats lunch and an anniversary is celebrated and neither party feels out of place.

Chicago's French restaurant landscape was either too formal or not French enough. She saw the gap. What she did not have was the operational language to explain the concept to a bank, to a landlord, to the staff she would need to hire and train. She understood the food. She needed the business architecture around it.

The guide's steak frites section — the resting instruction explained with the reason (the myoglobin relaxes and reabsorbs the juices that have been pushed to the surface by heat; cutting immediately loses 30% of the moisture content), the exact internal temperatures for each doneness level, the frite technique that produces the correct exterior crispness — was the moment she knew this guide was built on real kitchen knowledge. She trusted every number after that.

She opened a 46-seat brasserie in River North. The French onion soup is made with a 4-hour caramelised onion base. The croque monsieur uses Emmental and a proper bechamel, never processed cheese. Month five net: $52,000. French cooking is not complicated. It is precise. This guide is that precision, made operational.

Method

1
Slice onions thinly — 3mm. Melt butter in a heavy-based pot over medium heat. Add all onions. Cook uncovered, stirring every 5–8 minutes. The goal is true caramelisation — minimum 45–60 minutes. The onions must go from white to pale gold to deep amber-brown. This is the entire flavour of the soup — there is no shortcut. Adding sugar or bicarbonate of soda accelerates browning but produces artificial sweetness, not the complex depth of real caramelisation.
2
When onions are deeply caramelised, add white wine. Scrape all the fond (caramelised bits stuck to the pot) — this is concentrated flavour that must not be lost. Simmer until wine is mostly evaporated. Add beef stock, thyme, bay leaf. Simmer 30 minutes. Season. The soup should taste deeply savoury, sweet from the onions, and rounded — not sharp or thin.
3
Toast baguette slices under a grill at 220°C / 428°F until hard and golden — the crouton must be completely dry and crisp so it floats on the soup rather than sinking and going soggy immediately. Pile grated gruyère generously over the crouton — do not be conservative. The cheese is the visual wow of this dish.
4
Ladle hot soup into an oven-safe crock or bowl. Float the crouton on top. Place under a salamander or hot grill at 230°C / 446°F for 3–4 minutes until the gruyère is fully melted, bubbling, and spotting brown at the edges. Serve immediately — the crust softens quickly. Warn guests the bowl is extremely hot.
Chef's Tip: The single most common failure in French onion soup is not caramelising the onions long enough. Light golden onions produce a thin, slightly sweet soup. Deep amber-brown onions produce the complex, almost meaty, intense brasserie soup that guests remember for years. If your caramelisation takes less than 45 minutes, your heat was too high and you steamed the onions rather than caramelising them.

Method

1
Béchamel (batch — make morning): Melt 50g butter in a heavy saucepan. Add 50g plain flour all at once, stir vigorously over medium heat for 2 minutes — cooking out the raw flour flavour (this is the roux). Add warm milk 500ml in 3 stages, whisking continuously after each addition. The sequential addition and continuous whisking is what produces a smooth, lump-free sauce — adding all the milk at once creates clumps that are difficult to remove. Season with salt, white pepper, and a pinch of nutmeg. Cook until thick — the béchamel should coat the back of a spoon thickly. Makes enough for 12 serves.
2
Spread Dijon mustard on one inner face. Layer ham on the mustard side. Spread a thin layer of béchamel on the ham. Add a layer of grated gruyère. Close sandwich. Butter the outer faces of both slices of brioche.
3
Griddle at 160°C / 320°F with butter, pressing with a sandwich press for 3–4 minutes per side until golden. The lower temperature ensures the gruyère inside fully melts before the outside burns.
4
Transfer to a baking tray. Spread remaining béchamel and gruyère on the top face. Place under grill at 220°C / 428°F for 2 minutes until the top is bubbling, golden, and spotting brown. This gratin top is what makes a croque monsieur — without it, you simply have a toasted ham and cheese sandwich.
Chef's Tip: Use brioche, not sandwich bread. Brioche has a rich, buttery crumb that absorbs butter during griddling and becomes crisp outside while remaining soft inside. Sandwich bread dries out and becomes brittle. The bread quality is visible and tasted in every bite — source properly.

Method

1
Source beef that is fresh — ordered same-day from a trusted butcher. Tartare is raw meat; the quality and freshness of the product is the entire dish. Keep beef refrigerated at 1–3°C until the moment of preparation. Never use ground beef from a supermarket — the surface area exposure during grinding multiplies bacterial risk dramatically. Hand-chopped whole muscle is the only correct method.
2
Chill the knife and cutting board in the fridge before use — cold equipment keeps the beef cold during prep. Remove sinew and silver skin from beef. Slice thinly, then chop across the slices into a fine dice — approximately 3–4mm pieces. Do not mince to a paste; the texture of small, distinct pieces is what gives tartare its signature bite.
3
Mix Dijon, Tabasco, Worcestershire in a bowl. Add chopped beef, capers, cornichons, shallot. Season generously with salt and pepper. Mix briefly — do not overwork. Taste and adjust. Season assertively — raw beef needs more seasoning than cooked beef because there is no Maillard flavour to carry the dish. The tartare should taste vivid and complete on its own, before the egg yolk is added.
4
Using a ring mould (8cm), press tartare into a neat cylinder on a cold plate. Remove mould. Create a small well in the centre. Place egg yolk in the well. Arrange baguette toasts beside. Present to the guest as-is — the egg yolk is folded in at the table, either by staff or the guest. This tableside interaction is part of the theatre of tartare.
Chef's Tip: Make tartare maximum 15 minutes before service — never in advance. The acid from the capers and cornichons begins to "cook" the beef surface (ceviche effect) within 20 minutes, turning the colour grey and the texture slightly mushy. Fresh tartare should be bright red-pink throughout. If it is grey, it was made too long ago.

Method (batch — make weekly, serves 20+)

1
Grind 800g pork shoulder coarsely, 200g pork belly (for fat content). The ratio of lean to fat must be approximately 70:30 — fat is what carries flavour and keeps the pâté moist and sliceable. All-lean pâté is dry and crumbly and will not hold shape.
2
Sauté 1 onion + 3 garlic cloves until soft. Cool. Mix into the pork with: 2 eggs, 30ml cognac (or brandy), 10g salt, 3g black pepper, 2g allspice, 2g thyme. Mix thoroughly — this farce must be seasoned generously; pâté is eaten cold and cold food requires more seasoning than hot food.
3
Line a loaf tin with streaky bacon strips, overlapping. Pack pâté mixture in firmly, tapping the tin to remove air pockets. Fold over the bacon. Cover tightly with foil. Bake in a water bath at 160°C / 320°F for 90 minutes. Internal temp: 72°C / 162°F. Cool, press with a weight overnight in the fridge — pressing gives a dense, sliceable texture.
4
To serve: slice 1.5cm thick. Arrange on a board or slate with toasted sourdough, cornichons, Dijon, and cold butter. The pâté should have a dense, spreadable texture — rich, savoury, with the back-note of brandy and herbs. The cornichon's tartness is the essential counterpoint to the richness.
Chef's Tip: Season a small amount of the raw farce before the full batch goes in the tin — fry 10g in a pan, taste it. This "test patty" allows you to adjust seasoning before the batch is committed. Pâté that is under-seasoned cannot be corrected after cooking. This step takes 3 minutes and saves you from a 100-portion mistake.

Method

1
Boil eggs 10 minutes for fully set yolk (not chalky — hard-boiled but not over-boiled). Ice bath 5 minutes. Peel, halve lengthwise. Blanch green beans in heavily salted boiling water for 3 minutes — no longer. Refresh immediately in ice water. Blanching bright green and refreshing cold locks the colour and creates the crisp-tender texture. Overcooked green beans go khaki-grey and become soft.
2
Niçoise dressing (batch — make daily): 60ml olive oil, 20ml red wine vinegar, 5g Dijon mustard, 5g anchovy paste, salt, pepper. Whisk until emulsified. This dressing should taste intensely savoury, slightly tangy, with the anchovy providing depth without fishiness.
3
Arrange salad as separate components — not tossed. The arranged presentation is classic Niçoise and visually superior: leaves in the base, then green beans, halved tomatoes, tuna in the centre, eggs around the edge, olives and anchovy fillets draped over. Each component visible and distinct.
4
Dress at the table — pour dressing over the arranged salad in front of the guest, or serve dressing in a small jug alongside. The dressing should coat everything lightly. Season with a pinch of fleur de sel over the eggs just before serving.
Chef's Tip: The traditional Niçoise debate — no potato, no cooked fish, only canned tuna — is worth knowing and explaining to guests. The "authentic" Niçoise is composed of raw or briefly-prepared vegetables plus quality canned tuna in oil. The potato version is a "modern" interpretation. Both are delicious; knowing the history makes you the authority in the room.

Method

1
Beurre d'escargot (batch — make weekly): Let butter come to room temperature. Beat until completely smooth. Add very finely minced garlic, finely chopped parsley, finely minced shallot, salt, white pepper. Mix thoroughly. Roll in cling film into a log, refrigerate. This compound butter holds for 2 weeks refrigerated. The quality of this butter determines the entire dish.
2

Chef's Tip: Escargots are an extraordinary value proposition: inexpensive canned snails transformed into a luxurious, memorable dish entirely through the quality of the compound butter. The garlic must be minced to almost a paste — coarse garlic chunks burn at the cooking temperature and turn bitter. A fine microplane or mortar and pestle gives the right result.

Method

1
Triple-cooked frites (batch to 2nd cook in morning): Peel and cut potatoes into 1cm × 1cm sticks — uniform size is essential for even cooking. Rinse in cold water to remove surface starch. Blanch in unsalted water at 90°C / 194°F (not boiling) for 8–10 minutes until almost cooked through but not falling apart. Drain, dry, refrigerate on a wire rack. First fry at 130°C / 266°F for 4 minutes — seals without browning. Drain, cool. These are ready for the final service fry.
2
Béarnaise (batch — make 90 min before service, hold at 55°C): Reduce white wine vinegar 80ml with 3 shallots, 5g tarragon stalks, 5 peppercorns to 30ml — this is the gastrique. Strain. Whisk 4 egg yolks with gastrique over a double boiler at 65°C / 149°F until ribbon stage. Remove from heat. Slowly add 200g warm clarified butter, whisking constantly. Add fresh tarragon. A broken béarnaise can often be recovered by whisking vigorously with 1 tablespoon of hot water.
3
Bring bavette to room temperature. Season generously with salt and pepper just before cooking. Heat a cast iron pan until smoking. Add oil. Sear bavette — 3 minutes first side, do not touch. Flip. Add butter, thyme, garlic to pan. Baste continuously for 2–3 minutes. Internal temp for medium-rare: 55°C / 131°F. Medium: 60°C / 140°F. Rest on wire rack 4 minutes minimum — resting is not optional; it redistributes muscle juices and makes the difference between a dry steak and a juicy one.
4
Final fry frites at 190°C / 374°F for 2–3 minutes until deep golden and crispy. Drain, season immediately with fine salt while hot — salt only adheres to hot, wet frites; salting cold frites produces salt that falls off onto the plate. Slice steak against the grain on the bias. Plate frites in a tall pile. Lean sliced steak against the pile. Serve béarnaise in a warm ramekin beside.
Chef's Tip: The triple-cook method for frites — blanch, first fry, second fry — is the technique perfected by Heston Blumenthal and used in every serious French brasserie. The first cook makes them fluffy inside; the second seals the surface; the third creates the glass-like, shattering crust. No other method produces the same result. The extra effort is a few minutes of morning prep that defines the dish entirely.

Method

1
Scrub mussels under cold water. Remove beards — pull firmly toward the hinge end. Discard any mussels that are open and do not close when tapped sharply (dead mussels). Discard any with cracked shells. This safety check is mandatory — a dead mussel cooked and eaten causes food poisoning. The number of mussels discarded varies; order 15% more than you need to account for losses.
2
Heat a large, wide pot over high heat — it must be truly hot before anything goes in. Add butter, shallots, garlic. Sauté 90 seconds until just softened. Add white wine and bring to a boil immediately. Add all cleaned mussels in one movement, cover with a tight-fitting lid.
3
Steam over high heat for 4–5 minutes, shaking the pot once at 2 minutes. The mussels are done when they are all open. Any mussel that remains closed after 5 minutes must be discarded — it did not open because it was dead before cooking. Pour cream over, stir briefly, return lid for 30 seconds.
4
Transfer to a large warmed bowl — the pot is the traditional serving vessel in brasseries, which is equally correct. Scatter fresh parsley. Serve immediately with toasted sourdough for mopping. The golden rule of moules: the bread-mopping of the sauce is as important as the mussels themselves. Ensure sufficient bread.
Chef's Tip: Mussels must be cooked to order and served immediately — they become rubbery within minutes of being fully cooked. The entire dish from cold pan to table should be 10–12 minutes. Pre-prepping (cleaning and debearding) can be done in the morning, but cooking must happen to order. This is one dish where you cannot compromise on timing.

Method

1
Duck confit (batch — cure 24hr, cook 8hr): Combine 30g salt + 5g sugar + thyme + bay leaf + black pepper. Rub all over duck legs. Refrigerate 24 hours. The salt cure draws moisture from the skin and begins to season the flesh. Rinse cured legs briefly, pat dry.
2

3

4
Service: Remove duck leg from fat. Pat skin completely dry — any fat on the skin surface will prevent crisping. Place skin-side down in a cold oven-safe pan. Heat pan, allow duck to render at 180°C / 356°F for 6–8 minutes until skin is absolutely crispy — it should crackle when pressed. Serve over warm dressed lentils. The contrast of shatteringly crispy duck skin and earthy, yielding lentils is the entire experience.
Chef's Tip: Never add oil to the pan when re-crisping duck confit — the duck skin has enough fat from the confit process to render itself perfectly. Adding oil creates a frying environment that spatters, smokes, and produces uneven crisping. The skin must go in a cold pan that heats gradually — a pre-heated pan immediately seals the skin before the fat can render out, and you end up with a rubbery skin.

Method (large batch — start day before)

1
Pat beef completely dry. Season generously. Sear in batches in a hot pot — deep brown crust on all sides, working in batches to avoid crowding. Remove beef. Render lardons in the same pot until crispy. Remove. Sauté sliced onion and carrot until coloured. Add tomato paste, cook 2 minutes — this caramelises the tomato paste and removes rawness.

Chef's Tip: The best bourguignon is made 2 days before serving. This is not a compromise — it is the correct technique. The overnight rest allows the collagen from the beef to fully set in the sauce, thickening it naturally, and the wine's alcohol integrates completely. A same-day bourguignon is technically finished; a 2-day bourguignon is extraordinary.

Method

1

Method

1
Shortcrust pastry: combine 250g plain flour, 125g cold butter (cubed), 1 egg yolk, pinch salt. Rub butter into flour until resembles breadcrumbs. Add egg yolk, 1–2 tablespoons cold water. Bring together without overworking — gluten development makes pastry tough. Wrap and rest in fridge 30 minutes before rolling. This cold resting is essential: warm pastry shrinks dramatically during blind baking.
2
Roll pastry to 3mm, line a 26cm tin. Rest 15 minutes in fridge. Blind bake at 170°C / 338°F with weights for 12 minutes, remove weights, bake 5 more minutes until base is pale gold and dry. Render bacon lardons until just cooked but not crispy — they continue cooking in the oven with the custard.
3
Custard: whisk eggs and cream together — no more than 6 strokes. Season with salt, white pepper, nutmeg. The nutmeg is not optional — it is the flavour note that defines Lorraine custard. Strain through a fine sieve for a smooth, bubble-free custard that sets without pitting.
4
Scatter bacon and gruyère in blind-baked shell. Pour custard to 90% full. Bake at 160°C / 320°F for 28–32 minutes. The quiche is done when edges are set and centre still wobbles like jelly. It firms completely on cooling. Serve warm or room temperature — never refrigerator-cold, which dulls the flavour and hardens the pastry.
Chef's Tip: The custard-to-bacon ratio defines the character of the quiche. The classic Lorraine is 80% custard, 20% filling — it is first and foremost an egg cream custard with bacon accent, not a bacon pie with a bit of egg. If your quiche looks more brown than yellow when sliced, you have too much filling and not enough custard.

Method (batch — bake day before)

1
Split vanilla bean, scrape seeds into heavy cream. Add the pod. Heat to 80°C / 176°F — just below simmering. Steep 15 minutes off heat. Remove pod. The vanilla infusion must be done slowly at the correct temperature; too hot and the cream scorches and the vanilla becomes harsh.
2
Whisk egg yolks and sugar until pale — do not beat to a foam, just combined. Pour the warm cream slowly over the yolks in a thin stream, whisking gently. The gradual addition prevents the hot cream from scrambling the yolks. Strain through a fine sieve — this removes any cooked yolk strands and the vanilla pod fibres.
3
Pour into ramekins. Place in a deep baking tray, fill with hot water to halfway up the ramekins. Bake at 130°C / 266°F for 35–40 minutes. The custard is ready when the edges are set and the centre wobbles like set jelly. Remove from water bath, cool to room temp, refrigerate overnight. The slow low-temperature bake produces a silky, dense, trembling custard — high temperatures produce a grainy, scrambled-egg texture.
4
To order: pat surface of custard dry with paper towel (moisture prevents the sugar from caramelising evenly). Sprinkle 15g demerara sugar evenly. Caramelise with a blowtorch in circular sweeping motions until evenly golden — no pale spots, no burnt black spots. The brûlée crust should shatter cleanly under a spoon. Serve immediately.
Chef's Tip: The theatre of the tableside brûlée is a valuable service moment — if you caramelise it in the kitchen, you lose that interaction. Consider finishing the brûlée at the table with a visible blowtorch. The sound, the smell, and the visual of the sugar caramelising right in front of a guest is one of the most memorable restaurant moments possible.

Method (batch — bake daily)

1
Peel, core, and quarter Granny Smith apples. Granny Smith is the essential variety — its high acidity holds up against the sugar and butter rather than dissolving into mush. Sweeter varieties like Fuji or Gala become jam during cooking. Toss with lemon juice to prevent browning.
2
In a 24cm oven-safe pan, melt butter and sugar over medium heat. Do not stir — let it caramelise naturally. The caramel is ready when it turns a deep amber (not pale gold, not black — deep amber, which is approximately 170°C / 338°F). At this stage remove from heat — it continues cooking from the pan's residual heat.
3
Arrange apple quarters tightly in concentric circles directly in the caramel — they shrink during cooking so pack them very tightly. Return to medium heat, cook the apples in the caramel for 5–8 minutes. Cover the apples with a circle of all-butter puff pastry, tucking the edges down around the apples.
4
Bake at 190°C / 374°F for 25 minutes until pastry is deep golden. Rest 5 minutes. To unmould: place a serving plate firmly over the pan, invert in one decisive motion. The apples should be deep caramel-orange and glistening. Serve slices warm with cold crème fraîche.
Chef's Tip: The unmoulding is the moment of drama and also the moment of highest risk. The pan is full of boiling caramel — use thick oven mitts and invert with confidence. A hesitant flip results in caramel running down your arm. Practice the motion with an empty pan before your first service. Once inverted, any gaps in the apple arrangement can be quickly pushed back into place.

Method (batch — set minimum 4 hours)

1
Melt chocolate and butter over a double boiler at 50°C / 122°F — never directly on heat. Stir occasionally. Cool to 35°C / 95°F before adding yolks — above this temperature the yolks scramble. Stir in egg yolks.
2
Whisk egg whites to soft peaks. Add sugar gradually, continue whisking to stiff glossy peaks — the whites should hold shape but not be dry or granular. Overwhipped whites deflate when folded and cannot be corrected.
3
Fold one-third of the whites into the chocolate mixture to lighten it — use a cutting motion, not stirring. This first addition is sacrificial: it makes the chocolate lighter and more receptive to the remaining whites. Fold remaining whites in two stages, retaining as much air as possible. The key is speed and confidence — the more you fold, the more air you lose.
4
Divide into glasses. Refrigerate minimum 4 hours. Serve with shaved dark chocolate on top. The mousse should feel light and airy but intensely chocolatey — both qualities in perfect balance. If it tastes heavy, the chocolate-to-white ratio is wrong or the whites were under-folded.
Chef's Tip: Use Valrhona or Callebaut 70% minimum for mousse. The cocoa percentage and origin of the chocolate is the entire flavour of the dish — cheap compound chocolate produces a flat, overly-sweet mousse with no complexity. The extra $0.60 per portion for quality chocolate is the most high-impact ingredient investment in this dessert.

7 Service Training Module

Script 1 — The Technique Story

"The steak frites — we triple-cook our frites here. That means they're blanched, fried twice, then fried a third time to order. It takes most of the morning, but it's why the crust shatters when you bite into them. The steak is bavette, which is what every brasserie in Paris serves — it's the chef's cut. Want me to put one in for you?"

Why it works: Technical specificity builds authority. Guests who understand the work feel they are getting value. "Chef's cut" positions bavette as aspirational rather than a lesser cut than ribeye.

Script 2 — The Wine Bridge

"Can I get you a glass of our house red with that? It's a Côtes du Rhône — a southern French red, fruit-forward, works with everything. It's $11.99 for 150ml or we do a 500ml carafe for $32. Most people doing moules or steak go with it."

Why it works: Specific origin story + social proof ("most people doing moules or steak go with it") + carafe option as natural upsell. The carafe is 3.3 glasses but sounds like a casual choice rather than "more wine."

Script 3 — The Dessert Plant at Order

"Just so you know ahead of time — our crème brûlée is made yesterday and chilled overnight. We finish it tableside with a blowtorch when you order it. Takes about 2 minutes but it's worth watching. I'll mention it again at the end."

Why it works: Pre-framing the dessert at order time creates anticipation and pre-sells it before they feel full. The blowtorch theatre is the reason people say yes — sell the experience first.

Script 4 — The Aperitif Opener

"Would you like to start with something while you look at the menu? We have a Kir Royale — Prosecco with cassis, it's the classic French aperitif — or a pastis if you've had one of those before. They're $9.99 and $12.99."

Why it works: "If you've had one before" on pastis turns it into a knowing wink — experienced guests appreciate the recognition. Offering two specific options with personality is more effective than a generic "can I get you a drink?"

Script 5 — The Escargot Introduction

"Have you had escargot before? Our version is Bourgogne style — the snails go into their shells with a garlic, shallot, and parsley butter. They come out sizzling. The butter is genuinely the point — you mop it up with the baguette. It's $16.99 and it's one of the most French things we do."

Why it works: "Have you had it before?" is a gentle opener that works for first-timers (curiosity) and experienced guests (familiarity). Describing the butter-mopping in advance removes the awkwardness first-timers feel about the ritual.

8 Customer Retention System

The Brasserie Principle: A brasserie's best customers are not occasional special-occasion diners — they are the people who come every Tuesday for moules, every Friday for steak frites, every Sunday morning for a croque monsieur and a coffee. Build regulars who have a weekly ritual, not guests who come twice a year. One regular who comes weekly is worth more than ten annual visitors.
3-Visit Rule:
Visit 1: Perfect execution. Note what they ordered and if they loved it.
Visit 2: "You were in last week — the moules?" They will be genuinely surprised. Write their name down after they leave.
Visit 3: "The usual?" + name = you just created your best regular. The ritual is established.

5 VIP Micro-Moments

1. The Baguette Arrival. Warm baguette and cold butter should be on the table before the guest has fully settled. This is not a transaction — it is a statement: "You are already being cared for." It sets the tone for everything that follows.

2. The Regular's Order. If you know their usual, confirm it before they order: "Moules and a glass of the red?" If you're right, the feeling of being known is worth more than any loyalty card. If you're wrong, you've shown you care enough to try.

3. The Complimentary Amuse-Bouche. Once a month for your top regulars: a small amuse-bouche sent out before their first course with no explanation needed beyond "from us." One small dice of pâté on a crostino costs $0.22. The signal it sends is priceless.

4. The Perfect Coffee Timing. Bring the espresso at the right moment — not while they're still eating, not 15 minutes after the dessert is cleared. Watch the table. When the dessert plate is set aside and they lean back: that's the moment. Arrive with the espresso before they ask for it.

5. The Cold Day Extra. On particularly cold or rainy days, offer regulars a complimentary petit verre of vin chaud (mulled wine) while they study the menu. Costs $0.55. On a wet Tuesday when no one wants to be out, this turns a reluctant dining decision into a cherished memory.

Complaint Recovery — 3 Steps

Step 1 — Receive it fully. "Thank you for telling me — I want to know." In a brasserie setting, most guests will not complain loudly; they will simply not return. The guest who tells you has given you a gift.
Step 2 — Take ownership directly. "That's not right. I'm sorry." Short, direct, no corporate language. The French directness in a brasserie context: don't over-apologise, don't explain at length. Acknowledge it, own it, fix it.
Step 3 — Recover and invite back. Replace the dish or offer something else. "Your dessert tonight is on us. Please come back — I want to give you a proper experience." Follow up at the door: "Did we get it right?" The second impression is often more powerful than the first.

Social Media — 5 Best Shots

1. The crème brûlée torch shot: Film the blowtorch finishing a brûlée tableside — the flame, the crackling sugar, the moment the crust sets. This 15-second Reel requires zero editing and performs reliably every single time. Post weekly.

2. The steak frites cross-section: Cut the bavette on the board before plating, showing the pink interior against the seared crust, with a pile of golden frites visible. The contrast of textures and colours is immediately compelling.

3. Escargot arrival: Film the sizzling escargot pan arriving at the table from above — the theatrical sizzle sound, steam rising, the smell almost communicated through the screen. Pair with the butter-mopping closeup.

4. Morning baguette and café au lait: Overhead shot of a café au lait bowl, a baguette half torn to show the open crumb, butter and a croissant. This image communicates the entire brasserie identity in one frame — it is aspirational and accessible simultaneously.

5. The tarte tatin unmould: Film the moment the tarte tatin is inverted — the reveal of the caramelised apples, any escaping caramel, the steam. This is one of the great food theatre moments in any kitchen. Share it raw, unedited. The imperfection is part of the appeal.
