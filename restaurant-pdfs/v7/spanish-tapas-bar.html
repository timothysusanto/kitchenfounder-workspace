*La Plancha Bible & Spanish Pantry

The plancha is everything. A flat iron griddle running at 220–250°C is the beating heart of every Spanish kitchen. It does what no grill and no sauté pan can replicate: direct dry heat that creates a deep Maillard crust on seafood, meat, and vegetables in under 4 minutes, while keeping the interior perfectly moist. The plancha is why Spanish food tastes the way it does. Every dish in this guide is designed around it.
The Spanish Pantry — Non-Negotiables:

Olive oil: Extra virgin, Spanish (Arbequina or Picual variety). Never substitute with Italian. The flavour profile — grassy, slightly peppery, low bitterness — is specific to Spanish cuisine.
Smoked paprika (pimentón de la Vera): Made from peppers smoke-dried over oak in Extremadura. Three styles: dulce (sweet), agridulce (bittersweet), picante (hot). The oak smoke flavour is irreplaceable — Hungarian sweet paprika is a poor substitute.
Saffron: Spanish La Mancha saffron (PDO). Bloom it in warm water or warm stock for 15 minutes before use. Never add dry saffron directly — the flavour compounds need heat and liquid to activate. 0.1g bloomed in 30ml stock transforms a paella.
Manzanilla sherry: The driest, most saline sherry — from Sanlúcar de Barrameda. Essential for clams, gambas, and sauces. Do not substitute with white wine. The oxidative, almond-briney character is specific to manzanilla.
Jamón Ibérico: Dry-cured Iberian pork leg, minimum 24 months. The fat is soft and oleic-acid-rich — it melts at body temperature. Buy a whole leg and carve to order, or purchase pre-sliced from a Spanish importer. This is your single most prestigious ingredient.
Chorizo: Spanish dry-cured chorizo (not Mexican fresh chorizo — completely different product). Made with pork, pimentón, garlic, salt. The fat renders at 60°C, turning deep red and smoky — use this fat as a flavour base for sauces.
Manchego: Sheep's milk cheese from La Mancha, aged 3 months (fresco), 6 months (curado), or 12+ months (viejo). The older the cheese, the more crystalline, nutty, and complex. Always serve at room temperature — cold manchego has no flavour.
Piquillo peppers: Small, sweet red peppers from Navarre, wood-roasted and packed in their own juice. Intensely sweet, slightly smoky, with no bitterness. Use whole, stuffed, or puréed in romesco.
Marcona almonds: Spanish flat almonds with a richer, more buttery flavour than California almonds. Toast in olive oil with sea salt — they become intensely nutty and slightly caramelised. Essential for romesco and as a tapas bar snack.
The Barrafina Model — Counter Dining Philosophy: Barrafina in London (opened 2007, one Michelin star) is the template for this guide. 24 seats, no reservations, counter facing the open kitchen, queue outside every service. The queue is the marketing. The counter is the theatre. Every guest watches their food being cooked. This creates an intimacy and energy that no reservation-based restaurant can replicate. The no-reservations model is not a limitation — it is a statement: the food is good enough that people will wait. Your kitchen must be good enough to deserve that queue.

Method — The Catalan Technique

1
Toast bread directly on the plancha or under the grill until deeply golden with char marks — the exterior must be crisp and the interior still soft. The contrast of textures is the structure of this dish.
2
While the bread is hot, rub a raw garlic clove firmly across the surface — the garlic cells break and perfume the bread without being pungent. One garlic clove per two slices. Do not skip this step or use garlic oil.
3
Cut the tomato in half across its equator. Grate the cut face against a coarse grater held over a bowl — the flesh falls through as a rough pulp, leaving the skin behind. Season the pulp with a pinch of salt. Spread generously over the warm, garlicked bread.
4
Pour Arbequina EVOO over in a thin, steady stream across the entire surface. Finish with fleur de sel. Serve immediately — once the tomato pulp soaks into the bread, the texture is lost. This dish dies in 3 minutes after plating.
💡 The tomato must be completely ripe. An under-ripe tomato produces watery, acidic pulp with no sweetness. Buy vine-ripened tomatoes, store at room temperature (never refrigerate — cold destroys tomato flavour), and check them daily. If a tomato doesn't smell like a tomato when you hold it to your nose, it is not ready. This is the most important quality check in your entire kitchen.
Warm, charred sourdough → raw garlic perfume → sweet-acidic grated tomato → grassy Arbequina oil → salt crystals that pop on the tongue. It is simultaneously the simplest and most perfect thing on the menu.

Method — The Sizzling Cazuela

1
Use a small earthenware cazuela or heavy-bottomed cast iron pan. Add EVOO and garlic slices cold, then bring to heat together — the garlic infuses the oil as the temperature rises, rather than frying in already-hot oil. This produces golden, fragrant garlic rather than bitter, burnt garlic. Heat to a gentle sizzle: 120°C / 250°F — the garlic should slowly bubble, not aggressively fry.
2
Once garlic is pale golden (about 3 minutes), add guindilla chilli. Increase heat. Add prawns — they should immediately sizzle loudly in the hot oil. This sound is the dish's theatre at the counter. Cook 60-90 seconds per side until pink throughout. Do not overcook — overcooked gambas al ajillo are rubbery and dry. Pull them at the moment they turn pink.
3
Deglaze with manzanilla sherry — it will bubble violently and steam. Immediately add parsley. Carry the still-sizzling cazuela directly from the heat to the counter guest. The dish must arrive bubbling and crackling. If it has stopped sizzling, you waited too long.
💡 The garlic-infused oil at the bottom of the cazuela is the most valuable thing in this dish. After the prawns are eaten, guests will mop it with bread. Train your servers to say: "Save the oil for the bread — it's better than the prawns." Guests who hear this always do it, and it converts a $16.99 tapa into a full experience.
Sweet prawn flesh → olive oil richness → slow-cooked garlic that has given up its sharpness entirely → guindilla heat that builds late → the snap of manzanilla sherry cutting through → parsley freshness. This is oil, fire, garlic, and the sea in one dish.

Method — The Béchamel That Sets Overnight

1
The béchamel: Melt butter over medium heat. Add flour and cook for 2 minutes, stirring constantly — the raw flour taste must cook out entirely. Add warm milk in three additions, whisking vigorously each time to prevent lumps. The sauce will thicken dramatically. Continue cooking and stirring for 8-10 minutes over low heat until the béchamel is very thick — it should hold the shape of a spoon dragged through it. Add chopped jamón Ibérico, nutmeg, white pepper. Season carefully — the jamón adds significant salt.
2
Pour into a hotel pan. Press cling film directly against the surface to prevent a skin forming. Refrigerate minimum 4 hours — overnight is ideal. The béchamel must be completely cold and firm before shaping.
3
Using wet hands or two spoons, shape into cylinders roughly 5cm long. The mixture will be sticky — this is correct. Coat in flour, then egg wash, then panko. Fry at 180°C / 356°F for 3-4 minutes until deep golden. The interior should be molten and flowing.
💡 The test of a great croqueta: when you bite through the panko crust, the béchamel inside should flow out slowly, like a thick lava. If it holds its shape after biting, it was either under-fried or the béchamel was too thick. The ideal moment to eat a croqueta is 45 seconds after it leaves the fryer — hot enough to flow, cool enough not to burn.
The shatter of panko → then the molten, silky béchamel releases → the salty, complex jamón Ibérico blooms through the cream → nutmeg warmth at the finish. This is comfort food from a Michelin kitchen.

Method — The Double Fry Protocol

1
Peel and cut potatoes into irregular chunks — not neat cubes, not chips. The irregular shape creates more surface area and more crust. First fry (confit): fry at 140°C / 285°F for 8-10 minutes until completely cooked through but not coloured. Drain and cool. This can be done hours ahead.
2
Second fry (crust): at service, fry par-cooked potatoes at 190°C / 375°F for 3-4 minutes until deeply golden and blistered. The high heat creates the shatteringly crisp exterior. Season immediately with sea salt while hot.
3
Salsa brava (batch): sauté garlic and pimentón picante in oil, add tinned tomatoes, splash of white wine vinegar, cook down 20 minutes to a thick, intense sauce. Season. The sauce should be punchy, acidic, and smoky-hot — not sweet. Aioli: pound 2 garlic cloves to a paste in a mortar. Whisk in egg yolk. Add EVOO drop by drop to emulsify. Add lemon juice. Traditional aioli has no mustard — the emulsification is pure garlic and oil.
The double-fried potato has two textures: a thin, glass-like crust that shatters under the fork, then a floury, steaming interior. The brava sauce hits immediately: pimentón smoke, tomato acidity, chilli heat. The aioli cools and enriches. These three elements in one bite are the entire Spanish tapas philosophy.

Method — The Galician Technique

1
Boiling octopus (day before): Freeze octopus 24 hours first — the ice crystals break down the muscle fibres, tenderising more effectively than any tenderiser. Bring a large pot of unsalted water to a rolling boil. Dip the octopus in and out three times before submerging — this curls the tentacles cleanly. Simmer at 85°C (not a hard boil — a hard boil toughens octopus) for 45–60 minutes until a skewer meets zero resistance. Cool in the cooking liquid, then refrigerate overnight in the liquid. Overnight cold rest is not optional — the texture firms and the flavour deepens.
2
At service: remove tentacle portions from liquid. Pat completely dry — moisture on the plancha creates steam, not char. Heat plancha to absolute maximum: 250°C / 480°F. Brush tentacle with EVOO. Press firmly onto the plancha and do not move for 2 full minutes — let the Maillard crust form undisturbed. Turn, repeat. Total plancha time: 4 minutes. The exterior should be deeply caramelised with char marks; the interior still tender and yielding.
3
Warm sliced potato in a small pan with olive oil and sea salt — it should be warm, not cold, when it hits the plate. Arrange potato slices as a base on a wooden board or slate. Place octopus tentacle across the potato. Drizzle with generous EVOO. Dust heavily with pimentón dulce — it should cover the octopus visibly in a red-orange coat. Finish with fleur de sel flakes and lemon wedge. Serve immediately.
💡 The overnight liquid rest is the single most important step chefs skip. Octopus that hasn't rested overnight in its poaching liquid is 30% less flavourful and has a grainy, slightly chewy texture. The liquid penetrates back into the flesh as it cools, acting as a brine. Budget for this lead time in your prep schedule — octopus is always prepped the night before, never the morning of.
The char from the plancha brings smokiness and caramelised complexity. The waxy potato is nutty and rich with olive oil. The pimentón provides smoke and colour. The EVOO pools on the plate. Each element is simple; together they are extraordinary.

Method — The 20-Minute Ritual

1
The sofregit base: Heat paella pan over medium-high with EVOO. Sear chicken and rabbit pieces until deep golden brown on all sides — this fond is the flavour foundation of the whole paella. Push to the edges. Add grated tomato to the centre of the pan and cook down for 5 minutes until the moisture evaporates and it darkens to a thick, concentrated paste. Add paprika and stir for 30 seconds only — paprika burns at high heat.
2
Add bomba rice to the sofregit and stir for 1 minute to coat every grain in the flavoured oil. Add beans. Pour in hot saffron stock in one addition. Shake the pan gently to distribute rice evenly across the pan in a single layer — no stirring from this point. Never stir paella once stock is added. The starch must be allowed to settle and form the socarrat.
3
Cook over medium-high heat for 10 minutes until the stock has reduced to the level of the rice. Reduce heat to low for 8 more minutes. At the final 2 minutes, increase heat to high for 90 seconds — this creates the socarrat: the caramelised, slightly crispy layer of rice on the bottom of the pan. The socarrat is the most prized part of the paella.
4
Remove from heat. Rest 3 minutes covered with foil. Test the socarrat by dragging a spoon across the bottom — it should come up as golden, caramelised rice crust. Serve in the pan at the counter. Lemon wedges alongside.
💡 The socarrat is the marker of a correctly cooked paella. A pale, limp bottom with no crust means the heat was too low or the pan was lifted too early. Train servers to offer: "Café cortado or con leche to finish?" at the natural close of the meal.
💡 Coffee is your lowest food cost item and your highest frequency add-on. At 60-70% of tables ordering coffee, it adds $270-315/night on a 60-cover service. Never not offer it. The cortado is Spanish — it tells the story of the bar correctly.

⭐ La Plancha Station

  • Main protein cooking: octopus, lamb, prawns, pork, fish
  • Temperature zones: left side 180°C (gentle), right side 250°C (sear)
  • Pre-service: pre-heat 20 min minimum before service
  • Cleaning: scraper and water between each protein — never soap on cast iron
  • Oil: apply olive oil lightly before each cook — forms the non-stick layer
CRITICAL: Never put cold meat on an underheated plancha. The sear fails, protein sticks, texture is ruined.

🍟 Fryer Station

  • Primary use: croquetas (180°C), churros (180°C), patatas bravas first fry (140°C), second fry (190°C)
  • Oil: refined sunflower or light olive oil — Spanish EVOO is too strong and expensive for deep frying
  • Oil change: every 3 service days minimum; test daily with fry quality and colour
  • Filter oil daily using a fryer filter machine if available

🥘 Paella Station

  • Single-burner floor standing for drama and heat control
  • Pan size: 36cm for 1-2 portions; 50cm for 3-4 portions
  • Never cook more than 2 paella pans simultaneously (quality control)
  • Socarrat monitoring: listen for the sizzle changing to a crackle at the last 2 min — this is the socarrat forming

❄️ Cold Station / Pass

  • Jamón board + carving knife (jamonero if possible)
  • Manchego wedge — room temperature, not refrigerated
  • Chilled copita glasses for manzanilla (store in freezer)
  • Assembled sherry flight glassware
  • Churros batter (piping bag, ready to fry)

6Service Scripts — La Experiencia

Script philosophy: At a counter tapas bar, the server is also the storyteller. Every dish has a provenance, every wine has a region, every technique has a reason. The guest is 60cm from the kitchen — they can see everything. Use that proximity. Train staff to explain what guests are watching and why it matters.
OPENING SCRIPT — Welcoming a Guest to the Counter
"Welcome — grab any seat at the counter, you can see everything from there. Our menu is tapas-style, so everything comes as it's ready from the plancha — that flat iron griddle you can see right there. Order a few things to start, then more as you go. I'd strongly suggest starting with the pan con tomate while the kitchen fires up your order — it's been on the menu since day one and it never changes. Sherry, wine, sangria, or shall I walk you through the drinks list?"
Why this works: establishes counter theatre, explains the format (tapas = sharing, no courses), recommends a specific first order to anchor the experience, and moves immediately to drinks (35% of revenue).
SHERRY UPSELL SCRIPT — The Flight Pitch
"Have you had sherry before? Not the sweet stuff — proper dry Spanish sherry. We do a flight of three: manzanilla (bone-dry, almost salty), amontillado (nutty, dried fruit), and a tiny pour of Pedro Ximénez which is basically dessert. It's the best $18.99 you'll spend today and it pairs perfectly with everything coming off the plancha. Most people who try it end up ordering a full glass of their favourite afterwards."
Why this works: pre-empts the "I don't like sweet sherry" objection, creates curiosity, and plants the seed of a second drink purchase. The phrase "most people who try it" is social proof without pressure.
GAMBAS AL AJILLO SCRIPT — Counter Theatre Narration
"So what you're about to watch is the gambas al ajillo being made — that's prawns in garlic oil. The garlic goes into cold oil first, then they heat up together, so the garlic slowly turns golden without burning. Then the prawns go in, there'll be a big sizzle, and then the sherry goes in right at the end — that's the manzanilla you can smell. It'll come out still sizzling in the pan. When it arrives: the garlic oil at the bottom is better than the prawns themselves. Use the bread for the oil."
Why this works: creates anticipation and narrates the cooking process the guest can already see. "Better than the prawns" creates a memorable eating instruction that guests repeat to their companions. It makes the bread a feature, not an afterthought.
PAELLA UPSELL + TIMING SCRIPT
"The paella Valenciana takes about 22 minutes and cannot be rushed — if I put it in faster, it won't develop the socarrat, which is the caramelised crust of rice on the bottom of the pan. That's the best bit. Shall I put that on now so it's ready after your starters? While it cooks: should I bring the pan con tomate and gambas first to keep the plancha moving for you?"
Why this works: the timing explanation sounds like chef knowledge, not a delay excuse. "Can't be rushed" frames the wait as quality. The follow-up immediately converts the 22-minute wait into an opportunity to sell two more starters.

11The Chef's Lens — A Michelin Perspective on the Spanish Counter Model

Essay — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder

The Spanish tapas bar is, quietly, one of the most profitable restaurant formats ever devised. Small kitchen. Small footprint. No reservations overhead. No bloated floor staff. Every seat at the counter, facing the action — and every plate designed to be made in under four minutes. When you understand the economics of a plancha station running at full speed, you start to see why Spain has produced more radical culinary thinking per square kilometre than almost anywhere else on earth. This format rewards excellence and punishes mediocrity in equal and immediate measure.

Counter dining is the future of casual fine dining in the US. Strip away the hostess stand, the reservation software, the six-top tables that sit half-empty on a Tuesday night — and what you have left is a direct, honest exchange between cook and guest. Guests at a counter eat better, spend more per minute, and leave faster. That is not a cynical statement. That is hospitality distilled to its purest form.

The Barrafina effect is real and it is replicable. When you queue for something, you want it more. No reservations is not a limitation — it is a statement. It says: we do not take bookings because we do not need to. Come, queue, eat the best food of your week. The line becomes the brand. I have watched people queue forty minutes in the rain on Frith Street in Soho. They were not frustrated. They were excited. That is the difference between a restaurant that is merely good and one that creates genuine desire.

The plancha is the hardest station I have ever worked in professional kitchens. You can conceal a weak cook at a sauté station or a grill. On a flat iron plancha at 250°C, everything is visible, everything is timing, and nothing can be faked. Gambas al ajillo that is ten seconds too long turns rubbery and dry. Pimientos de padrón need split-second judgement between collapsed and perfect and cremated. Chuletillas de cordero must be pulled at exactly 57°C internal — no more, no less. The station demands a cook who thinks three dishes ahead, manages temperature zones across a single flat surface like a chess player manages a board, and plates with one hand while the other never stops moving.

Here is the honest truth about the US market right now: Spanish cuisine is one of the most underrepresented cuisines relative to its quality. Italian has a restaurant on every block. French still carries its fine-dining weight. But Spain — the country that gave us the greatest culinary revolution of the last forty years, that produced Ferran Adrià and elBulli and a generation of chefs who changed how the world thinks about food — has almost no presence outside New York, Los Angeles, and Chicago. The opportunity for an operator who gets it right is enormous, and the first-mover advantage in mid-tier US cities is real and available right now.

The product you are building if you follow this guide correctly is not just a restaurant. It is a piece of Spanish food culture transplanted into a US neighbourhood. The manzanilla in a chilled copita. The sizzling cazuela of gambas carried from the plancha to the counter in ten steps. The socarrat scraped from the bottom of the paella pan. These are experiences that no chain restaurant can replicate. They require skill, knowledge, and genuine hospitality — and they are available to any operator who invests in those three things.

One final warning, and I mean it seriously: the counter model only works if the food is exceptional. There is nowhere to hide at a 24-seat counter bar. When guests have queued thirty minutes and are sitting sixty centimetres from your kitchen, they are paying close attention. Every plate is auditioned in real time. If the pan con tomate arrives with under-ripe tomato and cheap olive oil, the guest knows immediately. If the gambas al ajillo comes out overcooked, the guest can smell that the garlic was bitter. The queue becomes resentment overnight.

Build the queue only when you are ready to deserve it. — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder
