Boil in heavily salted water: The salted cooking water seasons the bean inside the pod — this is why proper edamame tastes different from unsalted boiled edamame. Bring water to a rolling boil with 1 tbsp salt per litre. Cook from frozen 4–5 minutes. The pods should be bright green and the beans inside tender but with a slight bite.
2
Drain and season immediately: Drain well — wet edamame dilutes the finishing salt. While still hot, toss with a tiny drizzle of sesame oil and a generous pinch of coarse sea salt. The eating technique: Edamame is eaten by squeezing the pod with your teeth — the beans pop into your mouth and the pod is discarded. Upsell togarashi: Always offer at ordering: "Would you like them plain with sea salt, or with togarashi — Japanese seven-spice?" The choice creates engagement and the togarashi option adds $0.02 cost with no price change. Most guests choose togarashi once they know it exists — it makes a simple dish feel considered.

Salt and squeeze the cabbage: Toss finely shredded napa cabbage with salt, rest 10 minutes, squeeze out all moisture aggressively. Drain tofu thoroughly: Press silken tofu between paper towels with a light weight for 20 minutes. Excess moisture causes violent oil splatter when frying and prevents the starch coating from adhering. The tofu should be firm enough to handle without breaking — if it collapses, it was too wet or too soft a grade. Use medium-firm silken tofu, not extra-soft.

Potato starch, not flour: Coat tofu cubes in potato starch only — shake off excess. Potato starch creates a thinner, crisper, more translucent coating than wheat flour. 1
Skin-on thigh only — never breast: Yakitori is built on chicken thigh with skin on. The skin renders over the charcoal, basting the meat in its own fat, and caramelises with the tare glaze to create an intensely flavourful, slightly crispy exterior. Chicken breast dries out in 4 minutes over charcoal. This substitution is not acceptable. Thigh is the correct cut — full stop.
2
The tare is a living sauce: Tare glaze starts as a combination of soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar reduced to a thick syrup. It deepens and improves every time skewers are dipped into it during cooking — the dripping chicken fat and caramelised bits enrich the tare over weeks and months. Never discard your tare. Top it up. The tare at a 50-year-old yakitoriya is the accumulated flavour of 50 years.
3
Charcoal is non-negotiable: Yakitori cooked on a gas grill is not yakitori. The binchotan charcoal (white oak charcoal) burns at a consistent, radiant heat without flame — it caramelises the tare and renders the chicken fat without burning. The smoke from fat dripping onto charcoal infuses the meat with a flavour that is completely irreproducible on gas. Invest in a proper yakitori grill and binchotan.
4
Tare application — multiple passes: Begin grilling plain. After 3 minutes, dip the skewer in tare and return to the grill. Dip and grill 3–4 times total — each pass builds up a lacquered layer. The final pass is the most important — it creates the characteristic glossy, caramelised exterior. The salt option (shio) skips the tare and finishes with just sea salt and lemon — simpler, cleaner, equally traditional.
Chef's Tip: Yakitori is your highest-frequency reorder item — guests eat one round and immediately order another. Offer the tare and shio (salt) options for every skewer type. The variety of yakitori options across different cuts (negima, tsukune, sunagimo) can eventually become a dedicated yakitori menu. Start with thigh, master it, then expand.

1
Marinade minimum 2 hours: Soy, sake, mirin, freshly grated ginger and garlic. The sake and mirin tenderise the chicken while the soy penetrates deeply — 2 hours produces good flavour, overnight is better. Pat the marinated chicken dry before coating — wet chicken makes the coating gummy instead of crispy. The drying step is as important as the marinade itself.
2
Potato starch and flour coating — 3:1 ratio: The potato starch creates the extraordinarily craggy, irregular surface that is the visual signature of karaage. Pure potato starch produces a very fragile crust; the small amount of flour provides structural support. Coat each piece generously, press the coating on, shake off excess. The irregular surface creates more surface area — more crunch per bite.
3
Double fry for maximum crunch: First fry at 160°C / 320°F for 3 minutes — low heat cooks the chicken through without browning the crust. Rest 3 minutes. Second fry at 190°C / 374°F for 90 seconds — high heat caramelises and crisps the surface aggressively. The resting period allows moisture to migrate to the surface, which then converts to steam in the second fry, creating internal expansion and a dramatically crunchier crust.
4
Kewpie mayo is non-negotiable: Kewpie is Japanese mayonnaise — made with only egg yolks (not whole eggs), rice vinegar, and MSG. It is richer, more umami, and more acidic than American mayonnaise. It is not interchangeable. Serve it in a squeeze bottle alongside the karaage — the guest squeezes it over or dips. The contrast of crispy chicken, rich kewpie, and acidic lemon is one of the great flavour combinations in comfort food globally.
Chef's Tip: Karaage is the dish that converts first-time izakaya guests into regulars. It is genuinely the best fried chicken in the world when made correctly. The double-fry technique and kewpie mayo together create an eating experience that no other culture's fried chicken replicates. Own this dish completely.

The tataki technique — sear and shock: Heat a cast iron pan until smoking. Sear the beef loin on all four long sides — 30 seconds per side. The entire process takes 2 minutes. Immediately plunge into an ice bath for 2 minutes — this stops the cooking process and chills the exterior while the interior remains raw. Slice thin and arrange: Remove from ice bath, pat dry, slice 4mm thin against the grain. Ponzu is the dish's soul: Yuzu ponzu — citrus soy sauce with dashi — is light, acidic, and deeply savoury simultaneously. It does not overpower the beef; it frames it. Each garnish serves a function — none are purely decorative. The finished plate should be visually beautiful and offer multiple flavour dimensions in a single bite.
Chef's Tip: Beef tataki is your most premium entrée and the one that communicates the kitchen's technical capability most clearly. The ice bath technique is simple but precise — and the result is genuinely impressive. Source good-quality sirloin: the seared exterior caramelises beautifully and the raw interior is the entire point. Do not overcook.

1
The broth is your identity: Tonkotsu broth requires 12–18 hours of aggressive boiling of pork femur and trotters. The vigorous boil emulsifies the collagen and fat into the water — this is why tonkotsu is white and creamy, not clear. A gentle simmer will never produce the characteristic milky opacity. Begin your broth batch the afternoon before service. The bones must be blanched first (cold water to boil, discard water) to remove blood and impurities that would make the broth grey and bitter.
2
Chashu — the slow braise: Roll pork belly tight, tie with butcher's twine, sear all sides until deep brown. Braise in soy, mirin, sake, and water at 150°C / 302°F for 2.5 hours. Cool in the braising liquid overnight — the liquid becomes the chashu tare, used to season the broth. Slice cold for clean edges, torch or pan-sear each slice to order to caramelise the surface and warm through.
3
Ajitsuke tamago — the 6.5-minute egg: Boil eggs exactly 6 minutes 30 seconds from boiling water. Ice bath immediately for 5 minutes. Peel carefully — the white is set but the yolk is jammy, not liquid. Marinate peeled eggs in a 1:1 soy/mirin mixture diluted with water for minimum 4 hours. The eggs turn pale brown with a rich, savoury, slightly sweet flavour that complements the fatty broth perfectly.
4
Assembly speed is critical: Cook noodles in unsalted boiling water 90 seconds — rinse with cold water briefly to stop cooking, drain hard, place in warmed bowl. Ladle hot seasoned broth immediately — the hot broth finishes warming the noodles. Arrange chashu (2 slices, torched), halved egg, menma, nori upright at the side, spring onion, sesame, and a teaspoon of mayu (black garlic oil) in the centre. Total assembly: under 60 seconds.
Chef's Tip: Tonkotsu ramen sells itself — it is the single most-ordered Japanese dish in the US market. The broth batch is the investment; the assembly cost is low. Make broth twice weekly in large batches. Your ramen will develop its own personality over time as you adjust the seasoning and garnish. This is your anchor dish.

Japanese curry is not Indian curry: Japanese curry (karē) is sweeter, milder, and thicker — built on a roux base with fruit (apple, honey) and a proprietary spice blend that is distinctly Japanese. S&B Golden Curry blocks are the industry standard and are available at every Asian grocery at low cost. Scratch curry starts with the same concept: onion, carrot, potato, apple, spices, roux. 1
Dry the skin completely: Pat the salmon skin with paper towels until completely dry. Any moisture on the skin creates steam in the pan and prevents the skin from crisping. Score the skin lightly with 3 shallow cuts perpendicular to the fillet — this prevents the fillet from curling in the pan. Season the flesh only with fine salt. Do not season the skin — salt draws moisture to the surface.
2
Skin side down, cold pan start — or very hot: Two valid techniques: cold pan start (place salmon skin-down in a cold non-stick pan, apply medium heat — the skin renders slowly and crisps without burning) or very hot pan (searing hot, skin down, 3 minutes, then flip once). The flesh will turn opaque from the bottom up — flip when only the top 5mm remains translucent.
3
Teriyaki glaze — applied at the end: Teriyaki glaze contains sugar (mirin, sake, white sugar) which burns at high heat. Add glaze only in the final 90 seconds — pour into the pan and let it bubble and reduce around the salmon. Tilt the pan and baste the fish with the thickening glaze using a spoon. The glaze should coat the fish with a glossy, lacquered finish. Remove from heat the moment it becomes glossy — 30 seconds too long produces bitter, burnt sugar.
4
Rest, then plate skin-up: Rest the salmon on a rack for 90 seconds — never on a flat surface, which steams the skin soft again. Plate skin-side up so the guest sees the glazed, caramelised surface. The tsukemono pickled cucumber alongside cuts through the richness of the salmon and glaze — it is essential, not decorative. Steamed rice absorbs the excess glaze that runs from the fish.
Chef's Tip: Salmon teriyaki is your most popular dish with female guests aged 25–40. It photographs beautifully — the glossy glaze, golden skin, white rice — and is genuinely delicious. Keep your teriyaki glaze consistent by making large batches to the same recipe. The glaze is worth more than the fish — protect the recipe.

Never boil miso: Miso contains live fermentation cultures and delicate volatile aromatic compounds that are destroyed by boiling. The broth base should be hot but not boiling when miso is added. Whisk miso paste into a small amount of warm broth first to create a smooth paste, then incorporate into the full broth. Taste and adjust — different miso varieties vary greatly in saltiness. Wavy noodles hold miso broth: Use medium-width wavy noodles for miso ramen — their irregular surface traps the thick miso broth better than straight thin noodles. The bowl should be as hot as possible — miso ramen loses quality rapidly as it cools. Serve immediately and never let it sit.

5Service Training Module

Izakaya Service Philosophy: An izakaya is not a restaurant — it is a drinking establishment that happens to serve excellent food. The pace is unhurried. Guests arrive in groups, order progressively throughout the evening, drink consistently, and leave when they are ready. Your job is not to turn the table — it is to make the table want to stay. Revenue comes from depth of spending per guest, not speed of turnover.
// Opening Welcome
"Irasshaimase — welcome. My name is [name], I'll be taking care of you tonight. Our kitchen is Japanese izakaya style — everything is designed to share and graze. The best approach is to start with a round of small plates and drinks, then build from there as the evening goes. Can I start you with drinks while you look over the food?"
Why: Irasshaimase is the universal Japanese welcome — guests who know Japanese culture will feel the authenticity; guests who don't will be intrigued and ask about it. The ordering framework (share and graze) sets expectations and prevents the common mistake of guests ordering everything at once and being overwhelmed.
// Drinks Knowledge Demonstration
"For drinks — can I ask what you usually like? We have a full sake selection, Japanese whisky highballs, and some spirits you might not have tried. If you're beer people, we do Asahi Super Dry on draught — it's designed specifically to pair with food, very clean. If you want something lighter, our hojicha cold brew is our most popular non-alcoholic. What sounds good?"
Why: Asking what they usually like is more productive than listing options. It makes the guest feel understood and allows the server to make a targeted recommendation. Never ask "do you want drinks?" — always make the affirmative assumption and guide to a choice.
// Karaage Sell Script
"For starters — our karaage is double-fried chicken thigh with Kewpie mayo. It's what we're probably most known for. If you've never had it, it's the best fried chicken you'll try anywhere — the double-fry technique we use gives it a crunch that regular fried chicken doesn't have. It also pairs perfectly with the highball or cold beer."
Why: The best-known dish is the entry point. The double-fry detail sounds technical but is delivered as a food story — it signals kitchen craft. The beer pairing recommendation drives a drinks order simultaneously with the food order.
// Mid-Meal Check-In
"How is everything landing? Can I get you another round of drinks? — and if you're still hungry, the yakitori is worth going again on, or the okonomiyaki is good to share at the end of the meal while you finish your drinks."
Why: This check-in serves three functions: it opens a beverage reorder, it extends the food order with specific recommendations (not vague "do you want more food"), and it communicates that staying longer is welcome. The izakaya model requires guests to feel unhurried.
// Dessert Attachment Script
"For dessert — our matcha tiramisu is made with ceremonial grade Uji matcha and it's genuinely one of the things people come back for. We also do mochi ice cream in three flavours — matcha, black sesame, yuzu. Most tables end up doing both and sharing. Can I bring them?"
Why: "Most tables end up doing both" is a social proof statement that normalises ordering both desserts and removes individual hesitation. The affirmative close ("Can I bring them?") assumes yes rather than asking if they want any dessert — significantly higher attachment rate.

7Customer Retention System

// The Izakaya Return Driver
The izakaya's greatest asset is its format — it is not a special-occasion restaurant. It is a regular spot. The rhythm of ordering rounds, sharing plates, and lingering over drinks is designed for habitual return. Your job is to create regulars, not one-time customers. Every operational decision should be evaluated against: "Does this make someone want to come back next week?"
// The Seasonal Rotation Signal
"We rotate some specials seasonally — next month we're adding [dish]. If you want to be notified, we have a text list — just give your number at the front desk."
Why: The text/SMS list is your most powerful direct marketing channel. 98% SMS open rate vs 20% email. Seasonal rotation signals that there is always something new to discover — creates a reason to return beyond what they already know they love.
// The Regulars Recognition Protocol
When a guest returns a second time: "Welcome back — good to see you again. Are you in a karaage mood tonight or do you want to try some different things?" When they return a third time: remember their usual drink and have it waiting or bring it without being asked.
Why: Being remembered is the most powerful retention tool in hospitality. It converts guests into advocates. One remembered regular tells five people about the experience. The three-visit protocol (remember face, remember order, anticipate order) is achievable by trained staff at any volume level.
