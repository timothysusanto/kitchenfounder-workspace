Churrasco is not simply grilled meat. It is a Brazilian institution — born in the southern state of Rio Grande do Sul among the gauchos who drove cattle across the pampas and cooked over open fires at night. The rodízio service model, where servers carry skewered meats tableside in an unending rotation, transforms a meal into an event. This guide gives you every recipe, every cost, every script, and every number you need to build a profitable churrasco operation in the US market.

Chef's Lens

// What a Michelin-trained chef sees in Brazilian churrasco
The picanha is the most misunderstood cut in American butchery. In Brazil it is the undisputed king of the churrasco — the top sirloin cap with its thick fat layer left entirely intact, skewered in a C-shape so the fat renders slowly over charcoal and bastes the meat from within. In the US, most butchers trim it off. Your first operational task is finding a supplier who understands the cut. The fat is not waste — it is the flavour mechanism. A picanha without the fat cap is a different dish entirely. The entire churrasco philosophy is built on this principle: respect the animal, understand the cut, let fire do the work.
// What this guide does
The rodízio model is one of the most operationally elegant formats in casual dining. Fixed input cost, predictable output, continuous service flow with no à la carte complexity. When the P&L clicks into place — 52 covers at $58 average, 26% food cost, labour at 31% — the net margin is genuinely exceptional for the casual dining segment. This guide maps every cut, every fire, every service moment, and every dollar.
This is how you build a churrasco that runs like a machine.

The tapioca science: Pão de queijo uses tapioca starch, not wheat flour — making it naturally gluten-free. Polvilho azedo (sour tapioca starch) is preferred; it produces a slightly chewier, more complex result than sweet tapioca starch. The starch gelatinises when the hot liquid is added, creating a stretchy, sticky dough that is unique in texture.
2
Scald the liquid first: Bring milk, oil, and salt to a full boil. Pour immediately over the tapioca starch and beat vigorously — the hot liquid gelatinises the starch on contact. The mixture will look lumpy at first; continue beating until smooth and slightly tacky. Cool to 40°C before adding eggs.
3
Add cheese and eggs: Beat in the egg fully, then fold in the grated cheese. Traditional Minas cheese has a mild, milky flavour — Parmesan is an acceptable substitute with more saltiness. The dough should be soft, sticky, and slightly warm. Portion into 25g balls using wet hands or a small ice cream scoop.
4
Bake at 200°C / 392°F for 18–20 minutes until puffed, golden, and hollow-sounding when tapped. They deflate slightly on cooling — serve immediately from the oven. Batch-freeze unbaked balls for service efficiency: bake from frozen at 200°C for 22 minutes. Serve in a lined basket lined with a cloth to retain heat.
Chef's Tip: Pão de queijo is Brazil's most iconic food export after caipirinha. Every Brazilian recognises it. For Brazilian guests, it is an emotional comfort food. For first-time guests, it is a discovery. The hollow, chewy, cheese-scented roll is unlike any bread in Western baking culture. Serve it warm, without exception.

Make the filling: Poach chicken thighs in seasoned stock until fully cooked — 74°C / 165°F internal. Shred finely. Sauté with diced onion, garlic, and parsley until fragrant. Cool completely. Mix with cream cheese until bound. The cream cheese adds richness and helps the filling hold its shape inside the coxinha.

Chef's Tip: Coxinha is Brazil's most popular street food snack. Batch-produce and freeze unbaked — fry from frozen at 175°C for 6–7 minutes. Having a frozen batch means you can always have them available without daily production. This is one of your highest-margin entrées — protect it.

1
Understanding coalho cheese: Queijo coalho is a semi-firm Brazilian cheese with a high melting point — it grills and browns without melting into a puddle. This property makes it ideal for direct flame or grill cooking. Halloumi (Cypriot) has an almost identical cooking behaviour and is widely available in the US as a substitute.
2
Skewer and grill: Cut cheese into 3cm cubes, thread 3 pieces per skewer. Grill on a very hot charcoal or gas grill — 250°C+ surface heat. The goal is deep grill marks and a golden-brown exterior in 2 minutes per side without the cheese softening through. High heat is essential — medium heat causes sticking and collapse.
3
Glaze and finish: Remove from grill. Immediately drizzle with raw honey — the residual heat warms the honey and allows it to penetrate the surface slightly. Sprinkle dried oregano (the aroma released from dried oregano on hot food is significant — use generously). Add a few drops of malagueta sauce for the sweet-heat-savoury balance that defines this dish.
4
Serve immediately: Coalho cheese firms up as it cools and loses its yielding, squeaky texture within 3 minutes of leaving the grill. This is a tableside-speed dish — grill, glaze, plate, serve. On a beach in Northeast Brazil, this is sold from a portable grill by vendors shouting "Queijo! Queijo!" — capture that energy in your service.
Chef's Tip: This is your most visually distinctive entrée. The honey-glazed, charred cheese skewer on a rustic board looks expensive and tastes extraordinary. It is also your fastest-to-execute entrée — 6 minutes from order to table. Push it at every table.

1
What is farofa: Farofa is toasted cassava flour — the staple carbohydrate accompaniment to every Brazilian barbecue. The texture is coarse and dry, somewhere between couscous and breadcrumbs. It is sprinkled over meat and rice to add texture contrast and absorb the juices from the grilled meats. Without farofa, a churrasco feels incomplete to any Brazilian diner.
2
Render the bacon: Dice bacon into small lardons, render in a wide pan over medium heat until crispy and the fat is fully rendered. Do not drain the fat — it becomes the cooking medium for the farofa. The bacon fat toasts the cassava flour and adds deep savoury flavour.
3
Sauté onion and toast the flour: Add diced onion to the bacon fat, sauté until golden. Add butter. Add cassava flour all at once — stir constantly over medium heat for 3–4 minutes until the flour is toasted, golden, and fragrant. The flour is ready when it smells nutty and looks lightly browned.
4
Finish with egg and parsley: Push the mixture to one side, scramble eggs in the pan, then fold through the farofa. The egg adds richness and binding. Finish with fresh parsley and seasoning. Batch-produce and hold warm. Serve in a small bowl or ramekin alongside the main meats.
Chef's Tip: Farofa has a 3.6% food cost — your most profitable item on the menu. Never skip it. Brazilian diners will immediately notice if it is absent. Non-Brazilian diners who discover it will become regulars who order it every visit.

1
Desalt the bacalhau: Salt cod must be desalted before use — submerge in cold water for 24–48 hours, changing water every 8 hours. Taste after 24 hours — the fish should be pleasantly salty, not overwhelming. The desalting process also rehydrates the cod, changing its texture from stiff and dry to flaky and moist.
2
Poach and shred: Simmer desalted cod gently in water with a bay leaf for 8–10 minutes. Drain, cool, and shred finely with your fingers — remove any bones. The shred should be fine, not chunky. Combine with mashed potato, beaten egg, chopped parsley and garlic, black pepper. The mixture should hold a shape when formed into a ball.
3
Shape and coat: Using wet hands, form into oval quenelle shapes (traditional) or round balls — 35g each. Dust lightly with flour. The flour creates a thin crust during frying that keeps the fritter intact. Over-flouring creates a thick, doughy exterior that masks the delicate cod flavour.
4
Fry at 180°C / 356°F for 3–4 minutes until deep golden. The interior should be hot through and slightly creamy from the potato. Drain on a wire rack. Serve with lemon aioli. The combination of salt cod, potato, and the crispy exterior is one of the most satisfying flavour combinations in Portuguese-Brazilian cooking.
Chef's Tip: Bolinho de bacalhau is the Portuguese-Brazilian snack that transcends culture. It appears in every Brazilian boteco and pastelaria. The desalting process takes 24–48 hours — plan production accordingly. Batch-produce and freeze uncooked for service efficiency.

1
Source correctly: Picanha is the biceps femoris — the top sirloin cap with its distinctive thick fat layer. In the US it is often trimmed and sold as rump cap or coulotte. Specify to your butcher: bone-out, fat cap intact at minimum 1.5cm thickness. The fat cap is non-negotiable — it renders over the fire, bastes the meat continuously, and is the primary flavour vehicle of the dish.
2
Salt only — nothing else: Traditional churrasco picanha uses only coarse rock salt. No marinade, no oil, no pepper before cooking. The salt draws surface moisture which then re-absorbs, seasoning the meat from within. Apply generously 30 minutes before cooking, then brush off excess before it hits the fire.
3
C-shape skewer, fat side out first: Fold the picanha into a C-shape and skewer through both ends — the fat cap faces outward and hits the fire first. Cook fat-side toward the coals for the first 4 minutes — renders the fat and creates a golden crust. Rotate to flesh side for 3–4 minutes. Internal target: 52–55°C / 126–131°F for medium-rare.
4
Slice against the grain, serve immediately: Rest 3 minutes. Slice 1.5cm thick against the grain — the grain runs lengthwise, so slices are cross-sections. Each slice shows the rendered fat cap on one edge. Serve with chimichurri, farofa, and vinagrete. The picanha should be pink throughout with a golden-caramelised fat edge.
Chef's Tip: Picanha is your menu's identity. If your picanha is right, everything else is forgiven. If it is wrong, nothing else matters. The fat cap must render completely — a white, unrendered fat cap means the fire was too low or the cook time too short. Practice this cut until it is instinct.

1
Dry rub and rest overnight: Apply dry rub generously to all surfaces. Rest uncovered in refrigerator overnight. The dry rub draws moisture to the surface which reabsorbs carrying flavour deep into the meat — the same principle as a dry brine. The overnight rest also dries the surface for better crust formation.
2
Low and slow — 6 hours at 150°C / 302°F: Place ribs on a rack in a roasting pan with 200ml water in the bottom. Cover tightly with foil. Roast 5–6 hours until the collagen has fully converted to gelatin and the meat pulls back from the bone by 2cm. The probe should meet zero resistance — the "butter test."
3
Charcoal finish: Transfer the slow-cooked rib to a very hot charcoal grill for 3–4 minutes per side. This step adds the smoke flavour and caramelised bark that the oven cannot produce. 4
Present whole, slice at the table: Bring the rack to the table whole on a wooden board. Slice between bones at the table — the moment of carving creates theatre and demonstrates confidence. Each bone portion should have a deep bark exterior and fall-tender interior. The bone is structural — it insulates the meat during the long cook and adds flavour.
Chef's Tip: The 6-hour costela is your most labour-intensive item but also your highest-drama presentation. Start at 6am for dinner service. The ribs can hold at 63°C / 145°F wrapped in foil for up to 3 hours before the charcoal finish — this gives you service flexibility.

1
Marinade 4–12 hours: Combine garlic, lime juice, EVOO, parsley and oregano. The acid in the lime begins to denature the surface proteins — this is not tenderisation (acid toughens muscle fibre if over-marinated) but flavour penetration. 4 hours minimum, 12 hours maximum. Beyond 12 hours the lime acid begins to break down the surface texture unpleasantly.
2
Remove from marinade, pat dry: Wet meat steams rather than sears. Pat the flank completely dry with paper towels before grilling — any moisture on the surface prevents the Maillard reaction and produces a grey, steamed exterior instead of a brown, caramelised crust. Season with additional salt at this point.
3
High heat, fast cook: Flank steak is thin and cooks quickly — 230°C+ grill surface, 4–5 minutes per side for medium-rare. Flank has almost no intramuscular fat — it goes from perfect to overcooked in 2 minutes. Use a thermometer: pull at 52°C / 126°F internal. It will carry-over cook to 55°C during rest.
4
Rest 5 minutes, slice against the grain: The grain in flank steak runs lengthwise and is very pronounced — slicing with the grain produces tough, stringy pieces. Slice on a sharp diagonal, against the grain, into 5mm slices. The thin slice reveals the pink interior and dramatically shortens the muscle fibres, making each piece tender regardless of the cut's inherent toughness.
Chef's Tip: Fraldinha is your best value churrasco cut — flavourful, relatively affordable, and when cooked correctly, extremely satisfying. It is the cut that regulars order because they understand it. Train your staff on the slicing technique — wrong-direction slices are immediately obvious and noticeably tough.

Soak the cedar plank: Submerge cedar plank in cold water minimum 2 hours — this prevents it from catching fire on the grill. A dry plank will ignite within 3 minutes on a hot grill. The soaked plank smokes rather than burns, imparting a gentle cedar and resin aroma to the salmon that is completely distinctive.

"The highlight is the picanha — it's the top sirloin cap, Brazilian cut, cooked with just rock salt over charcoal. The fat cap renders over the fire and bastes the meat. They're room temperature and need about 20 minutes, so I mention them now so they're ready when you want them. $6.99 for three. The pudim — Brazilian flan — is chilled and ready anytime. Both worth leaving room for."
Why it works: Pre-selling at mains delivery removes the "too full" barrier. The brigadeiro production detail adds craft credibility. Mentioning both options gives guests a choice rather than a yes/no decision. This script increases dessert conversion from ~25% to ~55% in most operations.

Closing — Chef's Lens

// The technical truth about churrasco
Churrasco is one of the most forgiving cuisines to cook and one of the hardest to master. The forgiveness is in the fire — charcoal covers a multitude of mistakes with smoke and heat. The mastery is in knowing each cut intimately: its grain direction, its fat distribution, its optimal internal temperature, its resting requirement. The picanha at 52°C versus 65°C is not the same dish. The costela at 5 hours versus 7 hours is not the same dish. Temperature is the language of this cuisine. Learn it fluently.
// What changes when you run this correctly
The churrascaria that gets it right doesn't need to advertise. The smoke reaches the street. The boards of meat leaving the pass turn heads in the dining room. Tables point. Phones come out. The regulars bring their friends and tell them what to order before they sit down. This is not about food cost percentages — though the percentages here are genuinely excellent. It is about building a place people want to return to. The numbers are the foundation. The fire, the fat, and the fellowship are what you're actually selling.
Build the fire. Learn the cuts. Know your numbers. The rest follows.
