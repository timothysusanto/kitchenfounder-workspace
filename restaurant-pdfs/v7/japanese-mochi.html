The Foundation
Mochi Science — The 5 Formats & The Ingredient Rules
Mochi fails in one of two ways: too hard (wrong rice flour ratio or over-cooked) or too sticky to work with (under-cooked or wrong temperature). Understanding the science first makes every format predictable.

The 5 Commercial Mochi Formats

① Daifuku (大福)

The standard: glutinous rice cake (mochi) wrapped around a filling — most commonly koshian (smooth red bean paste). Round, soft, dusted in katakuriko (potato starch). Best known: Ichigo Daifuku (whole strawberry + koshian inside). Fresh shelf life: 1–2 days at room temp, 3 days refrigerated. Never freeze fresh daifuku — skin hardens irreversibly.

② Mochi Ice Cream

Thin mochi skin wrapped around a ball of ice cream. Commercial standard: Mikawaya (LA), Lotte Yukimi Daifuku (Japan). Key challenge: mochi skin must stay pliable at -18°C. Achieved by adding glucose syrup (lowers freezing point of skin) and keeping skin thin (≤3mm). Must be eaten within 5 min of pulling from freezer — skin hardens as it approaches room temp.

③ Warabi Mochi (わらび餅)

Made from warabiko (bracken starch) or a substitute blend — NOT glutinous rice. Translucent, wobbly jelly-like texture. Far more delicate than daifuku. Served dusted in kinako (roasted soy flour) with kuromitsu (black sugar syrup). A summer specialty. The highest-margin mochi format because ingredients are cheap and the product looks premium.

④ Dango (団子)

Rice flour dumplings on a skewer — denser than daifuku mochi. 3 varieties dominate: Mitarashi (sweet soy glaze), Hanami (pink-white-green tricolour, cherry blossom season), Yomogi (mugwort, spring). The skewer format is the highest-throughput mochi product — make in large batches, skewer 3–4 per stick, apply sauce to order. Best for market stalls and food hall kiosks.

⑤ Sakura / Specialty Mochi

Kanto-style Sakura Mochi: thin crepe-like dorayaki base wrapped around koshian, wrapped in a salt-pickled sakura leaf. Kansai-style: traditional pink mochi dome. Seasonal and ceremonial — the sakura leaf is eaten. Also includes Kusa Mochi (mugwort mochi — spring green), Yomogi, and modern fusion formats.

⑥ Modern Frozen Premium

The global commercial growth driver — premium mochi ice cream in boutique flavours (black sesame, yuzu, hojicha, matcha, sakura). Sold individually ($4–8 each) or in gift boxes (6 for $32+). Best retail format for food halls, online orders, and gift markets. This is where the highest per-unit margin and the highest gift-box order value live.

🔬 THE MOCHI SKIN SCIENCE — Why Shiratamako vs Joshinko vs Mochiko Matters

Shiratamako (白玉粉): Premium glutinous rice flour, wet-milled then dried into granules. Produces the smoothest, most elastic mochi. Most expensive. Use for daifuku and any premium mochi where texture is the selling point. The skin will be silky, stretchable, and pillowy.

Mochiko (餅粉): American-market glutinous rice flour (Bob's Red Mill standard). Dry-milled — produces a slightly drier, stickier mochi. Acceptable for mochi ice cream (frozen format masks the difference). Lower cost. Suitable for high-volume production where Shiratamako cost is prohibitive.

Joshinko (上新粉): Non-glutinous rice flour. Used for dango — produces a firmer, denser texture than glutinous rice. Does NOT produce a stretchy mochi. Not interchangeable with Shiratamako. Using the wrong flour is the single most common mochi failure.

Warabiko (蕨粉) / Kuzu / Tapioca blend: For warabi mochi — bracken starch (true warabiko) is expensive and rare; most commercial warabi mochi uses a blend of tapioca starch, kudzu, and potato starch. The translucent jelly texture cannot be achieved with glutinous rice flour. These are completely different products with different starch chemistry.

The water ratio rule: Too little water = skin tears when wrapping. Too much = skin is too sticky to handle. Target: 1 part Shiratamako to 0.85–0.90 parts water by weight. Adjust ±5% based on humidity — humid environments need slightly less water.

🔬 STORAGE PROTOCOLS — Definitive

Fresh Daifuku (all varieties): Room temperature (below 20°C): 1 day. Refrigerator: 2–3 days (the skin firms but remains edible — bring to room temp 20 min before serving). ❌ NEVER FREEZE fresh daifuku. The mochi skin crystallises and becomes hard and dry on thaw. It cannot be restored.

Mochi Ice Cream: Freezer -18°C: up to 3 months (commercial production standard). Serving: pull from freezer, serve immediately on a cold plate. The skin is at its most pliable at -12 to -8°C. Eat within 5 minutes — after 8 min the skin begins to stiffen as it approaches room temperature. Never re-freeze after partial thaw.

Warabi Mochi: Refrigerator: maximum 2 days. NOT frozen. The starch structure breaks down irreversibly when frozen — texture becomes grainy and watery. Warabi mochi must be made fresh daily. This is a same-day product.

Dango: Freshly made: 12 hours at room temp. Refrigerator: 1 day (skin hardens — microwave 10 seconds to soften before serving). ❌ Do not store with sauce — sauce applied to order only. Mitarashi sauce refrigerates separately up to 5 days.

Prepared fillings (koshian / tsubu-an): Refrigerator: 5 days in airtight container. Freezer: 3 months in portioned balls. Always portion fillings before freezing — thaw individual portions as needed.

Koshian — Smooth Red Bean Paste
こしあん · The Foundation of Japanese Confectionery
$0.28 / 30g portion
FC 12% · Freeze 3 months
Ingredients — makes approx 1.2kg finished koshian
IngredientQtyCost
Dried azuki beans (small red beans)500g$2.50
Caster sugar350g$0.70
Water (for cooking)2 litres—
Salt¼ tsp—
Method
  1. Rinse azuki beans. Cover with cold water, bring to boil. Discard this first water (removes bitterness — shibukiri method). Rinse.
  2. Cover again with 1.5 litres fresh water. Simmer 60–75 min until beans crush completely between fingers with no resistance. Add water as needed to keep beans submerged. Simmer 60–75 min
  3. Pass through a fine-mesh sieve or food mill (mouli) — this is what makes koshian smooth vs tsubu-an (chunky). Press every bean through. Discard skins.
  4. Transfer smooth bean purée to a muslin cloth. Squeeze out maximum water. The drier the bean paste now, the more controllable the cooking step.
  5. Transfer drained paste to a heavy-bottomed pot. Add sugar and salt. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly with a wooden spatula, for 15–25 min until the paste pulls away from the sides of the pot and holds a soft ball shape when dropped from a spoon. Cook until 110°C / paste holds shape
  6. Cool completely. Portion into 30g balls. Refrigerate in airtight container or freeze individually on a tray then transfer to a bag.
🔬 The Shibukiri Method (渋切り):

Discarding the first boiling water removes shibumi — the harsh tannin-astringent compounds in the azuki bean skin. Without this step, the finished koshian has a bitter edge that no amount of sugar can mask. This is a non-negotiable step in any serious wagashi kitchen. The second-boil liquid, by contrast, produces the clean, sweet, earthy red bean flavour that is the foundation of Japanese confectionery.

Chef's Tip: The correct moisture level of finished koshian is critical for wrapping. Too wet: the filling oozes through the mochi skin during wrapping. Too dry: it cracks and falls apart inside the mochi. Test: roll a ball in your palm — it should hold its shape without sticking to your hands and without crumbling. This is the koshian standard. If it sticks: return to the pot and cook 5 more minutes. If it crumbles: add 1 tsp water and stir over low heat.

Tsubu-an
つぶあん · Chunky Red Bean
$0.07 / 30g

Same process as koshian — STOP before straining. After cooking azuki to soft, add sugar directly in the pot. Cook stirring until thick enough to hold shape. Beans remain whole but very soft. Tsubu-an has textural contrast that koshian lacks — some guests specifically request it. Keep both in production: koshian for daifuku (smooth skin pairs with smooth filling), tsubu-an for dango and dorayaki (texture contrast).

Chef's Tip: Tsubu-an is faster — no straining step. Same cook time, 30% less labour. Stock both; tsubu-an is your production efficiency filling.

Ichigo Daifuku — Strawberry Daifuku
いちご大福 · Japan's Most Iconic Seasonal Sweet
$0.65 / piece
FC 14% · Menu $4.50
Ingredients — batch of 20 pieces
IngredientQtyCost
Shiratamako (premium glutinous rice flour)200g$1.80
Caster sugar50g$0.10
Water180ml—
Koshian, portioned into 25g balls500g total$1.60
Fresh strawberries, medium, hulled (point facing up)20$4.80
Katakuriko (potato starch for dusting)100g$0.20
Method
  1. Flatten each koshian ball to a disc. Place a hulled strawberry point-up in the centre. Wrap koshian around the strawberry — the point should just protrude slightly from the top or be fully covered (your choice of style). Refrigerate wrapped strawberries 30 min to firm. Chill wrapped strawberries 30 min
  2. Combine Shiratamako, sugar, and water in a microwave-safe bowl. Stir well. Microwave 2 min, stir, microwave 1 more min, stir. Repeat in 30-second bursts until the mixture is thick, glossy, and pulls away from the bowl in a mass. Total: approximately 4–5 min. Microwave method: 4–5 min total
  3. Alternatively (traditional): cook mochi mixture in a heavy pot over medium heat, stirring constantly with a wet wooden spatula for 10–12 min until glossy, thick, and translucent.
  4. Turn out onto a tray dusted generously with katakuriko. Dust the top also. Cool 5 min — mochi must be warm but handleable (55–60°C). Too hot: it sticks and tears. Too cold: it resists stretching. Work at 55–60°C
  5. Pull off a 35–40g piece of mochi. Press flat in your dusted palm. Place koshian-wrapped strawberry in the centre. Pinch mochi edges together underneath, sealing completely. The strawberry point faces down (the dome at the top is the presentation side).
  6. Place sealed-side down on a katakuriko-dusted tray. Dust lightly. Store uncovered at room temperature (not in airtight — condensation softens the skin). Serve within 6 hours for best texture.
Chef's Tip — Strawberry Season: Ichigo Daifuku is intrinsically seasonal (peak: November–March in Japan; March–May in Australia). Position it as a seasonal item — "available while strawberries are at peak." The scarcity and seasonality justify a premium price and create return customers who come specifically for it. Off-season: substitute with fresh lychee, white grape, or melon. These are accepted modern variations, not compromises.

Mochi Ice Cream — Matcha, Yuzu, Black Sesame
もちアイスクリーム · Premium Frozen Format
$0.85 / piece
FC 17% · Freeze 3 months
Ingredients — batch of 24 pieces (8 per flavour)
IngredientQtyNotes
Mochiko (glutinous rice flour)200gDry-milled, acceptable for frozen format
Caster sugar60g—
Glucose syrup30gCRITICAL — keeps skin pliable at -18°C
Water160ml—
Premium ice cream (3 flavours), portioned 30g balls720g totalFreeze balls solid before wrapping — minimum 2 hours

Matcha powder (for matcha skin)8gAdd to dry ingredients before water
Method
  1. Portion ice cream into 30g balls using an ice cream scoop. Freeze solid minimum 2 hours before wrapping. This is non-negotiable — soft ice cream will melt during the wrapping process. Ice cream balls: freeze 2 hrs minimum
  2. Combine Mochiko, sugar, glucose syrup, water (+ matcha for matcha batch). Mix until smooth. Cook in microwave: 2 min stir, 1 min stir, 30-sec bursts until thick, glossy, translucent. Glucose syrup is what creates the pliable frozen skin — do not omit.
  3. Roll mochi onto katakuriko-dusted surface. Roll to 3mm thickness — thinner than daifuku. Cut into 10cm circles.
  4. Work FAST with cold hands. Place a frozen ice cream ball in the centre of a mochi circle. Gather edges, pinch shut. Immediately wrap in cling film and freeze. Work fast — 60 seconds per piece max
  5. Freeze minimum 3 hours before serving. Store in airtight container -18°C up to 3 months.
  6. Service: pull from freezer, place on cold plate (pre-chilled). Serve immediately. State: "Eat within 5 minutes — the skin is best while the ice cream is firm."
🔬 Glucose Syrup — The Pliability Key

Standard mochi skin without glucose syrup freezes to a hard, tough, chewy exterior that is unpleasant at -18°C. Glucose syrup (low-DE glucose, not corn syrup) has hygroscopic properties — it retains moisture and depresses the freezing point of the mochi skin slightly, keeping it soft and pliable even when frozen. The ratio: 15% of total mochi weight as glucose syrup is the minimum effective level. Without it, your mochi ice cream skin is cardboard. With it, it is pillowy silk even straight from the freezer.

Sakura Mochi — Kanto Style
桜餅 · Spring's Most Iconic Confection
$0.65 / piece
FC 12% · Spring Only
Ingredients — 16 pieces
IngredientQtyCost
Domyoji-ko (cracked glutinous rice) OR fine semolina as substitute200g$1.60
Boiling water200ml—
Caster sugar40g$0.08
Natural pink food colouring (sakura pink)3 drops$0.02
Koshian, portioned 25g balls400g$1.28
Salt-pickled sakura leaves, soaked 30 min in cold water, patted dry16 leaves$3.20
Method
  1. Combine domyoji-ko, sugar, pink food colouring, and boiling water. Cover tightly with cling film. Steam over boiling water 20 minutes until rice grains are fully hydrated and sticky. Steam 20 min
  2. Cool to 55°C (workable but warm). The texture should be sticky and cohesive — each grain slightly visible but the mass holds together. This is the textural identity of Kanto Sakura Mochi vs the smooth skin of other formats.
  3. Wet your hands. Take 35g rice mass, press flat. Place koshian ball in centre. Wrap rice around filling — the slightly rough texture of the domyoji rice is intentional and traditional.
  4. Wrap each formed mochi in a de-salted, dried sakura leaf — the leaf curls around the mochi cylinder shape. The leaf's faint salt and cherry blossom aroma perfumes the mochi underneath it. The leaf is eaten — it is part of the flavour, not a garnish.
  5. Display at room temperature (not refrigerated — cold mochi loses its soft texture). Sell within 6 hours. Label as seasonal.
Chef's Tip — The Salt-Pickled Sakura Leaf: Source these from Japanese specialty grocers (Yamamotoyama brand, or similar). The leaf must be rinsed and soaked 30 min in cold water to reduce the salt to a pleasant gentle trace — not enough to taste salty, just enough to create the counterpoint to the sweet koshian inside. This salt-sweet contrast is the entire philosophy of sakura mochi in one bite. Do not skip the soak.

Tip: Oshiruko is a low-labour, very high-margin winter item. Batch-cook the tsubu-an soup; toast mochi to order (2 min). A $5.90 menu price for what is essentially red bean soup with toast is completely accepted in a quality Japanese confectionery context.

Guest has never eaten mochi before — standing at the counter looking uncertain.

Kitchen Workflow — Daily Production Protocol

Morning prep (6:00–9:00 AM):

  • Cook fresh mochi (Shiratamako method) for daifuku — make while still warm
  • Pull koshian and matcha-an from fridge, portion required balls
  • Wrap today's daifuku batch — complete by 9:30 AM
  • Prep warabi mochi for same-day service — pour, chill 1 hour
  • Dango: boil, skewer, sauce ready in station
  • Pull frozen mochi ice cream from blast freezer to display freezer

Mise en place rule: Every mochi piece on the display must be made the morning it is sold. Yesterday's daifuku is discarded at close. This is a non-negotiable freshness standard — communicated clearly to staff and to guests ("made fresh this morning" is on the menu).

Batch preparation (weekly):

  • Monday: cook koshian in bulk (3kg batch → portion and freeze)
  • Tuesday: mochi ice cream production batch (freeze for 2-week supply)
  • Wednesday: shiro-an base + flavoured an variants
  • Thursday: dango sauce production, kuromitsu syrup

Equipment critical path: Stand mixer → available 6–9 AM daily. Blast freezer → used Monday and Tuesday for weekly batch. Display refrigerator → 4°C maintained, temperature log checked twice daily (HACCP compliance).

Staff Structure (30–35 sqm shop)

  • Head Mochi Artisan: 1 × full-time — all production, recipe maintenance, quality control. This person defines the shop's quality standard.
  • Counter + Service: 2 × part-time (weekdays) / 3 × part-time (weekends). Trained in all service scripts, seasonal story, taste offers, gift box upsell.
  • Workshop Facilitator: Can be Head Artisan on Saturdays (premium rate) or a second trained artisan.

🍃 Michelin Chef's Lens — The Transience of Mochi

Mochi is built on impermanence. The freshly made daifuku skin is softest in the first hour after wrapping. The sakura leaf's faint salt perfume fades by hour four. The ichigo daifuku is at peak beauty when the strawberry is still firm, the koshian still holds its shape, and the mochi skin still carries the warmth of its making.

This is not a weakness. It is the entire philosophy. Japanese confectionery — wagashi — has always been a temporal art. The cherry blossom mochi exists because cherry blossoms fall in two weeks. The yuzu daifuku exists because yuzu is harvested once, in November, in the river valleys of Kochi. These products are not seasonal because of supply limitations. They are seasonal because seasonality is the point.

The word most central to understanding Japanese confectionery is mono no aware (物の哀れ) — the bittersweet awareness of impermanence. A sakura blossom is beautiful in part because it will fall. Sakura mochi is perfect in part because it will be gone by April.

The modern dessert industry has largely abandoned this principle in favour of year-round availability and operational simplicity. The mochi shop that reclaims seasonal impermanence — that makes guests understand that today's sakura mochi will not be here tomorrow — creates something no franchise can replicate: genuine emotional connection with a product.

"花は桜木、人は武士。" — Hana wa sakuragi, hito wa bushi. Among flowers the cherry blossom, among men the samurai. And among sweets — the mochi. Beautiful, temporary, and exactly right for its season.
