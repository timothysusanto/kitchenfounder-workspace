Dim Sum Folding Station

  • Dedicated dim sum prep table — separate from hot production
  • Cool environment (air-conditioned or separate cool area) — dough dries out at high temperature
  • 1 dim sum chef can produce 200-250 pieces/hour at full speed
  • Har gow pleating benchmark: 7+ pleats in under 30 seconds per piece
  • Pre-portioned filling trays — weighed portions prevent size inconsistency
  • Parchment paper squares pre-cut for steamer lining
Cool workspace | Pre-portion scale | Bamboo steamer × 30 | Cleaver for har gow

High-BTU Wok Station

  • Minimum 100,000 BTU per burner — the wok hei flavour requires this heat
  • Wok hei is the Cantonese "breath of the wok" — the slight char, caramelised edges, and smoky aroma only achievable at extreme heat
  • Never use a domestic cooktop for fried rice or stir-fry in a commercial setting
  • Wok ladle + wok spatula always together — tossing motion is the technique
  • Cold rice for fried rice in the walk-in: day-old rice only, never freshly cooked
100k BTU wok range × 2 | Wok ladle × 2 | Cold rice supply

Tea Service Station

  • Hot water urn at 95°C (not boiling — boiling water scalds green and white teas)
  • Tea selection display: loose leaf in labeled jars with tasting notes on display cards
  • Per-table teapots (ceramic preferred — heat retention) + tea strainers
  • Gongfu ceremony set: small clay pot + 6 small cups + brewing board
  • Refill policy: unlimited hot water for the base tea price — this is the tradition
95°C urn | Tea jar display | Ceramic teapots × 20 | Gongfu set × 2

Char Siu Roasting

  • Dedicated BBQ oven with rack and drip tray for char siu
  • The char siu aroma from the oven fills the dining room — this is a feature, not a problem
  • Baste every 10 minutes during final 30 minutes for glaze build-up
  • Rest char siu 5-10 minutes before slicing — juices redistribute
  • Char siu strips in walk-in ready for bao, cheung fun, lo mai gai throughout service
BBQ roasting oven | Basting brush | Rack + drip tray | Slicing board

6Service Training — The Yum Cha Table Scripts

Service philosophy: Yum cha service is circular and informal by Cantonese tradition — not a linear three-course Western meal. Guests order continuously, dishes arrive as they are ready, and the tea is refilled indefinitely. For a Western market teahouse, the key is to maintain the warmth and informality of the yum cha ritual while providing enough structure for guests who are unfamiliar with the format. The service briefing should always begin with tea — not menus, not specials, not the wifi password. Tea first.
// Opening Script — Table Arrival
"Welcome — we start with tea. What do you like? We have pu-erh, which is the traditional dim sum pairing — earthy, rich, helps with digestion. Jasmine if you want something floral and light. Or Dragon Well green tea from Hangzhou if you want to try something special. Once you've chosen your tea, the menu is just for guidance — you're welcome to order as much or as little as you like, whenever you like. More baskets arrive throughout the meal. There's no rush here."
Leading with the tea question before the food menu resets the guest's expectation from "Western restaurant" to "teahouse." The "no rush" reassurance signals the yum cha service model without requiring a lengthy explanation. The tea recommendation names specific varieties with brief descriptions — educating without overwhelming.
// Har Gow Introduction Script
"The har gow is our dim sum chef's signature piece — it's the dish every dim sum chef in Cantonese cuisine is specifically trained and judged on. The wrapper is made from wheat starch and tapioca — no gluten — which is why it's translucent when steamed. You should be able to see the prawn inside. There should be at least seven pleats on one side — that's the technical standard. Go ahead and check. The filling is just prawn, bamboo shoot, ginger, and sesame. Nothing else. The wrapper is the skill."
The invitation to "check the pleats" turns a food explanation into a participatory experience. Guests will count the pleats, show their table companions, take a photo. This creates engagement and a personal story attached to the dish. The "nothing else" explanation of the simple filling signals confidence in technique over sauce complexity.
// Tea Refill Script
"Traditionally, when you want more hot water, you leave the teapot lid slightly ajar — that's the signal in Cantonese teahouses that you need a refill. But we'll also come by. If you want to order a different tea for the second pot, the pu-erh and oolong get better with more steepings — the flavour deepens each time."
Teaching the teapot lid signal gives guests a cultural piece of knowledge they will use for the rest of their lives. The upsell to "different tea for the second pot" is gentle and educational. The note about pu-erh improving with steeping increases the perceived value of the tea and encourages second tea orders.
// Egg Tart Close Script (at dessert)
"Before you wrap up — the egg tarts just came out of the oven. They're best warm, within about fifteen minutes. The custard is still slightly trembling in the centre. It's the version they make in Hong Kong — flaky pastry shell, not the shortcrust Portuguese version. $5.99 for three. Three people, one each."
"Just came out of the oven" creates urgency. "Fifteen minutes" creates scarcity. "Custard still trembling" is a sensory description that creates anticipation. The specific note about HK vs Portuguese style signals culinary knowledge. The three-for-three table math closes the table softly without pressure.
