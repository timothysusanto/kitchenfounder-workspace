Method

1
Heat flat griddle or tawa to medium-high. Add 15g butter, sweat onion 2 min until soft.
2
Add ginger-garlic paste, cook 1 min. Add tomato, cook down 2 min until jammy.
3
Add all vegetables and mashed potato. Mix and mash on the griddle. Add pau bhaji masala and 30ml water. Cook, pressing and folding, for 3 min until unified.
4
Add remaining butter, fold through. Taste — it needs to be spicy, tangy, rich. Adjust salt and a squeeze of lemon.
5
Slice pau rolls, butter generously, toast cut-side on griddle until golden. Plate bhaji in a small cast iron or bowl, garnish with raw onion, coriander, lemon wedge. Rolls alongside.
DAIRY: butterGLUTEN: pau rolls
Pro tip: The bhaji is better the second day. Par-cook a big batch, portion and reheat to order on the tawa with a fresh knob of butter. The mashing on the griddle is the signature move — do it in front of guests at an open kitchen if possible.

Why it works: Gives guests permission to eat with their hands. Sets a casual, joyful tone for the meal.

DAIRYGLUTENTREE NUTS

Method

1
Mix khoya, flour, baking soda with just enough milk to form soft dough. Do not over-knead. Rest 5 min. Roll into smooth balls ~25g each — no cracks.
2
Make syrup: sugar + water (1:1 ratio) + cardamom + rose water. Bring to boil, simmer 5 min until slightly sticky. Keep warm.
3
Deep fry balls at 150°C / 300°F — low and slow. They need 6–8 min to cook through without burning. Turn gently. They should be deep golden-brown.
4
Immediately transfer to warm syrup. Soak minimum 30 min (overnight is better). Serve warm, 3 per plate, spooned with syrup, crushed pistachios on top.
💡 Chef's tip: The most common mistake is frying too hot — the outside browns before the centre cooks. Low and slow is the rule. If a ball cracks in the fryer, the dough is too dry. Add a few drops of milk to the batch.

Tandoor Deep-Dive — Your Most Important Piece of Equipment

The tandoor is the heart of a Bombay Cafe kitchen. It is not optional — it is the piece of equipment that makes chicken tikka, naan, seekh kebab and lamb chops possible at the authentic quality level. A charcoal grill is not a substitute. An oven is not a substitute. The clay walls of a tandoor radiate intense, even heat at 400°C that chars the exterior while keeping the interior moist — that is physics you cannot replicate with any other equipment.

Gas vs Charcoal tandoor: Gas is preferred for US commercial kitchens — easier to regulate, consistent temperature, meets most health code requirements without special permitting. Charcoal gives slightly better flavour but requires more management and may need additional fire code approval.

Pre-heat discipline: Your tandoor must be lit 45 minutes before service. Every morning. No exceptions. A cold tandoor produces steamed tikka, not charred tikka. The difference is immediately visible and tasted.

Loading and skewers: Use flat-blade skewers (not round) — chicken and kebab mix stays on. Load maximum 8 skewers at once in a medium tandoor. Over-crowding drops temperature. Under-loading wastes heat. Learn your tandoor's sweet spot and batch accordingly.

6Service Training Module

Service philosophy: In Indian hospitality tradition, a guest is never allowed to leave hungry or un-refreshed. That principle — applied to every table, every service, every night — is what builds a great restaurant. Your FOH team must understand the food, believe in it, and sell it with genuine enthusiasm. Scripts are a starting point. Mastery is when they don't sound like scripts anymore.

Our pau bhaji is a must — it's a Mumbai street classic, spiced potato on a butter-toasted roll. And if you eat meat, the chicken tikka is our most popular starter. 10Chef's Lens — The Bombay Cafe in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Bombay Cafe in the United States
The Bombay Cafe concept is one of the most compelling restaurant opportunities in the current American market. Indian cuisine is the fastest-growing major cuisine category in the US, with the market expected to surpass $8 billion by 2027. And yet, outside of certain metropolitan areas, quality Indian dining is still genuinely hard to find. The gap between what Indian food can be and what most Americans have experienced is enormous. That gap is your opportunity.

What Dishoom proved in London — and what this guide is built around — is that Indian food does not need to be cheap to be full. It needs to be excellent, hospitable, and consistent. The queue outside Dishoom at 8pm on a Tuesday is not because of marketing. It's because of masala chai and lamb chops and keema pau and the feeling that the restaurant genuinely cares whether you had a good time.

The dishes in this guide are not restaurant inventions. They are the food of Mumbai street stalls, Irani cafes, home kitchens, and wedding feasts. They have been eaten and loved for generations. Your job as an operator is not to create them — it is to honour them. Use the right ingredients. Cook with the right technique. Serve with the right warmth.

The cocktail program is your creative space. The Mumbai Mule, the Chai Old Fashioned, the Saffron Rose Gin Sour — these do not exist at a street stall in Mumbai. They exist at a Bombay Cafe in the United States, where your guests want the soul of Indian hospitality filtered through the lens of an American bar. That is your unique position: deeply rooted in culture, fully fluent in the market you are serving.

The tandoor is your commitment device. When you invest in a clay tandoor oven, you are telling your team, your guests, and your market: we are doing this properly. You cannot cut corners on tikka when you own a tandoor. You cannot serve mediocre naan when you have a 400-degree clay oven running every service. The equipment forces quality.

One last thing. In Indian hospitality tradition, a guest is never allowed to leave hungry or un-refreshed. Atithi devo bhava — the guest is God. That principle, applied to every table, every service, every night — is what builds a great restaurant. The numbers in this guide will only work if the hospitality is real.
The food is the foundation. The hospitality is the building. — KitchenFounder
