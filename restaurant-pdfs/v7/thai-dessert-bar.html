A Letter from the Kitchen

I grew up with the smell of pandan leaves simmering in coconut milk. My grandmother in Chiang Mai would press fresh coconut cream through muslin cloth every morning — not because she had to, but because she knew the difference it made. That memory lives in every dessert we create here.

When I came to Sydney as a pastry apprentice, I carried those flavours with me. Training under French-trained chefs sharpened my technique, but it was always the Thai soul — the balance of sweet, creamy, and fragrant — that guided my instincts. Over years working in both Bangkok patisseries and Sydney's dessert scene, I developed the conviction that Thai sweets deserve the same reverence as any great European pastry tradition.

A celebrated Sydney Thai dessert bar proved that conviction right. The queues stretched around the block on opening night. Sydneysiders fell in love with Mango Sticky Rice done properly — premium Nam Dok Mai mangoes, coconut cream that's never from a tin, sticky rice soaked overnight. The simplicity of authentic Thai dessert is also its sophistication.

This guide holds 28 recipes: from beloved street khanom to refined plated desserts. Every recipe carries the technique rationale, the fail-proof tip, and the plating direction that elevates a good dish into a memorable experience. Metric weights throughout. No shortcuts on quality.

Thai desserts are an act of love. They take time, attention, and respect for tradition. I hope these recipes become yours to treasure.

— Nattaya Srisomwong, Thai-Australian Pastry Chef
Concept

A Thai dessert bar celebrating authentic khanom tradition alongside contemporary plated desserts. Premium ingredients, classical technique, Sydney hospitality. Every dish rooted in real Thai pastry culture.

Mango Sticky Rice
Khao Niao Mamuang — premium Nam Dok Mai mango, jasmine-scented sticky rice, salted coconut cream, toasted sesame · Serves 2
$4.80
Food Cost
GF VG DF

Ingredients

  • Glutinous rice200g
  • Water (soaking)sufficient
  • Coconut cream (full-fat)200ml
  • Caster sugar50g
  • Fine sea salt4g
  • Pandan leaf (tied in knot)2 leaves
  • Nam Dok Mai mango, ripe2 whole
  • Coconut cream (sauce)80ml
  • Palm sugar, shaved15g
  • Fine salt (sauce)2g
  • Rice flour (thickener)5g
  • Toasted white sesame seeds8g
  • Fresh pandan leaf (garnish)1 strip

Method

  1. Soak glutinous rice in cold water for minimum 4 hours, preferably overnight. Drain and rinse thoroughly until water runs clear.
  2. Line a bamboo steamer with muslin cloth or banana leaf. Spread rice evenly, 3–4cm thick. Steam over rapidly boiling water for 20–25 minutes until grains are tender and translucent, turning once at 12 minutes.
  3. While rice steams, warm 200ml coconut cream with sugar, salt, and knotted pandan leaves over low heat. Stir until sugar dissolves — do not boil. Remove pandan.
  4. Transfer hot rice to a bowl. Pour warm coconut cream mixture over rice, fold gently with a spatula. Cover with cling film and rest 15 minutes for absorption.
  5. Prepare sauce: heat 80ml coconut cream with palm sugar and salt until dissolved. Whisk rice flour with 10ml cold water, add to sauce, stir over low heat 2 minutes until lightly thickened. Cool to room temperature.
  6. Peel mangoes and slice cheeks cleanly, then slice each cheek into fan of 5–6 pieces, keeping base intact.
  7. To plate: mould sticky rice into a neat oval using a rice mould or wet hands. Place beside fanned mango. Spoon coconut sauce over rice. Scatter sesame seeds. Garnish with pandan strip.
Chef Technique Note

The overnight soak is non-negotiable — it hydrates the starch so the rice steams evenly without gummy cores. The coconut cream absorption must happen while rice is hot; the starch gelatinises and locks the cream in, creating that characteristic sticky-yet-individual grain texture.

Fail-Proof Tip

Always salt your coconut cream — Thai desserts rely on the sweet-salty balance, and undersalted cream tastes flat. The sauce should taste almost too salty alone; paired with sweet mango and rice, it becomes perfect. Test with a pinch more salt if in doubt.

Plating Note

Use a fan of 5 mango slices at 45° to the rice mould. Drizzle sauce in a controlled arc over rice only — never flood the plate. Scatter sesame seeds last for contrast. A twisted pandan strip adds height and signals the fragrance within.

Sourcing Nam Dok Mai mangoes from Thai grocers (Haymarket, Cabramatta) when in season Oct–Jan. Off-season substitute R2E2 mango — inferior flavour but workable. Palm sugar from Thai or Indonesian grocery stores; avoid refined substitutes.

Tub Tim Grob
Red Ruby — water chestnut rubies in tapioca coat, jasmine-scented coconut milk, crushed ice · Serves 2
$2.90
Food Cost
GF VG DF NF

Ingredients

  • Water chestnuts (canned), drained200g
  • Red food colouring (natural)3ml
  • Tapioca flour80g
  • Coconut milk (full-fat)400ml
  • Caster sugar60g
  • Fine sea salt3g
  • Jasmine flowers or extract4 flowers or 2ml
  • Crushed ice200g
  • Pandan leaf2 leaves

Method

  1. Dice water chestnuts into rough 1cm cubes. Toss with red food colouring, coating evenly. Rest 5 minutes.
  2. Coat coloured chestnuts thoroughly in tapioca flour, pressing flour into crevices. Shake off excess — you want a full, even coat.
  3. Bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Drop in coated chestnuts in batches. Cook 3–4 minutes until tapioca coat turns translucent and floats. Remove with slotted spoon directly into cold water. Drain.
  4. Prepare coconut milk syrup: combine coconut milk, sugar, salt, and pandan leaves in saucepan. Heat gently until sugar dissolves, infuse with jasmine flowers off heat for 10 minutes. Strain and chill completely.
  5. To serve: place generous scoop of crushed ice in a chilled bowl. Pile ruby chestnuts on top. Pour cold coconut syrup around and over ice. Garnish with a jasmine flower or pandan strip.
Chef Technique Note

The double-texture is the dish's magic: the crisp water chestnut inside the yielding tapioca shell. This requires the chestnuts be fully dry before coating — any moisture prevents tapioca adhesion. Coat generously; thin coating tears during cooking and loses the jewel effect.

Fail-Proof Tip

Cook chestnuts in small batches — crowding drops water temperature, causing uneven cooking and tapioca clumping. Transfer directly from boiling water to cold water bath; this sets the tapioca coat and stops cooking. Chestnuts can be prepared 4 hours ahead and kept refrigerated.

Plating Note

Serve in a clear glass bowl or deep dish so the red rubies glowing through white coconut milk is fully visible. Crushed ice should peek through the chestnuts. The coconut milk should be well-chilled and poured tableside for drama — a small jug on the side elevates the presentation.

Sourcing Fresh water chestnuts give superior crunch but require peeling. Canned are reliable year-round. Natural red food colouring from beetroot powder at 15g per 100ml water gives a deep crimson without artificial dyes.

Bua Loy
Floating Lotus — pandan and taro-filled glutinous rice balls in warm coconut soup · Serves 2
$3.20
Food Cost
GF VG DF

Ingredients

  • Glutinous rice flour150g
  • Pandan juice (fresh-pressed)60ml
  • Warm water30ml
  • Taro, steamed and mashed100g
  • Palm sugar, shaved40g
  • Coconut cream (for filling)30ml
  • Coconut milk (soup base)500ml
  • Pandan leaves (soup)4 leaves
  • Palm sugar (soup)50g
  • Fine sea salt4g
  • Sesame seeds, toasted6g

Method

  1. Make pandan juice: blend 8 fresh pandan leaves with 80ml water, strain through fine sieve. Use 60ml for dough.
  2. Mix glutinous rice flour with pandan juice and warm water until a smooth, pliable dough forms. It should not crack when rolled. Cover and rest 10 minutes.
  3. Prepare taro filling: combine mashed taro with palm sugar and coconut cream in saucepan, stir over low heat until thick and pulls from sides. Cool completely, divide into 16 equal balls (approx 8g each).
  4. Divide rice dough into 16 portions. Flatten each, place taro ball in centre, seal and roll smooth. Set aside on lightly oiled tray.
  5. Prepare coconut soup: combine coconut milk, knotted pandan leaves, palm sugar, and salt. Heat gently until sugar dissolves. Taste — it should be lightly sweet and fragrant. Keep warm.
  6. Bring separate pot of water to boil. Cook rice balls in batches 4–5 minutes until they float and have puffed slightly. Remove with slotted spoon.
  7. To serve: ladle warm coconut soup into bowl. Add 8 rice balls per serve. Garnish with toasted sesame seeds and a pandan leaf strip.
Chef Technique Note

Fresh pandan juice rather than extract gives a truer green and a grassier, more complex fragrance. The rice ball dough must be pliable but not sticky — if cracking, add water teaspoon by teaspoon. Taro filling must be completely cooled before filling or it softens the dough during rolling.

Fail-Proof Tip

Float is your doneness signal — undercooked balls sink, properly cooked ones float cleanly. Give them 30 seconds more after first float. The soup should be warm, not boiling, when serving — boiling coconut milk splits and loses its silk. Keep at a gentle simmer.

Plating Note

Use a shallow, wide bowl to show the balls floating in white coconut soup. Alternate plain white balls with the taro-filled ones for visual variety. A diagonal pandan leaf across the rim and a scattering of sesame seeds adds elegance without clutter.

Sourcing Fresh pandan leaves from Asian supermarkets (Woolworths in Cabramatta, Harris Farm, Thai grocers). Thai taro has a natural purple hue when steamed — significantly more flavourful than regular taro. Source from Cabramatta or Thai suppliers.

Khanom Krok
Coconut Rice Pancakes — crispy-bottomed, custard-topped, spring onion and sweet corn filling · Serves 2 (12 pieces)
$2.40
Food Cost
GF VG DF

Ingredients

  • Rice flour120g
  • Glutinous rice flour30g
  • Coconut milk (base batter)200ml
  • Water100ml
  • Caster sugar (base)20g
  • Fine salt (base)3g
  • Coconut cream (topping batter)150ml
  • Rice flour (topping)20g
  • Caster sugar (topping)25g
  • Fine salt (topping)4g
  • Spring onion, finely sliced30g
  • Sweet corn kernels40g
  • Coconut oil (for pan)20ml

Method

  1. Whisk together base batter: rice flour, glutinous rice flour, coconut milk, water, sugar, and salt until smooth. Rest 30 minutes to hydrate flour.
  2. Mix topping batter: coconut cream, rice flour, sugar, and salt. Whisk until lump-free. This should be slightly thicker than the base batter.
  3. Heat khanom krok pan (or æbleskiver pan) over medium heat. Brush each cavity well with coconut oil — it should sizzle on contact.
  4. Fill each cavity two-thirds with base batter. Cover with lid and cook 2 minutes until edges set and base is slightly golden.
  5. Drop 3–4 kernels of sweet corn and pinch of spring onion into each pancake.
  6. Spoon 1 tablespoon topping batter over each to fill cavity. Replace lid, cook 2–3 minutes more until topping is just set — it should remain slightly wobbly in the centre.
  7. Remove with a thin offset spatula or chopstick, joining two halves together. Serve immediately — they become chewy as they cool.
Chef Technique Note

The two-batter system creates the textural contrast: crispy rice base from the high-heat direct contact, and a soft, barely-set coconut custard top. The salty-sweet topping against the neutral base is quintessentially Thai — salted coconut cream is a flavour principle across all khanom.

Fail-Proof Tip

Pan temperature is everything. Too cool and the base spreads before setting — you'll get flat pancakes. Too hot and the base burns before the top sets. Test with a drop of batter: it should sizzle gently and form a disc within 5 seconds. Adjust heat between batches as the pan stabilises.

Plating Note

Serve six pairs on a banana leaf-lined plate or wooden board. The golden-and-white contrasting halves should face upward. A scattered pinch of fresh spring onion and a tiny fresh chilli for colour (without heat) finishes the display. Eat immediately — these do not hold.

Sourcing A traditional khanom krok pan is essential — the curved cavities create the domed shape. Available from Asian cookware suppliers online (approx $25 AUD). No adequate substitute exists; an æbleskiver pan is the closest Western equivalent.

Lod Chong
Pandan Jelly Noodles — pandan-green rice noodles, cold coconut milk, palm sugar syrup, crushed ice · Serves 2
$2.60
Food Cost
GF VG DF NF

Ingredients

  • Rice flour100g
  • Tapioca flour30g
  • Pandan juice (fresh)250ml
  • Salt2g
  • Palm sugar120g
  • Water (for syrup)100ml
  • Pandan leaf (for syrup)2 leaves
  • Coconut milk400ml
  • Fine salt (coconut milk)3g
  • Crushed ice200g

Method

  1. Blend 12 pandan leaves with 300ml water, strain through fine sieve to yield 250ml deep green pandan juice.
  2. Whisk rice flour, tapioca flour, and salt with pandan juice until smooth. Pour into saucepan and stir constantly over medium heat until batter thickens into a smooth, pulling dough, approximately 8–10 minutes.
  3. While dough is hot, press immediately through a lod chong mould (colander with large holes, or piping bag fitted with 5mm round tip) held over a bowl of cold water. The noodles will set immediately on contact with cold water.
  4. Rinse noodles gently in cold water, drain, and refrigerate until needed.
  5. Make palm sugar syrup: combine palm sugar, water, and pandan leaves in saucepan. Stir over medium heat until sugar dissolves. Simmer 3 minutes until syrup coats the back of a spoon. Strain and cool.
  6. Season coconut milk with salt, chill completely.
  7. To serve: fill deep bowl with crushed ice. Pile generous nest of pandan noodles on top. Pour cold coconut milk around. Drizzle palm sugar syrup generously over noodles. Serve immediately.
Chef Technique Note

The dough must be extruded while still hot — as it cools it becomes too stiff to press. Work quickly with confidence. The combination of rice flour (structure) and tapioca flour (elasticity) gives the noodle its characteristic bounce and translucency. All rice flour produces a brittle noodle; tapioca alone, too gummy.

Fail-Proof Tip

Keep a second person ready to catch the noodles in cold water while you extrude. The window between workable and too stiff is only 2–3 minutes. If dough stiffens, return to heat briefly with a tablespoon of water. Pre-measure all components before cooking the dough.

Plating Note

Green noodles against white coconut milk on crushed ice is a visual centrepiece. Use a deep glass bowl for full visibility. Drizzle dark palm sugar syrup last in a figure-eight motion. The contrast of three layers — ice, noodles, coconut — should be unmistakeable from above.

Sourcing Fresh pandan leaves are essential — extract gives a murky colour and chemical flavour. The dough can be made up to 8 hours ahead and stored in cold water in the fridge. Palm sugar from Thai grocers gives a distinct caramel-treacle flavour unavailable from regular brown sugar.

Sangkaya Fak Thong
Thai Pumpkin Custard — steamed pandan coconut custard inside a whole Japanese pumpkin, served sliced · Serves 4
$3.80
Food Cost
GF NF

Ingredients

  • Japanese kabocha pumpkin (small)1 whole (~800g)
  • Coconut cream200ml
  • Eggs (whole)4 large (200g)
  • Palm sugar, shaved100g
  • Pandan leaf juice (fresh)30ml
  • Fine sea salt3g
  • Coconut cream (sauce)80ml
  • Pandan leaf (sauce)2 leaves
  • Palm sugar (sauce)20g
  • Salt (sauce)2g

Method

  1. Cut lid from top of pumpkin (approx 6cm diameter). Scoop out seeds and stringy membrane thoroughly, leaving clean cavity. Rinse and pat dry inside.
  2. Gently melt palm sugar with coconut cream over low heat until fully dissolved. Do not boil. Cool to room temperature (warm custard scrambles eggs).
  3. Whisk eggs gently — avoid creating foam. Strain through fine sieve. Whisk in cooled coconut-palm sugar mixture, pandan juice, and salt until uniform.
  4. Pour custard mixture into pumpkin cavity, filling to within 1cm of the top. Replace lid.
  5. Place pumpkin in bamboo steamer over rapidly boiling water. Steam on medium heat (not maximum) for 45–55 minutes until a skewer inserted in custard comes out clean and pumpkin flesh yields when pressed.
  6. Cool completely at room temperature — minimum 1 hour — before refrigerating. Serve chilled or at room temperature.
  7. To serve: cut into wedges, revealing the golden custard inside the green pumpkin. Drizzle with pandan coconut cream sauce alongside.
Chef Technique Note

The pumpkin walls continue to cook and compress after steaming, firming the custard as it cools. This is why cooling completely before cutting is essential — slicing too early produces a runny custard that falls from the pumpkin. The pandan juice (not extract) gives the custard a subtle green tint and true fragrance.

Fail-Proof Tip

If your custard is grainy or watery, the coconut cream was too hot when eggs were added — next time, cool to below 40°C before combining. Steam at moderate heat: violent boiling creates steam pressure that puffs the custard, producing an uneven, holey texture. A slow, steady steam produces silk.

Plating Note

A cross-section wedge is the reveal — the bright yellow custard against dark green kabocha skin is theatrical. Lay the wedge on its side at an angle on a dark plate. Pool the pandan coconut cream sauce beside it. Garnish with micro pandan leaf cuts or edible flower for colour contrast.

Sourcing Japanese kabocha (Kent pumpkin) from Asian grocers or any major supermarket. Its dense, sweet flesh and dark green skin create the signature presentation. Regular pumpkin can substitute but lacks the dramatic colour contrast. Sangkaya can be steamed 2 days ahead; refrigerates beautifully.

Mango Coconut Parfait
Layers of mango sorbet, whipped coconut cream, sticky rice crumble, fresh mango · Serves 2
$5.60
Food Cost
GF VG DF

Ingredients

  • Fresh Nam Dok Mai mango puree300g
  • Caster sugar60g
  • Lime juice, fresh15ml
  • Coconut cream (full-fat, chilled)200ml
  • Icing sugar20g
  • Vanilla extract2ml
  • Cooked sticky rice (cold)80g
  • Coconut oil15ml
  • Caster sugar (crumble)20g
  • Sesame seeds, toasted10g
  • Fresh mango, diced 1cm100g
  • Micro mint or Thai basil6 leaves

Method

  1. Make sorbet: blend mango puree with caster sugar and lime juice until sugar dissolves. Churn in ice cream machine until smooth and frozen, or freeze flat in tray, breaking up every 30 minutes over 3 hours.
  2. Make sticky rice crumble: spread cold cooked sticky rice on baking paper. Toss with coconut oil and sugar. Bake at 160°C for 25–30 minutes, stirring every 10 minutes, until golden and crisp. Toss with sesame seeds. Cool completely — it crisps further as it cools.
  3. Whip chilled coconut cream with icing sugar and vanilla to soft peaks. Do not over-whip. Keep refrigerated until assembly.
  4. To assemble: in a chilled 200ml glass, layer — sticky rice crumble, mango sorbet quenelle, whipped coconut cream, diced fresh mango.
  5. Repeat layering once more. Top with final scoop of sorbet, a cloud of coconut cream, and micro herbs.
  6. Scatter remaining crumble for texture contrast. Serve immediately.
Chef Technique Note

The sticky rice crumble is the textural anchor — it provides the crunch that counterpoints the sorbet and cream. Baking rather than frying extracts moisture evenly and gives a more consistent crisp. The slight saltiness of coconut cream against sweet mango and sorbet creates the Thai flavour signature in a Western format.

Fail-Proof Tip

Coconut cream whips best when the tin has been refrigerated overnight — the cream separates to the top and the watery liquid can be poured off. Only whip the solid cream. If it won't hold peaks, return to fridge for 20 minutes. Sorbet softens fast — assemble plates just before service.

Plating Note

Use tall, clear glasses to reveal all layers to the guest — the visual of green-white-gold stacked layers is as compelling as the taste. Keep layers distinct; avoid muddling. A quenelle of sorbet on top adds elegance. Serve on a small side plate with a long spoon.

Sourcing Full-fat coconut cream in tins (Aroy-D or Chaokoh brand) reliably separates when chilled. Light coconut cream will not whip. Mango sorbet can be prepped 5 days ahead and kept frozen in a covered container.

Pandan Crème Brûlée
Pandan-infused custard, caramelised palm sugar crust — a Thai riff on the French classic · Serves 4
$3.40
Food Cost
GF NF

Ingredients

  • Full-fat cream (35% fat)400ml
  • Coconut cream100ml
  • Pandan leaves, fresh8 leaves
  • Egg yolks6 (120g)
  • Caster sugar80g
  • Fine sea salt2g
  • Palm sugar, finely grated60g
  • Caster sugar (for brûlée)40g
  • Fresh mango, micro-diced40g
  • Pandan leaf strips (garnish)4 strips

Method

  1. Combine cream, coconut cream, and pandan leaves in saucepan. Heat to 75°C (just below simmer). Remove from heat, cover, and infuse 30 minutes. Strain through fine sieve, pressing leaves to extract all green fragrance.
  2. Whisk egg yolks, caster sugar, and salt until pale and ribbon-stage. Do not create foam.
  3. Slowly pour warm pandan cream into yolk mixture, whisking constantly. Strain through fine sieve again. Skim surface foam.
  4. Divide custard between 4 brûlée ramekins (120ml each). Place in deep baking tray. Pour boiling water around ramekins to come halfway up sides.
  5. Bake at 150°C for 28–32 minutes until set at edges but still has a slight wobble in the centre (like set jelly when nudged). Remove from water bath, cool to room temperature, refrigerate minimum 4 hours or overnight.
  6. Before service: mix palm sugar with caster sugar 50/50. Sprinkle 2 teaspoons evenly over each custard. Blowtorch in circular motion until fully caramelised with amber patches. Return to fridge 2 minutes to reset custard before serving.
  7. Garnish with micro-diced mango and pandan strip. Serve immediately after brûlée sets.
Chef Technique Note

Pandan infusion in fat captures the aromatic compounds far more effectively than water infusion. The combination of cream and coconut cream gives a richer, more complex custard than either alone. Palm sugar brulee creates a more complex, slightly smoky caramel with deeper flavour than pure caster sugar — the treacly notes complement pandan perfectly.

Fail-Proof Tip

The wobble test: gently nudge the ramekin — the edge should be set and the centre should wobble as a whole mass, not ripple like liquid. If it ripples, return to oven 5 more minutes. Over-baked brûlée is grainy. Under-baked brûlée weeps. When in doubt, slightly under is better — it firms in the fridge.

Plating Note

The brûlée is the presentation — serve in its ramekin on a small round plate lined with a folded napkin to stop sliding. The moment the guest taps through the cracking caramel is the payoff. Position the small dice of mango to one side of the crust, pandan strip diagonally across the top.

Sourcing Fresh pandan leaves are critical for colour and flavour. Dried or powdered pandan gives inferior green and a coarser fragrance. Custard can be prepared and baked 3 days ahead. Do not brûlée until service.

Thai Milk Tea Cheesecake
Thai tea-infused cream cheese, condensed milk, buttery graham cracker base, tea jelly top · Serves 8 slices
$3.90
Food Cost
NF

Ingredients

  • Graham crackers / digestives180g
  • Unsalted butter, melted80g
  • Caster sugar (base)15g
  • Thai tea leaves (Cha Yen)40g
  • Full cream milk200ml
  • Cream cheese, softened500g
  • Sweetened condensed milk150g
  • Caster sugar60g
  • Eggs (whole)3 (150g)
  • Sour cream100g
  • Vanilla extract5ml
  • Thai tea (for jelly)150ml (brewed strong)
  • Gelatine sheets3 sheets (6g)
  • Evaporated milk (jelly)50ml

Method

  1. Brew Thai tea: steep tea leaves in 200ml hot milk (not boiling) for 8 minutes. Strain thoroughly through fine sieve lined with muslin. Press leaves to extract all orange-amber liquid. Cool.
  2. Blitz crackers to fine crumbs. Mix with melted butter and sugar. Press firmly into base of lined 20cm springform tin. Refrigerate 30 minutes.
  3. Beat cream cheese until completely smooth. Add condensed milk, sugar, and cooled Thai tea milk. Mix until uniform orange colour with no streaks.
  4. Add eggs one at a time, mixing on low speed. Fold in sour cream and vanilla. Do not over-mix after eggs are added.
  5. Pour filling over base. Bake in water bath at 150°C for 55–65 minutes until edges set and centre has a gentle wobble. Turn off oven, leave door ajar, cool in oven 1 hour. Refrigerate overnight.
  6. Make tea jelly: bloom gelatine in cold water 5 minutes. Heat 150ml strong brewed Thai tea with 50ml evaporated milk to 60°C, dissolve gelatine in liquid. Cool to room temperature. Pour over set cheesecake. Refrigerate 2 hours until jelly sets.
  7. Remove from tin, slice with hot knife. Dust top with pinch of ground Thai tea.
Chef Technique Note

Thai tea (Cha Yen) is steeped in milk rather than water to extract fat-soluble aromatic compounds that give the tea its characteristic orange colour and spiced depth. The condensed milk reduces the added sugar needed while amplifying the dessert's signature sweetness profile — it's how Thai iced tea is made, so this preserves authenticity in the filling.

Fail-Proof Tip

Never mix cheesecake at high speed after adding eggs — it incorporates too much air, which expands in the oven and causes cracking on cooling. Low and slow from egg addition onward. The gradual oven cool-down (heat off, door ajar) prevents the sudden temperature drop that causes the centre to sink.

Plating Note

The tea jelly top gives a glossy, amber mirror finish — the slice reveal should show the distinct jelly layer over the orange-tan filling and golden base. Clean knife cuts (wipe between each cut) are essential. Serve on a small dessert plate with a thin drizzle of condensed milk and micro Thai tea leaf crumbles.

Sourcing Thai Cha Yen tea mix (often contains star anise, tamarind, food colouring) from Asian grocers. The distinctive orange colour comes from the dye in traditional Thai tea blend — use authentic product for the correct colour. Cheesecake keeps 5 days refrigerated.

Black Sesame Mochi Ice Cream
Glutinous rice shell, black sesame paste centre, coconut ice cream filling · Serves 6 pieces
$4.20
Food Cost
GF VG DF

Ingredients

  • Glutinous rice flour150g
  • Caster sugar30g
  • Water150ml
  • Coconut milk50ml
  • Black sesame paste80g
  • Palm sugar30g
  • Coconut cream30ml
  • Coconut ice cream300ml
  • Potato starch (dusting)40g
  • Toasted black sesame seeds15g
  • Gold leaf (optional garnish)2 pieces

Method

  1. Mix black sesame paste, palm sugar, and coconut cream in small saucepan over low heat until combined and smooth. Spread thin on tray, freeze 1 hour into firm discs (approx 15g each). Freeze solid.

  5. Top iced version with 2 tablespoons of thick coconut cream for a floating cream cap.

  • Lemongrass stalks, bruised4 stalks

  • Fresh ginger, sliced40g
  • Water800ml
  • Palm sugar60g
  • Fine salt2g
  • Lime juice20ml
  • Ice and lemongrass stalk (garnish)per serve

Method

  1. Bruise lemongrass by bashing with the back of a knife — this releases the aromatic oils. Knot pandan leaves. Slice ginger thinly.

Key Thai Pantry Ingredients

Pandan (Bai Toey): The vanilla of South-East Asia. Fresh leaves only for colour; extract for back-of-house prep.

Palm Sugar (Nam Tan Peep): Made from coconut palm or date palm sap. Darker = deeper flavour. Unrefined blocks from Thai grocers.

Glutinous Rice (Khao Niao): Short-grain sticky rice. Must be soaked minimum 4 hours. Do not substitute regular rice.

Tapioca Flour: Extracted from cassava root. Gives bounce, translucency, and chew. Non-negotiable in many recipes.

Butterfly Pea Flower (Dok Anchan): Dried flowers as natural blue-purple dye and antioxidant. pH-reactive.

The Philosophy of Thai Desserts

Thai desserts are built on a flavour philosophy quite different from Western pastry. Where French patisserie prizes precision, balance, and subtlety — Thai khanom embraces exuberance: vivid colour from natural sources, pronounced sweetness tempered always by salt, fragrance that leads before flavour, and texture contrasts that keep the palate engaged from first bite to last.

The salt in Thai desserts is not a mistake — it is the tradition. Coconut cream is salted because the Thai palate understands that fat and salt together amplify sweetness. The coconut sauce on Mango Sticky Rice should taste almost savoury alone; with mango, it becomes perfect. This is the principle of saneh — the charm or spell that a great Thai dessert casts. It is elusive but unmistakeable.

These 28 recipes are an invitation to understand that philosophy through practice. Make each dish at least twice — the first time to understand the technique, the second time to own it. Thai desserts reward patience and punish shortcuts. They also reward generosity: make extra, share widely, and accept the compliments with grace.

— Nattaya Srisomwong
