From the Chef

A Letter on Craft

"Patisserie is not baking. Baking feeds the body. Patisserie — true patisserie — feeds something deeper. It asks you to see, to feel, to remember."

I came to Sydney with flour-dusted hands and a head full of Paris. My years under a two-Michelin-star chef in the 7th arrondissement taught me that perfection lives in the gap between temperature and timing — that a ganache can break in ten seconds of inattention, that a proper laminated dough requires you to understand cold as a living force, not just a number on a dial.

But Sydney changed me. The strawberries here in December have a sweetness that no Parisian market can rival. The watermelons from the Hunter Valley are so full of juice they seem to breathe. Passionfruit climbs over suburban fences and perfumes entire streets. When I first encountered the casualness with which Sydneysiders treat their extraordinary produce, I felt something shift inside me — the rigidity of classical technique softened just enough to let the Southern Hemisphere in.

This guide is the result of that collision. Twenty-eight recipes that speak both languages — the precise, exacting grammar of French patisserie and the warm, generous vocabulary of Australian ingredients and culture. You will find the architecture of a Paris-Brest sitting comfortably beside an elevated Lamington Entremet. A St Honoré in which the caramel carries a faint memory of Brittany, assembled in a kitchen that looks out over the Pacific.

Each recipe in these pages was developed with the same philosophy I absorbed at the Pass: technique is only the beginning. The goal is always the moment a guest takes a first bite and goes quiet. That silence — that tiny arrest of breath — is what every pastry chef is truly working toward.

Some notes on using this guide: ingredient weights are given in grams because volume measurements lie. Oven temperatures assume a deck oven or a well-calibrated fan-forced domestic model — always verify with a thermometer. Where I note an allergen alternative (NF sunflower praline, for example), it is not a compromise; it is simply a different path to the same destination.

Read the chef notes. They contain the things I had to learn the hard way — the things no recipe text ever tells you because they are assumed knowledge in a professional kitchen. In sharing them here, I am trying to close that gap.

Cook with patience. Taste constantly. And if something goes wrong — and it will, because that is patisserie — do not panic. Understand. Then try again.

Chef Isabelle Moreau
Trained Paris (2-Star Michelin) · Head Patissier, Sydney · Guide #41 Author

Recipe No. 01

Strawberry Watermelon Cake

Rose water cream — almond dacquoise — Australian summer fruit
$14.50 AUD / serve
GF
Ingredients
  • Seedless watermelon (chilled, rind removed)1.2 kg
  • Fresh strawberries (hulled, halved)600 g
  • Thickened cream (35% fat)600 ml
  • Crème fraîche120 g
  • Icing sugar (sifted)80 g
  • Rose water8 ml
  • Vanilla bean paste5 g
  • Freeze-dried strawberry powder15 g
  • Micro herbs or edible rose petals (garnish)as needed
  • Pistachio dust (optional, for colour)10 g
Method
  1. Using a long serrated knife, slice watermelon into three even discs approximately 4 cm thick, trimming to an even round (20 cm diameter). Pat dry with paper towel and refrigerate on a wire rack for 30 minutes to remove surface moisture.
  2. Whip thickened cream to soft peaks in a chilled bowl. Add crème fraîche, icing sugar, rose water, and vanilla bean paste. Continue whipping to firm, spoonable peaks. Do not over-whip.
  3. Place the first watermelon disc on a chilled serving board. Pipe or spread a generous 1.5 cm layer of rose cream, smoothing with an offset spatula.
  4. Arrange halved strawberries in a single layer over the cream, cut-side down, pressing gently to adhere.
  5. Add second watermelon disc. Repeat cream and strawberry layers. Top with final watermelon disc.
  6. Apply a thin crumb coat of cream around the exterior. Refrigerate 20 minutes to set.
  7. Apply a final generous exterior coat of cream. Use a bench scraper to create a clean or textured finish.
  8. Dust top generously with freeze-dried strawberry powder. Arrange whole or halved strawberries and edible rose petals over the top. Finish with pistachio dust if using.
  9. Refrigerate assembled cake for at least 1 hour before service. Slice with a hot, dry knife — wiping clean between cuts.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The watermelon must be genuinely cold and as dry as possible — moisture is the enemy of cream stability. In my Paris years, we would never use a fruit with 92% water content as a structural layer. Sydney taught me that if you respect the cold chain and work fast, watermelon becomes one of the most visually arresting base elements in modern patisserie. The rose water should whisper, not shout — 8 ml per 600 ml cream is the precise ratio where the floral note elevates rather than dominates.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If your cream begins to weep during service on a warm day, fold in 1 tsp of cornflour per 300 ml before whipping. Chill all equipment — bowl, whisk, spatulas — in the freezer for 10 minutes. The cake must never leave refrigeration for more than 15 minutes at a stretch. Assemble cold, serve cold, cut cold.
🎨 Plating Note
At a celebrated Sydney patisserie, this style of cake is presented whole at the pass so guests can witness the moment of slicing — the theatrical reveal of pink watermelon flesh against white cream. For plated service, cut a generous wedge and lay on its side to show the layered cross-section. Dust the plate with additional strawberry powder. A single fresh rose petal placed at 11 o'clock completes the composition. Serve within 10 minutes of plating.
Recipe No. 02

Matcha Opera Cake

Almond joconde — ceremonial matcha buttercream — 70% dark chocolate ganache
$12.80 AUD / serve
Ingredients — Almond Joconde (3 sheets)
  • Almond meal (blanched)225 g
  • Icing sugar225 g
  • Whole eggs270 g (5 eggs)
  • Egg whites200 g (6 whites)
  • Caster sugar30 g
  • Plain flour60 g
  • Unsalted butter (melted, 50°C)45 g
  • Ceremonial matcha (for buttercream)18 g
  • Unsalted butter (room temp, buttercream)280 g
  • Italian meringue base (sugar + whites)180 g
  • Valrhona 70% dark chocolate (ganache)180 g
  • Thickened cream (ganache)160 ml
  • Glucose syrup (ganache)25 g
  • Strong espresso or coffee syrup (soak)120 ml
  • Gold leaf or matcha powder (finish)as needed
Method
  1. Preheat oven to 220°C fan. Whisk almond meal, icing sugar, and whole eggs together until pale and tripled in volume — approximately 8 minutes on high speed.
  2. Separately, whip egg whites with caster sugar to a firm, glossy meringue. Fold meringue into the almond mixture in two additions using a large silicone spatula.
  3. Sift flour over and fold through. Stream in melted butter and fold to combine. Spread thinly (4–5 mm) on three lined 30×40 cm trays. Bake 6–8 minutes per sheet until just golden. Cool completely on wire racks.
  4. Make Italian meringue: boil sugar and water to 121°C; stream onto whipping whites. Cool to 35°C. Beat softened butter into cooled meringue until smooth. Sift matcha into buttercream and beat thoroughly. Rest 15 minutes.
  5. Make ganache: bring cream and glucose to a simmer. Pour over chopped chocolate in three additions, emulsifying with a spatula from the centre outward. Rest at room temperature until spreadable (26–28°C).
  6. Assembly: brush first joconde sheet with espresso soak. Spread a 5 mm layer of matcha buttercream. Add second joconde sheet; soak; spread ganache layer. Add third sheet; soak; spread remaining buttercream. Refrigerate 30 minutes.
  7. Pour remaining ganache over top in a smooth, even layer. Refrigerate until set (1 hour).
  8. Trim edges with a hot knife to reveal perfect layers. Mark portion lines with a warm ruler. Garnish each portion with a touch of gold leaf and a dusting of sifted matcha.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The opera cake is one of the most technically demanding of the French classics — its beauty is entirely in the precision of layering and the temperature control of each element. The joconde must be baked thin enough to absorb the soak while retaining structure. Using ceremonial-grade matcha rather than culinary-grade is non-negotiable — the flavour depth is categorically different, and the vivid green colour eliminates the need for artificial colouring. The ganache pour temperature is critical: too hot and it melts the buttercream; too cold and it pulls.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If your buttercream curdles (looks like scrambled eggs), it has temperature-shocked. Simply warm the bowl gently over hot water while beating — it will come back together. Always have all components at consistent temperatures before assembly. Chill the assembled cake overnight before cutting for cleanest layers.
🎨 Plating Note
Serve as a precisely cut 8×4 cm rectangle, positioned on the plate at a slight diagonal for dynamism. A quenelle of unsweetened crème fraîche alongside tempers the richness. Dust a fine line of matcha powder across the plate using a stencil. The cake should have visible, distinct alternating layers of green and dark brown — this cross-section IS the plating statement.
Recipe No. 03

Raspberry Lychee Tart

Crème pâtissière — fresh Queensland lychee — raspberry mirror glaze
$11.20 AUD / serve
Ingredients
  • Plain flour (pâte sucrée)250 g
  • Unsalted butter (cold, cubed)125 g
  • Icing sugar (pâte sucrée)90 g
  • Egg yolks2
  • Ice water20 ml
  • Full-cream milk (crème pât)500 ml
  • Egg yolks (crème pât)5
  • Caster sugar (crème pât)120 g
  • Cornflour40 g
  • Vanilla bean (split)1
  • Unsalted butter (finish, crème pât)30 g
  • Fresh lychees (peeled, pitted)300 g
  • Fresh raspberries200 g
  • Raspberry coulis (mirror glaze base)150 ml
  • Caster sugar (glaze)60 g
  • Pectin NH5 g
Method
  1. Make pâte sucrée: rub cold butter into flour until sandy. Mix in icing sugar. Add yolks and ice water; bring dough together without overworking. Wrap and rest in refrigerator for 2 hours minimum.
  2. Roll to 3 mm on a lightly floured surface. Line individual tart rings (8 cm). Freeze 20 minutes. Blind bake at 170°C with baking weights for 15 min; remove weights, bake further 8 min until pale gold. Cool completely.
  3. Make crème pâtissière: bring milk and split vanilla bean to a near-simmer. Whisk yolks, sugar, and cornflour until pale. Stream hot milk onto yolk mixture while whisking. Return to pot; cook over medium heat, whisking constantly until thick and boiling. Cook a further 60 seconds. Remove vanilla. Beat in cold butter. Press cling wrap to surface; cool to room temperature, then refrigerate until cold.
  4. Beat chilled crème pâtissière until smooth. Pipe into tart shells, filling to 5 mm from rim. Smooth with palette knife. Refrigerate 30 minutes.
  5. Arrange halved fresh lychees and whole raspberries over the cream in a tightly packed, decorative pattern.
  6. Make raspberry glaze: blend coulis with mixed sugar and pectin; bring to boil, stirring. Cool to 60°C. Brush or spoon gently over fruit to coat and seal.
  7. Refrigerate 20 minutes before service. Remove tart rings at the last moment.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The combination of lychee and raspberry is one of the great pairings — both have high esters, both carry a rose-like aromatic, and they reinforce each other magnificently. The key to a great crème pâtissière is patience at the final cooking stage: once it boils, you must cook for a full 60 seconds to eliminate the raw starch flavour. Cutting this short is the most common error in the home kitchen. The pectin NH glaze sets at room temperature but melts on the palate — never use gelatin-based glaze on fresh fruit as it creates an unpleasant rubbery sensation.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If your tart shell shrinks during baking, your dough was overworked or not cold enough when it went into the oven. Always dock the base with a fork before blind baking. If crème pâtissière develops lumps during cooking, strain immediately through a fine-mesh sieve while still hot, then continue to cool — it will be perfectly smooth.
🎨 Plating Note
Present the tart slightly elevated on a small round pedestal or thick cardboard round on a white plate. The concentric arrangement of lychee and raspberry should look almost architectural — guests should see the geometry before they see the colour. A few drops of raspberry coulis dragged across the plate with a spoon, and three fresh raspberry halves alongside, completes the composition without overpowering the tart's own visual statement.
Recipe No. 04

Burnt Basque Cheesecake

Extra creamy molten centre — deeply caramelised top — gluten-free
$8.50 AUD / serve
GF
Ingredients (serves 12 — 20 cm cake)
  • Full-fat cream cheese (room temp)900 g
  • Caster sugar280 g
  • Whole eggs5
  • Egg yolks (extra richness)2
  • Thickened cream (35%)400 ml
  • Cornflour (sifted)20 g
  • Vanilla bean paste8 g
  • Sea salt flakes3 g
  • Baking paper (for lining)2 sheets
  • Seasonal berry coulis (service)optional
Method
  1. Preheat oven to 230°C fan (or 240°C conventional). Grease a 20 cm springform pan. Line with two overlapping sheets of baking paper, allowing 5 cm to rise above the rim — do not trim; the rustic paper pleating is intentional.
  2. Beat cream cheese and caster sugar on medium speed until completely smooth, approximately 4 minutes. Scrape bowl thoroughly.
  3. Add eggs and yolks one at a time, beating well between each addition. The batter should be very smooth and slightly glossy.
  4. Add thickened cream, cornflour, vanilla bean paste, and sea salt. Beat on low until just incorporated — do not over-aerate at this stage.
  5. Pass batter through a fine-mesh strainer into the prepared tin. This removes any lumps and ensures a silky texture.
  6. Bake at 230°C for 28–32 minutes. The top should be deeply, dramatically dark — almost charred in appearance. The centre should still jiggle like set jelly when shaken.
  7. Remove from oven. Do not touch. Cool in tin at room temperature for 2 hours, then refrigerate overnight. The centre will firm from liquid to creamy as it cools.
  8. Remove from tin while still cold. Bring to room temperature (30 minutes) before service for optimal texture.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The Basque cheesecake inverts every rule of classical cheesecake technique: we want it dark, we want it to jiggle, we want the centre barely set. The Maillard reaction and caramelisation on the top surface create a bittersweet crust that counterpoints the rich, lactic interior. The overnight refrigeration is non-negotiable — the egg proteins need time to set from the outside in, creating the characteristic gradient from firm exterior to molten core. At room temperature, the cream cheese fat melts back to velvet. Never serve this cold.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If the top is browning too fast before the sides are set, the oven runs hot — drop 10°C next time and extend by 5 minutes. If the centre stays liquid even after overnight refrigeration, the eggs were beaten too fast (air was incorporated), preventing clean setting. Beat cream cheese and eggs slowly after the initial sugar cream stage. Every oven bakes this differently — keep notes on time and temperature for your specific oven.
🎨 Plating Note
Serve at room temperature as a thick wedge, allowing the creamy gradient to be visible at the cut edge. No garnish is needed — the contrast of caramelised black-to-amber top against ivory interior IS the dish. A very small puddle of seasonal berry coulis (passionfruit, raspberry, or mango) at the base of the wedge adds colour without competing. A few sea salt flakes on the top surface just before service elevates the caramel notes dramatically.
Recipe No. 05

Passionfruit Mille-Feuille

Rough puff pastry — crème diplomat — passionfruit curd
$10.20 AUD / serve
Ingredients
  • Plain flour (rough puff)300 g
  • Unsalted butter (very cold, diced)240 g
  • Ice water120 ml
  • Fine salt5 g
  • Caster sugar (for caramelising)40 g
  • Full-cream milk (crème pât base)350 ml
  • Egg yolks4
  • Caster sugar (crème pât)85 g
  • Cornflour28 g
  • Gelatine leaves (diplomat fold)2
  • Thickened cream (whipped, diplomat)250 ml
  • Passionfruit pulp (fresh, strained)120 ml
  • Caster sugar (curd)80 g
  • Whole eggs (curd)2
  • Unsalted butter (curd)60 g
  • Icing sugar (finish)30 g
Method
  1. Make rough puff: mix flour and salt. Cut cold butter into rough 1 cm cubes — do not rub. Add ice water and mix until dough just comes together with visible butter pieces. Turn out; roll into a rough rectangle. Fold in thirds (letter fold). Repeat 4 more times, resting 15 min in the refrigerator between each fold. Rest 1 hour before rolling.
  2. Roll rough puff to 3 mm on a lightly floured surface. Cut three rectangles per portion (10×4 cm). Dock all over with a fork. Lay between two lined baking trays (to prevent rising). Bake at 200°C for 18–20 minutes until deep gold. Remove top tray; dust with caster sugar and grill or blast with blowtorch to caramelise. Cool completely on wire rack.
  3. Make passionfruit curd: whisk eggs, sugar, and strained passionfruit juice in a heavy-bottomed saucepan. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until mixture coats the back of a spoon (82°C). Remove from heat; whisk in cold butter in pieces. Strain; cool with cling wrap touching the surface.
  4. Make crème diplomat: prepare crème pâtissière (as per Recipe 03 method). Bloom gelatine in cold water; squeeze out and dissolve in warm crème pât. Cool to room temperature. Fold whipped cream into cooled crème pât in two additions. Refrigerate 30 minutes.
  5. Assembly: pipe crème diplomat onto first pastry layer in neat rosettes or a smooth layer. Drizzle with passionfruit curd. Add second pastry layer; repeat cream and curd. Place final pastry layer on top, flat-side up.
  6. Dust top heavily with sifted icing sugar. For the classic burnt-line finish, heat a metal skewer and brand a grid pattern across the sugar surface. Serve within 20 minutes of assembly.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The mille-feuille is an act of theatre that succeeds only when every texture is alive at the moment of service — the pastry must shatter, the cream must be cold and light, the curd must provide an acidic counterpoint to the fat. This is why mille-feuille is assembled to order or at most 20 minutes before service. The passionfruit curd elevates the classic vanilla version into something unmistakably Australian — the tropical acidity cuts through the crème diplomat in a way that nothing else achieves. Booking the pastry layers the day before and storing in an airtight container is the professional way to manage service pressure.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If rough puff is not puffing evenly, your butter was too warm — the steam that drives the lamination escapes before structure sets. Chill all components aggressively. If the assembled mille-feuille collapses when cut, the cream is too soft — refrigerate the assembled pastry for 10 minutes before cutting. Always use a serrated knife with a very gentle sawing motion, never a pushing cut.
🎨 Plating Note
Stand the mille-feuille upright on the plate — the stack is the visual. A generous streak of passionfruit curd across the plate from one corner, three dots of cream at varying sizes, and a halved fresh passionfruit pod placed beside the pastry. Dust a final whisper of icing sugar over the entire composition just before it leaves the kitchen. The sound of the fork breaking through the caramelised pastry layers is part of the dining experience — do not let it sit and soften.
Recipe No. 06

Croissant aux Amandes

Twice-baked laminated dough — frangipane — flaked almond — icing sugar
$6.80 AUD / serve
Ingredients
  • Day-old house croissants6 pieces
  • Unsalted butter (frangipane)150 g
  • Caster sugar (frangipane)150 g
  • Almond meal (blanched)150 g
  • Whole eggs2
  • Plain flour20 g
  • Dark rum or kirsch15 ml
  • Simple syrup (soak)80 ml
  • Almond extract (soak)3 ml
  • Flaked almonds80 g
  • Icing sugar (heavy dusting)30 g
  • Apricot jam (glaze, optional)40 g
Method
  1. Make frangipane: cream softened butter and sugar until very pale and fluffy (5 minutes). Add eggs one at a time; beat well. Fold in almond meal, flour, and rum. The mixture should be smooth and pipeable. Rest 30 minutes in refrigerator.
  2. Make almond soak: combine simple syrup with almond extract. Slice each day-old croissant in half horizontally — like opening a book.
  3. Brush the interior cut surfaces of both halves generously with almond soak. The croissant should feel moistened throughout but not soggy.
  4. Pipe or spoon a generous layer of frangipane (approximately 40 g) along the interior of the bottom half. Press the top half back on, gently compressing.
  5. Pipe or spread additional frangipane along the top exterior of the croissant, covering the entire surface.
  6. Scatter flaked almonds generously over the frangipane-topped surface, pressing lightly to adhere.
  7. Bake at 175°C fan for 14–16 minutes until the frangipane is fully cooked through (a skewer should come out clean) and almonds are golden brown. Watch closely after 12 minutes — the window between golden and burnt is narrow.
  8. Cool on wire rack for 10 minutes. Dust heavily with sifted icing sugar. Serve warm or at room temperature.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The croissant aux amandes is the most honest of all patisserie — it is literally built from yesterday's surplus, transformed by frangipane and a second baking into something better than the original. In Paris, every boulangerie does this. The magic is in the almond soak: the stale butter dough absorbs the moisture and becomes tender again, the frangipane melts into the layers during the second bake, and the top almonds toast to a deep nuttiness. Using a day-old croissant is not a shortcut — it is the correct technique. A fresh croissant is too soft and will not hold the frangipane cleanly.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If the frangipane puffs excessively and runs off the sides, it was too warm when applied. Chill it in the refrigerator until firm before loading the croissants. If the interior is still raw-tasting after 16 minutes, your oven temperature is too high (surface cooks before interior). Drop by 10°C and add 4 minutes. A perfectly cooked croissant aux amandes should feel substantially heavier than a plain croissant when you pick it up.
🎨 Plating Note
Place the warm croissant on a small wooden board or white plate at a slight angle. A snowdrift of icing sugar is the non-negotiable visual signal — guests expect the white powder. A small ramekin of room-temperature apricot jam alongside for dipping adds a classical note that many guests remember from childhood trips to French bakeries. No further garnish required. The beauty is in the simplicity.

Paris-Brest

Choux ring — praline mousseline — caramelised hazelnuts — NF: sunflower praline
$13.50 AUD / serve
NF opt
Ingredients
  • Water (choux)125 ml
  • Full-cream milk (choux)125 ml
  • Unsalted butter (choux)115 g
  • Plain flour (sifted, choux)140 g
  • Whole eggs4–5 (240 g)
  • Fine salt (choux)3 g
  • Caster sugar (choux)5 g
  • Hazelnuts (praline)200 g
  • Caster sugar (praline caramel)200 g
  • Full-cream milk (mousseline)500 ml
  • Egg yolks (mousseline)5
  • Caster sugar (mousseline)120 g
  • Cornflour40 g
  • Unsalted butter (mousseline, beaten in)240 g
  • Praline paste (beaten in)120 g
  • Flaked almonds (craquelin)40 g
  • Icing sugar (finish)20 g
Method
  1. Make praline: spread hazelnuts on a tray; roast at 180°C for 10 min until golden. Rub skins off in a cloth. Make a dry caramel with 200 g sugar until amber. Pour over hazelnuts on a silicone mat; cool. Blitz to a fine paste in a high-speed food processor until oil is released and paste is smooth (10–12 min). For NF version: use sunflower seeds roasted and caramelised in exactly the same way — the result is a slightly more delicate, nuttier flavour with no sacrifice in richness.
  2. Make mousseline base: follow crème pâtissière method (see Recipe 03). Once cooled to room temperature, beat in 240 g soft butter a tablespoon at a time until silky. Beat in praline paste. Refrigerate until firm (1 hour), then beat again until light and mousse-like.
  3. Make choux: bring water, milk, butter, salt, and sugar to a full boil. Add flour all at once; stir vigorously with a wooden spoon until paste pulls cleanly from sides. Cook, stirring, for 2 minutes to dry out. Transfer to stand mixer; beat on medium 2 minutes to cool. Add eggs one at a time — stop when paste falls in a slow, steady V-shape from the beater.
  4. Pipe a ring (8 cm diameter) on a lined tray using a 1.5 cm round nozzle. Pipe a second ring just inside the first, and a third ring on top of the join. Scatter flaked almonds over the top. Bake at 190°C for 28–32 minutes without opening the oven. Cool completely on a wire rack.
  5. Slice the ring in half horizontally. Pipe praline mousseline in generous rosettes on the bottom half, filling the space generously. Place the top half. Dust with icing sugar. Finish with a few caramelised whole hazelnuts.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The Paris-Brest was created in 1910 to celebrate a bicycle race — the wheel shape is deliberate. The praline mousseline is the most nuanced butter cream in the classical repertoire: rich yet airy, nutty yet delicate. The key is temperature — the crème pât must be room temperature and the butter must be soft before combining, or the mousseline will split. Homemade praline paste is infinitely superior to purchased — the fresh roasted hazelnut flavour is incomparable. The NF sunflower praline is a genuine discovery: it carries more toffee notes and pairs beautifully with the choux.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If your choux is hollow and dry, it was baked at too high a temperature — start at 190°C and never open the oven in the first 20 minutes or the steam escapes and the shells collapse. If the mousseline splits, both components were at different temperatures. Warm the bowl slightly and beat more aggressively. Always taste the praline paste — roasting depth directly determines flavour depth; under-roasted hazelnuts produce pale, bland praline.
🎨 Plating Note
The Paris-Brest should be served whole as an individual portion, positioned centrally on a white round plate. The cream filling should be visible and generous, spilling slightly beyond the choux ring — this signals abundance. Three caramelised hazelnuts placed asymmetrically on the cream surface, a dusting of icing sugar through a fine sieve. A small streak of praline paste on the plate itself, dragged with a spoon, connects the visual story from plate to pastry.
Recipe No. 08

Éclair au Chocolat

Choux shell — 70% Valrhona ganache — mirror glaze
$9.80 AUD / serve
Ingredients
  • Choux pastry (as per Recipe 07)1 batch
  • Valrhona Guanaja 70% dark chocolate200 g
  • Thickened cream (ganache)200 ml
  • Glucose syrup20 g
  • Unsalted butter (ganache, shine)25 g
  • Water (mirror glaze)75 ml
  • Caster sugar (glaze)150 g
  • Glucose syrup (glaze)150 g
  • Sweetened condensed milk100 g
  • Gelatine leaves (glaze)10 g (5 leaves)
  • Valrhona 70% chocolate (glaze)150 g
  • Gold lustre dust (optional finish)pinch
Method
  1. Pipe choux into 12 cm logs using a fluted 1.2 cm nozzle on a lined tray. Bake at 190°C for 30–35 minutes. Pierce each end with a skewer immediately upon removal from oven to allow steam to escape. Cool completely.
  2. Make ganache filling: bring cream and glucose to a bare simmer. Pour over chopped chocolate in three additions, emulsifying from centre. Add cold butter at the end; stir until completely melted. Cool to 28°C — it should be thick, glossy, and pipeable. Load into a piping bag with a small round nozzle.

💡 Chef Technique Note
The éclair is the examination piece of every pastry apprentice — it reveals everything about a chef's choux technique, ganache emulsification, and finishing precision. ✅ Fail-Proof Tip
Bubbles in mirror glaze are the enemy — always blend with the head fully submerged and strain through a fine sieve before resting to temperature. If the glaze sets before you finish, gently rewarm over a bain-marie (do not boil) and re-blend. Underfilled éclairs collapse when bitten — fill until you meet genuine resistance. A small scoop of vanilla bean ice cream between the éclairs bridges richness and temperature contrast. Three micro raspberries at the base for acidity and colour. The contrast between the bright yellow curd (visible at the sides) and the golden-tipped white meringue is the dish's identity — do not obscure it. Three very small pieces of preserved lemon peel or a few fresh lemon thyme sprigs placed at the base provide aromatic and visual framing. Afternoon Tea Selection

Three-tier stand — finger sandwiches — warm scones — petit fours — serves 2
$58.00 AUD / 2 pax
Components & Ingredients
  • White sandwich bread (finger sandwiches)8 slices
  • Smoked salmon + crème fraîche + dill80 g + 40 g
  • Free-range egg mayo + watercress2 eggs + handful
  • Cucumber + cream cheese + mint½ cucumber
  • Plain flour (scones)300 g
  • Baking powder (scones)12 g
  • Unsalted butter (cold, scones)75 g
  • Caster sugar (scones)30 g
  • Buttermilk160 ml
  • Egg wash1 egg
  • Clotted cream + house strawberry jam60 g each
  • Petit fours selection (4 types, 2 each)8 pieces
  • Seasonal fruit (garnish tier)as needed
Method
  1. Scones: rub cold butter into flour and baking powder until sandy. Add sugar, then buttermilk; bring together quickly without overworking. Turn onto a lightly floured surface; press to 3 cm thickness. Cut with a 5 cm cutter (push straight down — do not twist or sides won't rise evenly). Brush tops with egg wash. Bake 220°C for 12–14 minutes until risen and golden. Serve warm.
  2. Finger sandwiches: prepare fillings fresh on the day. Spread bread generously with appropriate bases (crème fraîche, cream cheese, egg mayo). Layer fillings; press lids on firmly. Trim crusts with a sharp serrated knife. Cut each sandwich into three fingers. Keep covered with a damp cloth until service.
  3. Petit fours selection (four types): (i) Chocolate-dipped strawberry with gold leaf; (ii) Lemon shortbread rounds with raspberry jam dot; (iii) Pistachio macarons (or NF equivalent); (iv) Chocolate truffle rolled in cocoa.
  4. Assembly: polish the three-tier stand. Bottom tier: finger sandwiches arranged in alternating lines by filling. Middle tier: warm scones with separate pots of clotted cream and strawberry jam. Top tier: petit fours and seasonal fruit arranged as a tableau. Finish with small edible flowers throughout all tiers for colour.
  5. Serve with chosen tea or coffee. Provide small dessert plates, butter knives, and cloth napkins. The stand should arrive at the table fully assembled — it is a presentation moment, not a DIY exercise.
💡 Chef Technique Note
Afternoon tea is choreography as much as cooking. Every element must be timed so the scones arrive warm, the sandwiches arrive fresh, and the petit fours are at room temperature. In our kitchen, scone timing is the anchor — everything else is prepared to the moment the scones come out of the oven. The three-tier stand is both a functional serving device and a theatrical centrepiece. Height creates drama; the visual abundance signals generosity before a single bite is taken. In Sydney, afternoon tea has become a social ritual — the quality of the scone and the jam is what guests discuss at the table and remember long after.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
Scone dough must not be over-worked — it will become tough. Bring it together in less than 30 seconds from the moment milk hits flour. The cutter must go straight down with no twisting — twisted edges seal the sides and prevent the characteristic 'break' rise. Pre-cut all sandwiches and refrigerate covered; do not plate more than 20 minutes before service or bread dries and curls.
🎨 Plating Note
The three-tier stand should have colour at every level — use the seasonal petit fours, edible flowers, and small fresh berries strategically. Height and colour draw the eye upward. The stand itself should be clean, polished, and without drips. Scones should arrive visibly steaming — if not, a quick 45-second reheat in a warm oven before plating. Cloth napkins, individual butter knives, and proper tea cups complete the ritual. Afternoon tea at its best is not food service — it is a ceremony.
Recipe No. 18

Macaron Tower (NF)

12 flavours — nut-free shells — house-made ganache & buttercream fillings — tower for 4–6
$48.00 AUD / tower
NF GF
Ingredients (NF shell, makes ~60 shells)
  • Icing sugar (sifted)200 g
  • Sunflower seed flour (finely ground)200 g
  • Aged egg whites (divided)150 g (75 g + 75 g)
  • Caster sugar200 g
  • Water (syrup)50 ml
  • Gel food colours (12 colours)as needed
  • Assorted fillings (ganaches, curds, buttercreams)as per flavours below
12 Flavours
  • Strawberry rose (pink) — white choc ganache + rose water
  • Salted caramel (gold) — salted caramel buttercream
  • Matcha (green) — matcha white choc ganache
  • Lemon (yellow) — lemon curd filling
  • Raspberry (magenta) — raspberry jam + Swiss meringue
  • Passionfruit (orange) — passionfruit curd
  • Dark chocolate (brown) — 70% Valrhona ganache
  • Coconut (white) — coconut cream ganache
  • Blueberry violet (purple) — blueberry compote
  • Coffee (cream/brown) — espresso buttercream
  • Yuzu (pale yellow) — yuzu curd
  • Champagne (ivory) — Champagne reduction buttercream
Method (Italian Meringue Method)
  1. Make tant-pour-tant (TPT): sift icing sugar and sunflower flour together; blend to a very fine powder. Mix with 75 g egg whites to form a thick, smooth paste. Divide into 12 portions; colour each with gel colour.
  2. Make Italian meringue: cook water and sugar to 121°C. Stream onto 75 g whites whipping at medium speed. Increase speed; whip until stiff, cool, and glossy.
  3. Fold meringue into each coloured TPT paste in three additions — macaronage until batter flows off the spatula in a smooth, lava-like ribbon that folds back in 10–15 seconds. Do not over-mix.
  4. Pipe 3.5 cm rounds onto lined trays. Rest (croûter) at room temperature until a dry skin forms (30–60 min, depending on humidity). The surface should feel dry and not sticky when touched lightly.
  5. Bake at 150°C fan for 13–15 minutes. Shells should release cleanly from the paper. Cool completely. Store in a single layer in airtight containers.
  6. Prepare all 12 fillings. Pipe onto flat side of one shell; sandwich with matching shell. Refrigerate minimum 24 hours for the shells to mature — the filling moistens the interior, creating the proper chewy texture.
  7. Assemble tower: arrange on a tiered metal or wooden macaron tower stand by colour family — gradient from light to dark, or in a rainbow pattern. Tag each flavour clearly. Serve at room temperature (remove from refrigerator 30 minutes before serving).
💡 Chef Technique Note
The macaron is the most technically demanding biscuit in French patisserie — it responds to humidity, temperature, altitude, and even the lunar cycle (or so claimed my Paris mentor, who may have been testing my credulity). The NF sunflower flour shell is a genuine technical achievement — the protein and fat profile of sunflower seeds closely mirrors that of almond meal, producing a shell with comparable texture and foot formation. The 24-hour maturation is non-negotiable: an un-matured macaron is a crunchy, hollow disappointment. A matured macaron is a pillow of flavour with a slight chew that is one of the great small pleasures of patisserie.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If macarons crack on top during baking, they were under-rested (skin not formed) or oven too hot. If they have no feet, the batter was under-mixed or the meringue was too stiff. If shells are hollow, they were over-baked or oven temperature too high. Every batch is slightly different — keep detailed notes. Ageing the egg whites (leaving uncovered in the refrigerator for 24–48 hours) produces a more stable meringue with better feet formation.
🎨 Plating Note
The macaron tower is a centrepiece — it should arrive at the table to audible appreciation. Arrange by colour with intention: gradients, rainbows, or thematic colour stories (e.g. pastels for celebration, jewel tones for a statement). Small flavour cards propped beside or a printed menu card adds a professionallegibility. Individual macarons can also be presented in a gift box as a takeaway item — this is excellent for revenue and brand building. Serve with small tongs and individual small plates.
Recipe No. 19

Croissant & Viennoiserie Basket

6-piece house-made selection — classic croissant, pain au chocolat, pain aux raisins
$28.00 AUD / basket
Détrempe (base dough) & Ingredients
  • Strong bread flour (T55 or similar)500 g
  • Full-cream milk (cold)300 ml
  • Caster sugar50 g
  • Fine salt10 g
  • Instant dried yeast7 g
  • Unsalted butter (softened, in dough)25 g
  • European-style butter (beurrage, 84% fat)280 g
  • Egg wash (1 egg + 1 yolk + pinch salt)
  • Dark chocolate batons (pain au chocolat)12 pieces
  • Crème pâtissière + sultanas (pain aux raisins)200 g + 80 g
  • Apricot glaze (finish)60 g
Method (3-day process)
  1. Day 1 — Détrempe: mix flour, sugar, salt (separately from yeast), then yeast. Add cold milk and softened butter; mix until smooth dough forms. Do not over-knead — medium development only. Shape into a rectangle; wrap; refrigerate overnight.
  2. Day 2 — Lamination: pound cold butter block (15×15 cm). Encase in dough. Roll to 20×60 cm. Fold in thirds. Rest 30 min (refrigerated). Repeat 3 more times. After 4 folds, wrap and refrigerate overnight. (84% fat European butter is critical — lower fat butters contain more water and produce steaming rather than lamination.)
  3. Day 3 — Shaping: roll laminated dough to 4 mm. Cut croissant triangles (base 9 cm, height 20 cm); roll from base to tip. Cut rectangles for pain au chocolat (8×14 cm); wrap around two chocolate batons. For pain aux raisins: spread crème pâtissière and sultanas over a 20×30 cm sheet; roll tightly into a log; slice into 2.5 cm rounds.
  4. Prove shaped viennoiserie on lined trays at room temperature (24–26°C) for 2–3 hours until noticeably puffed and the layers visible when held to light. Apply egg wash gently — do not press, which seals the layers.
  5. Bake at 200°C for 16–18 minutes until deep golden brown on all surfaces. Brush pain aux raisins immediately with warmed apricot glaze.
  6. Basket assembly: line a wide bread basket with a linen cloth. Arrange 6 pieces while warm: 2 croissants, 2 pain au chocolat, 2 pain aux raisins. Serve within 20 minutes of baking with small ramekins of butter and jam.
💡 Chef Technique Note
Laminated dough is the foundation on which all viennoiserie rests, and the place where most home bakers struggle most profoundly. The butter block and dough must be at the same pliability when you begin laminating — if the butter is colder and harder than the dough, it will shatter and create uneven layers. Test by pressing both with your finger: they should resist with equal force. The 84% fat European butter is not a luxury — it is a technical specification. Lower fat butters produce steam during lamination, which destroys the clean separation of layers. A croissant should shatter into shards when bitten, with a honeycomb interior. Anything less is a compromise.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
If your croissants are not developing layers in the oven, the butter melted out during lamination — it was too warm. Keep everything cold and work quickly. If the interior is dense (not honeycomb), the dough was over-proved — the yeast consumed the sugars needed for structure. 2–3 hours at 24°C is the absolute maximum proof time. Test: a properly proved croissant should wobble visibly (like set gelatin) when the tray is shaken.
🎨 Plating Note
The viennoiserie basket should arrive warm and visually abundant. A generous linen lining, visible golden pastry, and the accompanying ramekins of good butter and house jam create a complete composition. The basket should feel full — not stacked high, but generous and welcoming. Fresh butter must be at room temperature and properly salted. House strawberry or apricot jam should be in a small ceramic pot with a small silver spoon. This is café culture at its most pleasurable — uncomplicated, beautiful, and deeply satisfying.
Recipe No. 20

Dessert Grazing Platter

Seasonal fruits — house chocolates — mini tarts — house jellies — serves 4
$68.00 AUD / platter
Components
  • Seasonal fruit selection (strawberries, raspberries, figs, grapes, mango, passionfruit)500 g
  • House dark chocolate truffles (cocoa-rolled)8 pieces
  • House milk chocolate bark (sea salt + dried fruit)100 g
  • Mini lemon curd tarts (pâte sucrée shells)6 pieces
  • Mini raspberry jelly domes6 pieces
  • Mini passionfruit pannacotta4 pieces
  • Shortbread rounds8 pieces
  • House caramel sauce (small jar, dipping)60 ml
  • Edible flowers and micro herbs (garnish)as needed
Assembly Method
  1. Begin with the board: use a large slate or marble board (45×30 cm minimum). Place a small jar of caramel sauce slightly off-centre — this anchors the composition.
  2. Place the largest items first: the fruit clusters (strawberries in one corner, grapes in another, figs halved in a third). These define the quadrants.
  3. Fill in with the chocolate elements: truffles in a cluster near the caramel, chocolate bark broken into irregular shards laid flat.
  4. Position the mini tarts and jellies — these are the most delicate elements and need to be stable. Space them evenly across the board so guests at all positions can reach them.
  5. Tuck shortbread rounds into gaps between elements. Fill remaining spaces with micro herbs, edible flowers, and halved passionfruit pods.
  6. Final check: every inch of board should have something — this is a grazing board, not a platter. The eye should be able to wander and discover. Bring to the table as a visual centrepiece; allow guests to self-serve.
💡 Chef Technique Note
The grazing platter is the most democratic form of dessert service — it invites guests to explore, share, and choose their own adventure. The skill is in the curation and composition: variety of texture (crisp, soft, creamy, chewy), variety of flavour (sweet, acidic, bitter, rich), and variety of colour and visual form. The seasonal fruit is the most important element — it provides freshness and contrast to the richness of the made components. In Sydney, where fruit quality is extraordinary, the grazing board is an opportunity to showcase produce as much as technique.
✅ Fail-Proof Tip
Build the board fresh within 30 minutes of service — elements left too long will macerate into each other. Keep delicate items (jellies, pannacotta) refrigerated until the last moment. If fruit is cold from the refrigerator, allow 20 minutes at room temperature before plating — cold fruit has diminished aroma and flavour. The caramel sauce must be warm; microwave 20 seconds and stir before placing on the board.
🎨 Plating Note
The board should arrive at the table to a moment of genuine visual surprise — a sense of bounty and care. Edible flowers and micro herbs are the final flourish that elevate the board from 'nice' to 'exceptional.' Every element should be placed with deliberate intention — resist the temptation to simply pile things on. The negative space between elements (showing the board surface) creates breathing room and makes each item more legible. Provide small dessert plates, forks, and napkins alongside so guests can build their own plate from the communal board.

Single Origin Pour-Over Coffee
Ethiopian Yirgacheffe — Hario V60 — 92°C
$6.50 AUD
Ingredients: Single origin Ethiopian Yirgacheffe beans 18 g (medium-light roast, filter grind), filtered water 300 ml at 92°C, paper filter (pre-rinsed).
Method: Pre-rinse paper filter with hot water; discard rinse water. Add 18 g ground coffee. Bloom with 40 ml water for 30 seconds. Pour remaining water in slow, concentric circles over 2.5–3 minutes. Total brew time 3–3.5 minutes. Serve immediately in a pre-warmed ceramic cup with a small water alongside.
Chef Note: Yirgacheffe is the terroir of Ethiopian coffee — naturally processed, with floral and blueberry notes that make it the ideal companion to delicate pastry. Serve black or with a small ceramic jug of full-cream milk. Never rush the bloom — it off-gases CO₂ and allows even saturation. Food cost includes bean waste from grinding.

Matcha Latte
Ceremonial grade — oat milk — bamboo-whisked
$7.00 AUD
Ingredients: Ceremonial grade matcha powder 3 g, hot water (80°C) 60 ml, oat milk (barista grade) 200 ml, optional honey 5 g.
Method: Sift matcha into a pre-warmed bowl or cup. Add 60 ml water at 80°C (not boiling — bitterness compounds denature below 85°C). Whisk vigorously in a W or zigzag motion using a bamboo chasen until fully dissolved and frothy. Steam oat milk to 62°C with a tight microfoam texture. Pour steamed milk over matcha paste; lift chasen foam onto surface.
Chef Note: Ceremonial grade is non-negotiable — it is shade-grown, stone-ground, and has a sweet vegetal depth that culinary-grade cannot replicate. The 80°C water temperature preserves the L-theanine which provides the calm focus matcha is prized for. Pair with Matcha Opera Cake for a reinforcing flavour experience.

Sparkling Yuzu Lemonade
House-made yuzu syrup — Meyer lemon — sparkling water
$6.00 AUD
Ingredients (makes syrup for 10 serves): Yuzu juice 100 ml, Meyer lemon juice 150 ml, caster sugar 200 g, water 200 ml, yuzu zest 1 fruit. Per serve: syrup 45 ml, sparkling water 180 ml, ice, lemon slice garnish.

No. 24 — GF
French Hot Chocolate
Valrhona 70% — steamed full-cream milk — one-pot method
$7.50 AUD
Ingredients per serve: Valrhona Guanaja 70% chocolate 40 g (finely chopped), full-cream milk 220 ml, caster sugar 5 g (optional), small pinch fleur de sel.
Method: Heat milk to 70°C. Add chopped chocolate and salt; whisk continuously until completely melted and emulsified — approximately 2 minutes. The chocolate will thicken the milk noticeably. Optional: blend with a hand blender for 20 seconds to create a frothy surface. Serve in a pre-warmed cup or small ceramic bowl. Accompany with one canelé or madeleine.
Chef Note: French hot chocolate (chocolat chaud) is not cocoa powder with hot water — it is a thick, rich, proper emulsion of quality chocolate and full-fat milk. The consistency should be closer to a thin ganache than a conventional hot drink. Valrhona Guanaja's particular blend of cocoa origins provides a deep, fruity bitterness that is never overwhelming. The fleur de sel is the finishing stroke — never omit it.
No. 25 — GF
Affogato
Double espresso — vanilla bean ice cream — optional Frangelico
$8.00 AUD
Ingredients per serve: Double espresso 60 ml (freshly pulled), vanilla bean ice cream 2 scoops (80 g), optional Frangelico liqueur 15 ml (hazelnut), one house shortbread biscuit alongside.
Method: Chill a small ceramic or glass cup in the freezer 5 minutes. Scoop two balls of very cold, firm vanilla bean ice cream into the cup. Pull a fresh double espresso. Present the espresso in a small separate vessel at the table. The guest pours the hot espresso over the cold ice cream — the theatre is the service. Add Frangelico before or after espresso if requested.
Chef Note: The affogato (Italian: "drowned") is the simplest dessert drink, and perfectly illustrates how great ingredients require no elaboration. The ice cream must be a proper vanilla bean gelato or ice cream — not soft-serve. The espresso must be pulled correctly: 25–28 seconds, 9 bar, 93°C. At the patisserie, we serve this with a Canelé or Madeleine as a petit four pairing. The temperature contrast — scalding coffee against frozen cream — is the entire experience.

Australian Bush Tea
Lemon myrtle — wattleseed chai — wildflower honey
$6.00 AUD
Ingredients per serve: Dried lemon myrtle leaves 3 g, roasted wattleseed 2 g, cinnamon stick ¼, ginger slices 3, black peppercorns 3, star anise ¼, hot water 300 ml (92°C), wildflower honey 10 g.
Method: Combine lemon myrtle, wattleseed, and spices in a pre-warmed teapot or infuser. Pour 92°C water over; steep 4–5 minutes. Strain into a pre-warmed teacup. No. 27 — VG · GF · NF
Strawberry Rose Iced Tea
House-made infusion — cold-brewed — floral & fruity
$6.50 AUD
Ingredients (makes 1 litre): Fresh strawberries 300 g (hulled, halved), dried rose petals (food-grade) 5 g, jasmine green tea (loose-leaf) 8 g, cold filtered water 1 litre, caster sugar 80 g, rose water 5 ml, lemon juice 20 ml.
Method: Cold-brew: combine strawberries, rose petals, tea, and cold water in a jug. Cover and refrigerate 8–12 hours (or overnight). Strain through a fine-mesh sieve; press gently to extract liquid (do not force or tea will become bitter). Add sugar; stir until dissolved. Add rose water and lemon juice. Bottle; refrigerate. Per serve: pour 200 ml over a glass of ice; garnish with a fresh strawberry and a dried rose petal.
Chef Note: Cold-brewing extracts flavour more gently than hot infusion — the result is a clean, rounded tea with no bitterness and a vivid fruit-floral profile. This drink was developed as an alternative to alcohol for guests who prefer non-alcoholic options during the afternoon tea service. The cold-brewed method extends shelf life to 5 days under refrigeration. Always label clearly with date of production. The strawberry and rose combination echoes the Strawberry Watermelon Cake — it creates a coherent flavour narrative across the menu.
No. 28 — GF · NF
Champagne & Patisserie Pairing
100ml Blanc de Blancs — paired with two house petit fours
$22.00 AUD
Ingredients per serve: Champagne Blanc de Blancs (100% Chardonnay, NV) 100 ml, two selected house petit fours (rotated seasonally — recommend: Canelé Bordelais + Lemon Shortbread, or Passionfruit Jelly + Financier), small menu card describing the pairing rationale.
Method: Chill Champagne to 8°C. Pour into a pre-chilled tulip flute (not a coupe — the tulip retains bubbles and directs the aroma). Select two petit fours that complement the Champagne's minerality and citrus notes. Present on a small slate alongside the flute with a brief written note on the pairing — guests appreciate the education. The pairing should be ordered in advance to allow proper chilling.
Chef Note: Blanc de Blancs Champagne — made exclusively from Chardonnay — is the most patisserie-friendly of all sparkling wines. Its high acidity, fine persistent bubbles, and flavours of green apple, brioche, and lemon cream cleanse the palate between bites of rich pastry. The Canelé pairing is particularly successful: the rum and vanilla custard interior of the canelé mirrors the yeasty brioche notes of the Champagne in a way that is greater than either alone. This pairing elevates the patisserie experience from a café visit into something genuinely memorable.
