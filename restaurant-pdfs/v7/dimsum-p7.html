// Chef's Lens — Opening a Dim Sum Teahouse Outside the Cantonese Diaspora
Dim sum is one of the most technically demanding restaurant concepts in the world. The har gow wrapper requires a specific non-gluten dough worked at a specific temperature in a specific way. The cheung fun rice sheet must be steamed to the exact moment when it is set but still pliable enough to roll. The bao dough requires the specific low-protein Hong Kong flour to achieve the characteristic white pillow texture. These are not approximations — they are precise techniques that take years of practice to master. The first question to answer before opening is not about location or funding. It is: do you have a dim sum chef who has made har gow? Not someone who can follow a recipe and try. Someone who has made har gow many hundreds of times. The gap between a first-attempt har gow (thick wrapper, uneven pleats, filling leaking) and a professional har gow (translucent wrapper, seven clean pleats, filling visible but contained) is a year of daily practice. That gap is visible immediately to anyone who has eaten dim sum in Hong Kong, in San Gabriel Valley, in Flushing. They will not return. If you cannot find a trained dim sum chef in your market, consider a smaller teahouse concept with bao, cheung fun, congee, and lo mai gai as the core items — and purchase your har gow and siu mai from a reputable Asian wholesale supplier while you build the in-house skill. Be honest with your menu: "Our har gow is made in collaboration with [supplier name], a traditional Hong Kong dim sum kitchen." That honesty builds trust. The alternative — poor quality har gow you pretend is house-made — destroys it. But when you do get it right — when the har gow wrapper is translucent and the prawn is visible through it, and when a Sunday morning table of eight Chinese grandmothers orders three rounds without comment because the comment IS the silence, because silence from a Cantonese grandmother who has eaten har gow every Sunday of her life means it is acceptable — then you have built something that will outlast you. Yum cha is not a restaurant format. It is a weekly family ritual that families entrust to you to host. That trust, once earned, is Sunday after Sunday for the rest of your business life. 茶飯 (Tea and rice.)
