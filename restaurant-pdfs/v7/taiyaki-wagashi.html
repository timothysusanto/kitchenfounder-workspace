Concept & Origin
The Fish That Never Goes Out of Style
From Edo-period ningyo-yaki to modern taiyaki — and the 1,200-year wagashi tradition that sits behind every bean paste filling.

Taiyaki — The Origin

Taiyaki (鯛焼き — literally "baked sea bream") was invented in 1909 at Naniwaya Sōhonten in Azabu-Jūban, Tokyo. The fish shape was chosen deliberately: sea bream (tai) is Japan's most auspicious fish, associated with celebration, luck, and new beginnings. The original filling was koshian — smooth red bean paste, the foundation of Japanese wagashi confectionery. The recipe has not changed in 115 years.

The iron mould is the technology. Two cast-iron fish-shaped cavities on a long handle. Batter in, filling in, close, flip, two minutes each side over a gas flame. The result: a crisp, golden fish-shaped cake with a molten filling visible only when bitten. The suspense of the first bite — where is the filling? — is part of the experience.

Naniwaya Sōhonten still operates today. The queue still forms at 10am. The taiyaki still costs ¥180. The iron moulds are original.

Wagashi — 1,200 Years of Confectionery

Wagashi (和菓子 — "Japanese confectionery") encompasses every traditional Japanese sweet: mochi, daifuku, yokan, namagashi, dorayaki, monaka, and hundreds of others. The tradition dates to the Heian period (794–1185 AD) when sweets were offered at imperial court ceremonies and Buddhist temples.

Wagashi philosophy is inseparable from the seasons — each sweet is designed to evoke a specific moment in the natural year. Spring: sakura namagashi, cherry blossom daifuku. Summer: yokan with cooling mint, kakigori mochi. Autumn: chestnut kinton, persimmon nerikiri. Winter: yuzu monaka, oshiruko sweet soup. A wagashi shop that doesn't change its menu with the seasons is not a wagashi shop.

The modern opportunity: Pairing the approachability of taiyaki (street food, under ¥300, anyone can eat it) with the craft and beauty of wagashi (premium, seasonal, gift-worthy, ¥500–2,000) creates a business with two revenue streams, two price points, and two audiences that overlap perfectly in the same small shop.

The Business Model

Retail + Café Hybrid
  • Taiyaki counter: Street-facing window, iron mould production, queue culture, ¥180–350 per piece
  • Wagashi display case: Interior counter, seasonal items, gift boxes, ¥400–2,000 per item
  • Tea service: 4–8 seats, matcha + wagashi pairing, ¥1,200–2,000 per set
  • Gift retail: Boxed wagashi assortments, omiyage (souvenir) packaging, ¥2,000–5,000
  • Combined model: Foot traffic buys taiyaki → curious guests enter for wagashi → seated guests order tea sets → departing guests buy gift boxes

Seasonal Calendar Overview

  • Spring (Mar–May): Sakura nerikiri, hanami daifuku, cherry blossom taiyaki (sakura cream filling)
  • Summer (Jun–Aug): Mizuyōkan (chilled yokan), mint-yuzu daifuku, kakigori mochi
  • Autumn (Sep–Nov): Chestnut kinton, persimmon nerikiri, kuri yokan, classic koshian taiyaki
  • Winter (Dec–Feb): Oshiruko (hot sweet soup with mochi), yuzu monaka, strawberry daifuku
  • Year-round: Classic taiyaki (koshian + custard), basic daifuku, dorayaki

Method
  1. Sift flour, baking powder, baking soda, sugar, and salt into a bowl. Mix dry ingredients thoroughly.
  2. Whisk egg, milk, oil, and mirin together in a separate bowl.
  3. Pour wet into dry. Whisk until just combined — a few small lumps are acceptable. Do not overwork the batter: gluten development makes it tough.
  4. Rest batter 20 minutes minimum — 40 minutes preferred. The baking powder activates and the batter relaxes. Rest 20–40 min
  5. The mirin is the secret: it adds a light sweetness and a subtle glaze to the finished taiyaki skin. Do not skip it. Naniwaya uses it. This is why their taiyaki has a different finish to others.
Chef's Tip — Iron Mould Temperature: Preheat the iron mould over medium gas flame for 3 minutes — both sides. Brush lightly with neutral oil. The correct temperature produces a taiyaki that releases cleanly and has an even golden-brown finish. Too cool: batter sticks and tears. Too hot: exterior burns before interior cooks. Test: a drop of batter should sizzle and set in 10 seconds — that is the correct temperature. Never use butter to grease the mould — it burns at this temperature.

Classic Koshian Taiyaki
こしあん鯛焼き · The 115-Year Original

IngredientQtyCost

Water (for cooking)1.2L—
Koshian Method
  1. Rinse adzuki beans. Soak overnight in cold water or quick-soak: boil 5 min, drain, cover with fresh cold water.
  2. Simmer beans in 1.2L fresh water until completely tender — 60–75 minutes. Beans should crush between fingers with no resistance.
  3. Drain beans. Pass through a fine sieve or food mill to remove skins — press firmly. You want the pure smooth paste, not the skins.
  4. Transfer smooth paste to a wide heavy-based pan. Add sugar and salt. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly, until the paste pulls away from the sides of the pan and holds a shape. Cook until firm · 15–20 min
  5. Cool completely. Shape into 30g cylinders (one per taiyaki). Freeze unused portions — they keep 3 months.
Assembly
  1. Heat mould. Pour 25ml batter into each fish cavity — fill to 80%.
  2. Place one 30g koshian cylinder into the centre of the batter. Close mould firmly.
  3. Cook over medium gas flame: 2 minutes each side. Flip without opening. The batter expands around the filling as it cooks.
  4. Open mould — taiyaki should be golden, slightly crisp, and release cleanly. Cool on a wire rack for 2 minutes before serving.
Chef's Tip: Koshian is made in large batches and frozen. Defrost overnight in the refrigerator. Never microwave to defrost — uneven heating changes the texture. The discipline of making koshian in-house (vs buying commercial paste) is the single most important quality decision you will make. Commercial paste is sweeter, less complex, and less interesting. Your koshian is the product.

Vanilla Custard Taiyaki
カスタード鯛焼き · Modern Classic

Whole milk250ml$0.38

Caster sugar60g$0.12
Cornstarch30g$0.06
Vanilla bean, seeds only½ bean$0.70

Method
  1. Heat milk with vanilla seeds to just below simmer. Whisk yolks, sugar, and cornstarch until pale and thick.
  2. Pour hot milk over yolk mixture whisking constantly. Return to heat, cook to full boil stirring — 2 minutes. Full boil · 2 min
  3. Remove from heat, whisk in cold butter. The custard must be thicker than a crème pâtissière — it needs to hold its shape inside a hot taiyaki without running. If too loose, return to heat 1 minute more.
  4. Press plastic wrap directly on surface. Refrigerate until completely cold. Scoop into 35g balls for filling.
Chef's Tip: The custard filling must be thick enough to hold its shape at 180°C — the internal temperature of a cooking taiyaki. Standard crème pâtissière is too thin. Increase the cornstarch to 30g per 250ml milk (vs the usual 20g) to achieve the correct consistency. When you bite the taiyaki, the custard should be hot, molten, and thick — not watery. Test one before service every day.

Method
  1. Make matcha batter: add 6g sifted Uji matcha powder to the dry ingredients in the master batter. The cooked taiyaki exterior will be a delicate pale green — beautiful contrast with any filling.
  2. Matcha custard: whisk 6g sifted Uji matcha into the warm custard mixture before setting. The green custard should be vivid — if it looks grey-green, the matcha quality is too low.

いちご大福 · Spring Icon
$0.95 / piece

Fresh strawberry, small-medium, hulled1$0.35

Method
  1. Mix shiratamako, sugar, and water until smooth. Microwave 2 minutes. Stir vigorously. Microwave 1 more minute. Stir — the mochi should be translucent, elastic, and very sticky. Microwave 3 min total
  2. Turn out onto a surface dusted generously with katakuriko. Divide into portions (40g each). Dust hands — mochi sticks to everything.
  3. Flatten each mochi piece into a circle. Place 20g koshian in centre. Press koshian around the whole strawberry (point up), encasing it in bean paste. Place bean-coated strawberry on mochi, bean side down.
  4. Wrap mochi up around the strawberry, pinch seam closed at the bottom. The finished daifuku should be round, smooth, with no seam visible from the top.
  5. Dust with katakuriko. Serve within 3 hours — mochi hardens after this time.
Chef's Tip: The strawberry should be exactly the right size — too large and the mochi tears, too small and the ratio is wrong. Source consistent small-medium strawberries (30–40g per berry). The katakuriko (potato starch) dusting is traditional and essential — cornstarch is an acceptable substitute but potato starch has a cleaner flavour. Make daifuku in batches of 12 every 2 hours during service — never more than 3 hours old.

Method
  1. Dissolve baking soda in warm water. Whisk eggs and sugar until pale and thick — 3 minutes. Add honey and mirin.
  2. Add baking soda solution. Fold in sifted flour until smooth. Rest 30 minutes — the baking soda needs time to activate and the batter needs to relax.
  3. Cook pancakes on a lightly oiled flat griddle at 170°C — pour 30ml batter per pancake, cook until bubbles form across the surface and edges are set (90 sec). Flip, cook 30 seconds more. Griddle 170°C · 90 sec
  4. Cool flat on wire racks. Sandwich pairs with 40g tsubuan (chunky paste — always tsubuan for dorayaki, never koshian). The chunky texture contrasts with the soft pancake.
  5. Wrap tightly in cellophane if storing. Best within 6 hours of assembly.
Chef's Tip: The honey and mirin in the batter give dorayaki its distinctive deep golden colour and slight shine. Plain pancake recipes produce flat colour — dorayaki must be amber-gold. The tsubuan filling is intentional: the visible red bean chunks through the sides of the sandwich are a quality signal. Offer: classic tsubuan · matcha cream + tsubuan · custard + fresh cream (modern variant). Three fillings, one pancake recipe.

Method

Method
  1. Combine domyoji flour, sugar, and pink water (add colouring to hot water before mixing). Mix until a coarse, slightly sticky dough forms.
  2. Steam in a bamboo steamer for 20 minutes. Remove, cool slightly. The texture should be chewy, with visible rice grains intact — this is correct. Steam 20 min

  5. The leaf is edible and should be eaten with the mochi. It imparts a delicate floral-briny flavour. Never remove it before serving.
Chef's Tip: The preserved sakura leaf is the signature flavour of sakura mochi — without it, this is just pink mochi. Import from Japanese specialty suppliers (Kataoka & Co, or similar). The leaf's subtle saltiness against the sweet bean paste and floral pink rice is one of the great flavour contrasts in wagashi. Method
  1. Combine tsubuan and water in a small saucepan. Simmer over low heat 5 minutes, stirring, until the bean paste dissolves into a pourable soup. Adjust water for consistency — it should flow from a spoon in thick ribbons, not run like water.

Usucha Matcha (Whisked)
Hot · Ceremonial · The Signature
Sift 2g Uji ceremonial matcha into a pre-warmed chawan bowl. Add 70ml water at exactly 80°C. Whisk in a rapid W motion until a fine foam appears — 20–30 seconds. The froth should be stable, fine-bubbled, and uniform. No sweetener. Served with a small seasonal wagashi on the side. This is the soul of the shop — if the matcha quality is wrong, nothing else matters.

Temperature: water at 80°C, bowl pre-warmed.
Food Cost $1.20 · Menu Price $7.50 · GP 84%
Hojicha Pot Tea
Hot · Roasted Tea · Best Seller
5g high-quality roasted hojicha leaves steeped in 300ml water at 90°C for 90 seconds — no longer, or it becomes bitter. Served in a small cast-iron or ceramic teapot with 2 matching cups. The roasted, caramel-amber colour and nutty aroma are immediately comforting. Refills of hot water available. This is the most approachable tea for non-matcha guests and drives the highest table time of any beverage. Long table dwell time = higher food spend.
Food Cost $0.45 · Menu Price $6.50 · GP 93.1%
Iced Matcha Latte
Cold · Year-Round
3g Uji matcha whisked with 30ml water at 80°C to a smooth paste. Pour over ice (4–5 large cubes) in a tall clear glass. Add 200ml cold oat milk (or full-fat) — pour slowly, the green swirling into white milk is the visual moment. Optional: 10ml honey or koshian syrup on the side for guests who prefer sweetness. The contrast of forest green matcha against white milk in a clear glass is the signature drink photo.
Food Cost $0.95 · Menu Price $7.00 · GP 86.4%
Amazake (Hot Sweet Rice Drink)
Hot · Non-Alcoholic · Winter
Amazake is a traditional Japanese sweet drink made from fermented rice or sake lees — naturally sweet, low alcohol (virtually nil in the fermented rice version). Heat to 65°C. Serve in a small earthenware cup with a tiny pinch of fresh grated ginger on top — the warmth, sweetness, and ginger heat is one of the great cold-weather drinks. Available Dec–Feb at traditional matsuri festivals — offering it in a permanent shop makes it special outside of that context. Pairs with oshiruko as a winter set.
Food Cost $0.55 · Menu Price $5.50 · GP 90%
Yuzu Honey Soda
Cold · Non-Alcoholic · Spring/Summer
Fresh lemon juice 15ml + bottled yuzu juice 20ml + honey syrup 20ml (honey:water 1:1, warmed to combine). Shake briefly over ice. Pour into a tall glass with ice. Top with sparkling mineral water 160ml. Garnish: thin lemon round + yuzu zest strip curled over the rim. The citrus brightness and honey floral sweetness is the ideal accompaniment to the sweetness of wagashi — it resets the palate between bites.
Food Cost $0.60 · Menu Price $5.50 · GP 89.1%
Genmaicha Pot
Hot · Brown Rice Green Tea
4g genmaicha (green tea blended with roasted brown rice) steeped in 280ml water at 80°C for 60 seconds. The toasted rice in genmaicha gives a nutty, savoury quality that is completely different from standard green tea. It pairs beautifully with sweet wagashi — the savoury-nutty tea against the sweet bean paste is a contrast that enhances both. Served in a ceramic teapot. Offer as the alternative pairing for guests who find matcha too strong.

Sakura Milk (Spring)
Hot or Iced · March–May
Steam full-fat milk to 65°C. Add 20ml preserved sakura blossom syrup (Japanese import). The milk turns a delicate pale pink and takes on a floral, lightly briny cherry blossom flavour. A drop of rose water (2 drops only — more is overwhelming) amplifies the floral note. Garnish with one preserved sakura blossom floating on the surface. The pale pink milk is the spring visual. Serve hot in a white ceramic cup or iced over ice in a clear glass.

Kinako Warm Milk
Hot · Comfort Drink · All Year
Steam 200ml full-fat milk to 65°C. Stir in 10g kinako (roasted soybean flour) until fully dissolved — use a small handheld whisk. Add 8ml honey. The result: a naturally sweet, nutty, caramel-golden warm milk that is distinctly Japanese in flavour. Kinako is the flour of soybean — roasted, it develops a complex peanut-butter-adjacent flavour. Dust with additional kinako on top. This is the highest-comfort drink in the menu and the best paired with fresh mochi.

Scripts & Customer Retention
Script 01 · Welcoming — Window & Shop
Guest approaches the taiyaki window or enters the shop.
At the window: "Fresh taiyaki — just out of the iron. Classic red bean, custard, or matcha today. All made here." [Point to the iron mould on the flame.] The visual and the sound of the iron does most of the selling. Staff should be working — not standing idle — when guests approach.
Entering the shop: "Welcome. Take your time — everything in the case is made this morning. The seasonal items are on the top shelf. If you'd like a tea set, we have seats at the counter."
Script 02 · Explaining Wagashi to Non-Japanese Guests
Guest looks at the display case with curiosity but hesitation.
Staff: "These are wagashi — traditional Japanese sweets. They're made from bean paste, mochi rice, and seasonal ingredients. Nothing artificial. The flavour is quite different from Western desserts — less sweet, more earthy. Would you like to try a small piece before you decide?"
If they agree to try: Offer a tiny sample of yokan or a small daifuku piece. This conversion step turns hesitant browsers into buyers 60–70% of the time. Never offer to explain every item — ask which catches their eye and explain that one.
Script 03 · Tea Set Upsell
Guest is buying 2+ wagashi items individually.
Staff: "If you're having a few pieces, our matcha ceremony set includes the matcha and three wagashi — it's a nicer experience than taking them separately, and it's [price comparison]. Would you like to sit at the counter?"
Key: Never say "cheaper" — say "a nicer experience." You are selling an upgrade, not a discount.
Script 04 · The Gift Box Recommendation
Guest is buying for someone else, or mentions "gifts" or "souvenir."
Staff: "We have gift boxes — 8 pieces assorted, all made today. [Price.] We wrap it here. It travels well — everything is individually packaged inside." [Show the box.] "It's a fairly unusual gift because most people haven't seen wagashi like this outside Japan."
That last sentence is the close. Uniqueness is the strongest gift justification.
Script 05 · Seasonal Item Explanation
Guest asks about a seasonal item in the display case.
Staff: "That's [sakura mochi / kuri kinton / oshiruko] — it's only available in [spring / autumn / winter]. Wagashi follows the seasons very strictly, so this one will be gone in [weeks]. The [flavour detail]. It pairs beautifully with the hojicha tea."
Script 06 · Taiyaki Freshness
When a guest asks how fresh the taiyaki are, or if they can wait for a fresh batch.
Staff: "We make them every 15–20 minutes. The ones in the warmer have been there about [X] minutes — still warm. If you'd like one straight from the iron, that's about [Y] minutes." Always give the honest answer. A guest who waits 5 minutes for a fresh taiyaki is a guest who trusts you. That trust converts into a return visit.

6 Customer Retention Mechanisms

1 · Seasonal Announcement Cards: Every guest who buys a tea set receives a small card: "Next season begins [date] — [name of next seasonal item]. We announce it on Instagram one week before." This converts one-time buyers into seasonal regulars.

2 · The Nerikiri Display as Daily Social Content: Photograph today's nerikiri arrangement every morning before opening. Post on Instagram with the season and the wagashi name. This is your daily content strategy — no extra effort, consistent posting, always beautiful.

3 · Gift Box Pre-Order Programme: Before Golden Week, Obon, and New Year — open pre-orders for gift boxes 3 weeks in advance. Post on Instagram: "Gift boxes for [holiday] — pre-order closes [date]." Creates urgency, guarantees revenue, and reduces production waste.

4 · Taiyaki Fresh Batch Timer: A small chalkboard or digital display at the window showing "Next fresh batch: [time]." Guests who see this will wait, or will return at the correct time. It communicates craft and systems simultaneously.

5 · Wagashi Making Workshop (Monthly): One Saturday morning per month — 6 guests, ¥4,500 per person, 90 minutes learning nerikiri or mochi making. The workshop is also your content creation session. Films generate 5–10 posts. Participants post their own content. Revenue from the workshop itself covers 2 weeks of Instagram advertising at zero incremental cost.

6 · Loyal Guest Wagashi Tasting (Quarterly): Post: "Our next new seasonal items are being tested this [date]. First 10 guests to DM us get a complimentary tasting." This creates a micro-community of invested regulars. They become brand advocates. They bring friends on the official launch day.

Shop Design — 20–35 sqm Hybrid

Zone 1 — Street Window (3 sqm): Taiyaki iron moulds on gas burners, facing outward. Guests can see the production. The smoke and aroma draws foot traffic. This is your live advertisement. Never let this station be empty during trading hours.

🎋 Michelin Chef's Lens — The Sweetness of Transience

The Japanese word for wagashi contains the character 和 (wa) — harmony, Japan, peace. Japanese confectionery is not merely sweet food. It is a material expression of mono no aware — the awareness of impermanence, the bittersweet recognition that beautiful things do not last. The cherry blossom mochi is beautiful because it will be gone in six weeks. The chestnut kinton tastes like autumn precisely because autumn ends.

A wagashi shop that doesn't change with the seasons is not a wagashi shop — it is a candy store. The seasonal calendar is not a marketing technique. It is the philosophical core of the entire tradition. When you remove the sakura mochi from your display case on May 16th, you are not managing inventory. You are honouring the season's end. The discipline of removal is as important as the craft of creation.

Taiyaki sits alongside wagashi as the populist expression of the same value system. The fish mould has not changed since 1909 because it doesn't need to change. The sea bream shape is auspicious. The casting iron is the correct tool. The red bean filling is the correct filling. Improvement here is not innovation — it is erosion. The taiyaki shops that last for generations are the ones that change nothing.

Your job, as an operator who has read this guide, is to understand which parts of your menu to protect from change (the classic taiyaki, the koshian recipe, the seasonal calendar structure) and which parts to evolve (the modern fillings, the matcha cream variants, the gift packaging). Protect the soul. Evolve the surface. This is the discipline that makes 115-year-old businesses.

"物の哀れ — Mono no aware. The pathos of things. The awareness that beauty and impermanence are the same thing." — Heian period, 1,200 years ago. Still the most important idea in Japanese aesthetics.
