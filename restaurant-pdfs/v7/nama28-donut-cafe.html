The NAMA28 Concept · Guide Bible · Why This Business Works02
The I'm Donut? Generation — Japan's Craft Donut Revolution
In 2017, a small donut shop opened in Fukuoka called I'm Donut? — and within three years it had lines stretching around the block, a dedicated Instagram following in the hundreds of thousands, and had spawned an entire category of Japanese craft donut shops across Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, and beyond. The secret was deceptively simple: sake lees yeast + tangzhong method + fry at 160°C. The result was a donut so feathery, so impossibly light, so different from the Krispy Kreme or Mister Donut consumer had previously known, that the entire country had to try it. NAMA28 is built on that foundation — and extends it. Where I'm Donut? built six flavours brilliantly, NAMA28 builds twenty-eight variants across four distinct dough bases and seven flavour families. It is a system, not just a recipe. It is designed from the start for replication, training, and franchising.

Why 28 Variants — The Business Logic

The Discovery Model

Most donut brands run 6–10 flavours. A guest who visits twice in one week can try everything and has no reason for a third visit. NAMA28's 28-variant, 7-family architecture creates a genuinely different experience on every visit — a guest who tries three donuts one day and three more the next is experiencing a different product each time. The collection creates a reason to return that no 6-flavour menu can.

Additionally, families create vocabulary: "Have you tried the Tropical Asian family yet?" becomes a staff question that drives exploration and upsell. The naming architecture — numbered donuts, named families, Base A/B/C/D designation — gives guests language to use when sharing on Instagram. "I had the #17 Pandan Coconut on Base B." This specificity signals authority. It makes the guest feel like a connoisseur.

The Seasonal Scarcity Engine

Family 7 (4 seasonal variants) is built on Base D — the proprietary hybrid dough not disclosed outside the franchise system. It changes quarterly. This alone drives 4 social media moments per year, 4 press/blog mentions, and 4 waves of "have you tried the new one?" conversations. The limited, time-bound nature of F7 donuts creates urgency that year-round menus cannot replicate.

The Four Dough Philosophy

Base A — Sake Lees Brioche (Classic Nama)

Fry 160–165°C · Families F1, F2, F3
The original. Sake lees (酒粕) from the rice wine brewing process is activated as a yeast. Combined with tangzhong (milk-cooked roux paste), the result is an ultra-fine, featherlight crumb with a faint floral, barely-perceptible ferment note. Pale gold crust only. This is the donut that started the Japanese craft donut movement.

Base B — Potato Brioche (Cloud-Soft)

Fry 162°C · Families F4, F5
Riced Russet potato (steamed, never boiled) replaces 15–20% of flour weight. The result: dramatically softer crumb, mochi-adjacent texture, 6–8hr freshness window vs 4hr for Base A. Best paired with heavy, bold fillings — miso caramel, ganache, tropical compotes. The potato is invisible to the palate — it is a texture tool, not a flavour ingredient.

Base C — Almond Meal Enriched

Fry 165°C · Families F4, F6
100g blanched almond meal + 30g almond butter in the dough. Dense, moist, nutty undertone. Pairs with bold flavours that benefit from a substantial structural contrast — dark chocolate, citrus curd, espresso. Almond fat slows gluten development — allow 10 min longer kneading.

Base D — Potato + Almond Hybrid ✦ (Franchise Signature)

Fry 162°C · Family F7 only · Proprietary
120g riced potato + 60g almond meal combined. Pillowy from potato, nutty from almond, complex from sake lees. 16hr minimum cold proof. Used exclusively for seasonal F7 donuts. Full formula is not disclosed in franchisee documents — supplied as pre-mixed dry blend. This is the franchise IP moat.

The NAMA28 Selling Proposition
In a market where every café sells the same six coffee drinks and the same three pastries, NAMA28 offers something genuinely unexplained: a donut that doesn't taste like any other donut, made with ingredients (sake lees, tangzhong, kinako, miso) that most customers cannot name but immediately want more of. The first donut sells on curiosity. The second sells on addiction. The third sells on the identity of being someone who knows about Japanese craft donuts. At that point, the customer is no longer buying a donut — they are buying membership in a small group of people who understand something the rest of the world hasn't discovered yet. That is the most powerful sales mechanism in food retail.

Starter 01 · Savoury Bite
Miso Butter Tamago Sando
味噌バターたまごサンド
Shokupan milk bread · miso butter scrambled egg · Japanese mayo · shiso — the NAMA28 signature sandwich
Eggs 75°C soft set1 serve · $12
Ingredients (per serve)
Miso Butter Scrambled Egg
Free-range eggs3
Unsalted butter20 g
White miso (Marukome Shiro)1 tsp — dissolved in butter first
Double cream1 tbsp — added off heat
Fine saltpinch only — miso is salty
The Sando
Shokupan (milk bread), thick-cut, crusts removed2 slices
Kewpie mayonnaise1.5 tbsp — both inner faces
Fresh green shiso, chiffonade5–6 large leaves
Toasted sesame seeds½ tsp
Furikake (optional light dusting)pinch
Method
  1. 1Melt butter in cold pan. Add white miso and stir to dissolve completely before adding eggs — never add miso to the eggs directly (it clumps). The miso-butter mixture should be smooth and fragrant.
  2. 2Add eggs off heat. Return to very low heat. Stir constantly with silicone spatula — slow, constant figure-8 strokes. Remove from heat when eggs are still very soft, wet, and barely set (75°C). Add double cream. Stir. The cream arrests cooking. Rest 45 seconds. The finished eggs should be glossy, soft curds — not dry or bounced.
  3. 3Spread Kewpie generously on both bread faces. Layer shiso chiffonade on base slice — cover the whole surface. Spoon eggs on top. Scatter sesame. Close sandwich. Press firmly but gently. Trim to perfect square. Slice diagonally — the cross-section showing layered egg, shiso, bread is the visual goal. Display cut-side-up.
The Miso Scramble PrincipleThe miso provides salt, umami, and a barely-perceptible ferment note that elevates the egg from breakfast to signature dish. The shiso adds a herbal freshness that cuts the richness. These three flavour layers — egg richness, miso depth, shiso freshness — in combination is what makes this starter memorable rather than forgettable.

Starter 02 · Savoury Bite
Hojicha Cold Brew Chawanmushi
ほうじ茶冷製茶碗蒸し
Cold-set steamed savoury egg custard · hojicha dashi · edamame · shiso oil · seasonal garnish
Steam 85°C · 18 min1 ramekin · $13
Ingredients (per 4 ramekins)
Hojicha Dashi Base
Kombu dashi (or quality liquid dashi)300 ml
Hojicha loose leaf8 g — steep 4 min at 80°C, strain
Light soy sauce (usukuchi)1.5 tsp
Mirin1 tsp
Fine salt¼ tsp
Custard
Whole eggs3
Hojicha dashi (above, cooled)300 ml
Egg:dashi ratio1:3 by volume — silky set
Garnish
Edamame (shelled, blanched)5–6 per ramekin — placed in base
Shiso oil (blended shiso + neutral oil)3–4 drops on set surface
Micro shiso or yuzu zestto finish
Method
  1. 1Steep hojicha in kombu dashi at 80°C exactly 4 minutes. Strain immediately — the dashi turns an extraordinary warm amber-brown with a roasted, smoky note. Season with soy, mirin, salt. Cool to 30°C.
  2. 2Gently whisk eggs — do not create foam. Combine with cooled dashi. Pass through a fine sieve twice. Skim any surface bubbles with paper towel. This step is non-negotiable for a smooth custard surface.
  3. 3Place 5–6 edamame in base of each ramekin. Ladle custard gently to fill. Cover with foil (prevents condensation on surface). Steam at 85°C for 18 minutes. Never exceed 90°C — bubbles form in the custard and destroy the silky texture.
  4. 4Rest 5 min. Remove foil. Add 3–4 drops shiso oil across the surface — it pools in small green circles against the amber custard. Add micro shiso or yuzu zest. Serve immediately warm, or chill and serve cold (both formats work).
Why Hojicha Dashi WorksTraditional chawanmushi uses plain kombu + bonito dashi. The hojicha addition creates a warm, roasted undertone in the custard that echoes the donut menu — a flavour through-line connecting the starter to the main experience. This is the Japanese concept of mise en scène applied to food: the whole experience is a conversation, not a series of unrelated dishes.

Purpose + Method

Donut holes are made from the off-cuts and trimmings when shaping the full donuts. In most kitchens they are discarded or eaten by staff. In NAMA28, they become the highest-margin item on the starter menu — $14 for what is essentially zero-cost raw material. Fry at the appropriate temperature for each base (A: 160°C, B: 162°C, C: 165°C). Because they are small, cook time is 45–60 seconds per side only.

The flight is served with a small printed card explaining the three bases — what makes them different, which flavour family each is paired with. This is not just a dish, it is an education. Guests who understand the dough architecture order more precisely, tip more, and return more often.

The Flight StrategyWhen a guest orders the flight first, every subsequent donut order is more confident and specific. They are now educated. "I want two on Base B because I prefer the softer texture." That specificity is repeat-visit behaviour. The flight is the most valuable $14 on the menu because it turns a casual visitor into a regular.

The 10 hero donuts represent the core daily menu — 2–3 from each flavour family rotating through the week. Each recipe below is a full build: dough base assignment, filling, glaze, and finish. All Base A donuts fry at 160–165°C, 90 sec per side. Cool completely before filling.

Hero Donut 01 · Family 1 · Base A
Shiro An Vanilla Bean
白あんバニラ · Donut #01
White bean paste · Madagascar vanilla diplomat cream · kinako coating · tonka bean · $9.80
Base A ClassicFill cold · Serve 4hr
Ingredients (per 6 donuts)
Shiro An (White Bean Paste)
Dried white beans (navy or cannellini)200 g dry weight
Caster sugar100 g
Fine saltpinch
Unsalted butter15 g — adds gloss and richness
Vanilla Diplomat Cream
Whole milk250 ml
Egg yolks3
Caster sugar50 g
Cornflour20 g
Madagascar vanilla bean (split + scraped)1 pod
Unsalted butter20 g
Gelatine leaves1.5 sheets
Double cream, cold, whipped to soft peaks120 ml
Kinako Finish
Kinako + icing sugar (3:1 mix)generous dusting
Tonka bean, microplanedtiny grating — 1 pass per donut
Method
  1. 1Shiro an: soak beans 8hrs, cook until very soft (45–60 min). Drain. Pass through tamis or fine sieve to a smooth paste. Return to pan with sugar, salt, and butter. Cook on medium heat, stirring, until the paste pulls away from the sides and holds a shape — approximately 15–20 min. Cool. The shiro an should be ivory-white, smooth, barely sweet, gently earthy.
  2. 2Vanilla diplomat: soak gelatine. Bring milk + vanilla pod to simmer. Whisk yolks, sugar, cornflour. Temper hot milk in. Return to pan, cook to 80°C until thick. Add butter, squeezed gelatine. Strain. Cling on surface. Cool to room temp, then refrigerate 3 hrs. Whisk set cream to loosen. Fold in whipped cream diplomat-style. Keep cold.
  3. 3Pipe 20g shiro an into each Base A donut via side nozzle (into the centre cavity). Then pipe vanilla diplomat as a generous rosette on the top (cut a shallow well first). 40g per donut. The shiro an provides the Japanese soul. The vanilla diplomat provides the cloud-soft crown. Two flavour layers that are distinct but harmonious.
  4. 4Dust kinako mix generously over the cream rosette. Microplane tonka bean — one single pass across the cream surface. The tonka aroma is powerful: vanilla + almond + cherry + hay. Use it as a whisper, not a flavour. Display at room temperature. Serve within 4 hours.
Chef's Note — Tonka BeanTonka bean is legally restricted in some markets (FDA, USA) due to coumarin content — use vanilla bean or a single scraping of benzoin resin instead if operating in US market. In Australian and most Asian markets it is fully permitted. The flavour contribution is irreplaceable if legally available.

Hero Donut 02 · Family 2 · Base A
Sakura Double Cream
桜生クリーム · Donut #05
Salted cherry blossom cream · sakura glaze · preserved petal · strawberry dust · Spring hero · $9.80
Base A ClassicSpring seasonal
Ingredients (per 6)
Sakura Cream
Double cream, cold200 ml
Mascarpone60 g
Salted cherry blossom petals (shiozuke sakura) — rinsed, dried, finely chopped12 g
Icing sugar20 g
Sakura water (from soaking petals)1 tsp — reserve from rinsing
Sakura Glaze
Icing sugar, sifted200 g
Sakura water + warm water3–4 tbsp total
Natural pink colouring (tiny drop)optional — pale blush only
Finish
Preserved sakura petal (whole, rinsed)1 per donut

Method
  1. 1Sakura petal prep: salted cherry blossom petals (shiozuke sakura) are packed in salt — rinse under cold water, soak 10 min to reduce saltiness, taste. They should be faintly salty-floral, not overwhelming. Dry completely. Chop finely. Reserve the pink water from soaking — this goes into the glaze and cream for colour and aroma.
  2. 2Whip cream + mascarpone + icing sugar + sakura water to firm peaks. Fold in chopped petals. The cream should be barely pink with visible petal flecks. The flavour: floral, faintly savoury, delicately sweet — unlike anything in European pastry tradition.
  3. 3Cut shallow well on top of each donut. Pipe cream in an open rosette (45g). Dip the side and base of the donut in pale pink sakura glaze. Set on rack 5 min. While glaze is still slightly tacky, press one whole preserved sakura petal flat to the donut side — it adheres to the glaze. Dust strawberry powder lightly over cream.

Online: Japanese specialty food suppliers. Store unopened in cool dry place. Once opened, keep refrigerated — lasts 12 months. Available year-round despite being "spring" product.

Uji Matcha White Chocolate
宇治抹茶白チョコ · Donut #09
Ceremonial Uji matcha cream · Valrhona Ivoire ganache core · azuki · gold leaf · $10.50
Base A ClassicGanache set 2hr cold
Ingredients (per 6)
Matcha Diplomat Cream
Whole milk220 ml
Egg yolks3
Caster sugar45 g
Cornflour18 g
Ceremonial grade Uji matcha, sifted2.5 tsp — must be Uji grade
Unsalted butter20 g
Gelatine1.5 sheets
Double cream, cold, whipped soft100 ml
White Chocolate Ganache Core
Valrhona Ivoire 35% white chocolate120 g
Double cream, hot50 ml
Yuzu juice½ tsp — brightens the ganache
Finish
Koshian (smooth azuki paste)1 tsp per donut, on cream
Gold leaf1 small fragment
Matcha glaze (thin, deep green)dipped side + base
Method
  1. 1Make ganache first — pour hot cream over chopped white chocolate, rest 1 min, stir from centre. Add yuzu juice. Mix until smooth. Cool to room temperature, then refrigerate 2 hrs until firm but pipeable.
  2. 2Matcha diplomat: soak gelatine. Sift matcha directly into egg yolk + sugar + cornflour mix to avoid lumps. Temper warm milk in. Cook to 80°C. Add butter + gelatine. Strain through fine mesh. The cream should be vivid jade green — pale matcha cream means cheap matcha was used. Cling on surface. Cool, refrigerate 3 hrs. Fold diplomat-style with whipped cream.
  3. 3Pipe 20g white chocolate ganache into the donut centre via side nozzle. The ganache is the hidden surprise layer — guests find it when they bite in. Cut shallow top well. Pipe matcha diplomat in a high dome (40g). Dip donut sides in deep green matcha glaze.
  4. 4Place a small spoon (½ tsp) of smooth koshian azuki on the cream — not mixed in, placed as a deliberate dark-red contrast dot against the jade cream. Apply gold leaf. The visual: green, white-ganache hidden, red-black azuki dot, gold. Each element earns its place.
Why Uji Matcha SpecificallyUji (宇治) is a region south of Kyoto considered the birthplace of Japanese tea culture. Ceremonial grade Uji matcha has a deep umami, grassy sweetness and vivid colour that culinary grade cannot replicate. The price difference ($35/100g vs $8/100g) is significant but justified — you need less of it, the colour is dramatically better, and guests who know matcha will notice. Those who don't will simply know it tastes different from any matcha they've had before.

Pandan Coconut Diplomat
パンダンココナッツ · Donut #17
Jade pandan diplomat · butterfly pea colour-change glaze · kaffir lime · toasted coconut · $9.80

Pandan Diplomat

Method

  2. 2Soak gelatine. Whisk yolks, sugar, cornflour. Temper pandan coconut milk in. Cook to 80°C until thick and jade green. Off heat: add butter, squeezed gelatine. Stir smooth. Cling on surface. Cool completely. Refrigerate 4 hrs. Fold in whipped cream diplomat-style. The diplomat will be a lighter, airier jade — beautiful against the donut's pale gold.
  3. 3Pipe diplomat into Base B potato donut generously (40g top dome + 15g inside via nozzle). Butterfly pea glaze: steep flowers 10 min, strain deep violet-blue liquid. Mix with icing sugar. At moment of dipping, add the lemon juice — the glaze shifts violet to vivid purple-magenta before your eyes. Dip immediately and set on rack.
  4. 4While glaze is still wet, press large toasted coconut flakes standing upright — they set at natural angles in the glaze. Microplane kaffir lime leaf at the moment of service. The volatile oils evaporate within minutes; this step happens at the table or pass, not during prep.
The Colour Change MomentThe butterfly pea + lemon glaze shift is a service moment — if done in front of guests, it generates an immediate reaction. The chemical is simple: anthocyanins (natural pigment in butterfly pea) change colour in response to pH. Lemon (acid) turns violet → magenta. Baking soda (alkaline) turns it green-blue. The colour change is real food chemistry, not food colouring.

Service Training · Staff Scripts · Customer Retention System10
The NAMA28 Service Standard — Education as Hospitality
Most food retail staff are trained to take orders. NAMA28 staff are trained to teach. Every interaction is an opportunity to leave the guest knowing something they didn't know before — one ingredient, one technique, one cultural story. Guests who are educated buy more, return sooner, and bring friends. The donut sells on taste the first time. It sells on knowledge every time after that. This service standard is the competitive moat.
Script 01 · Counter Welcome
First 10 seconds when guest approaches counter
Staff
"Welcome to NAMA28. Take your time looking at everything. If you'd like to understand the difference between our doughs or know what any filling tastes like — just ask. Today's seasonal is the [F7 donut name] if you haven't tried it."
One pointer maximum. Never list more than one item in the welcome. More than one choice creates decision paralysis and pushes guests toward "just a coffee." The seasonal mention is always the last sentence — it's the hook.
Script 02 · The 4 Dough Explanation
When a guest asks "what's the difference?"
Staff
"We make four different doughs — each one creates a completely different eating experience. Base A is our classic: featherlight, faintly floral from sake lees yeast. Base B is potato-enriched — almost cloud-like, softer than anything you've had. Base C has almond meal — a bit denser, nutty. And Base D is our signature hybrid — we only use it for the seasonal donuts."
When a guest understands the four bases, they don't order "just one." They order one from each category to compare. Average spend increases $15–20 immediately. This explanation is the most valuable 45 seconds in the NAMA28 training programme.
Script 03 · Drink Pairing Upsell
When a donut is ordered without a drink
Staff
"Would you like something to drink? The hojicha latte pairs really well with that one — they're both roasted, so the warmth echoes through the whole bite. Or the yuzu soda if you'd like something cold and sharp."
Two options only — one hot, one cold. Always explain the flavour connection (why they pair). Never ask "would you like a drink?" without a reason — a reason doubles the conversion rate. Target: 80%+ drink attach rate.
Script 04 · Sake Lees Explanation
When a guest asks "what makes your dough different?"
Staff
"We use sake lees as our yeast — it's the pressed solids left over after brewing Japanese rice wine. Instead of commercial dry yeast, these sake lees give the dough a very slow, complex fermentation — which is why the crumb is so fine and why there's a barely-there floral note you can't quite name. Combined with tangzhong — a cooked roux paste — it's why these feel so different from any other donut."
This explanation converts a "nice donut" into a "I need to tell everyone about this" moment. The specificity (sake lees, tangzhong, fermentation) signals craft and legitimacy. Not required on every interaction — read the guest. Use it when someone is genuinely curious.
Script 05 · Sell-Out Management
When a requested item has sold out
Staff
"The [F7 seasonal donut] sold out at about 11:30 today — we limit it to 10 a day so it's fresh and specific. If you'd like to be here early tomorrow, we open at 8am and they'll be ready from opening. We post on Instagram when the seasonal ones are available too, if you follow us."
Never apologise excessively. "Sold out at 11:30" signals demand. "10 a day, fresh and specific" reframes scarcity as a quality choice. The Instagram follow converts a disappointed guest into a future returning customer. Sell-out is marketing. Treat it as such, always.
Script 06 · Gift Box Upsell
When guest is ordering 3+ items or appears to be purchasing for someone else
Staff
"If these are for a gift or you want to take some home beautifully packaged — we do gift boxes from $35. We can mix any combination, and we wrap them in Japanese tissue paper. Very popular for birthdays or just because."
"Just because" removes the special occasion requirement. Gift boxes run at 2–3× the margin of counter sales because the packaging cost is absorbed in the $35 base price and the experience justifies premium pricing. Train staff to spot the gift-purchase signals: hesitation at the counter, asking "which ones would you recommend for someone else," purchasing larger quantities.

  • Guests who understand the 4 bases have a specific reason to return
  • They come back to try the Base they didn't try, or compare donuts across bases

Kitchen Design · 40-Seat Donut Café

Ground Floor — Production Kitchen
  • Commercial deep fryer (15–20L oil capacity, dual basket) — temperature-controlled to ±1°C; one basket per base to avoid cross-contamination of flavour bases
  • 20L planetary mixer — all doughs; keep bowl cold for butter addition
  • Proofing cabinet (temp + humidity control) — consistent final proof regardless of ambient conditions
  • Commercial fridge/cold room — overnight cold retard for all doughs; also holds pre-filled donuts waiting for café service
  • Frying station ventilation — critical; oil vapour from 160°C frying requires strong extraction
  • Pastry prep bench (cool marble surface if possible) — filling piping station
  • Display counter pass-through — direct from kitchen to counter, allows real-time restocking
Upper Floor — Café Seating
  • 40 seats total: 12 counter/bar + 16 shared long table + 12 window seats
  • Commercial espresso machine (2-group) + grinder + hojicha/matcha station
  • Drinks pass: close to seating, not buried in the back
  • Japanese aesthetic: warm timber, dried botanicals, washi paper pendant lights, dark ceramic tableware — the visual supports the product story
  • Show window to street: if possible, make the frying station visible from street level — nothing drives foot traffic like watching donuts being made
  • No reservations policy: simplifies operations, creates line-as-marketing dynamic
Space Requirements
  • Minimum viable: 60 sqm (30 production + 30 café)
  • Ideal: 100–120 sqm (50 production + 50–70 café)
  • Narrow-front, deep floorplan preferred — the "laneway" format maximises street presence
