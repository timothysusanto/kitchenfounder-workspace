1Entrees — Vietnamese Street Starters

Entree philosophy: Vietnamese street food is built on contrast — crispy vs soft, hot vs cool, sour vs sweet, light vs rich. Every entree should deliver that contrast in a single bite. Goi cuon (fresh spring rolls) are the handshake — light, healthy, Instagram-perfect. Cha gio (fried spring rolls) are the hook — crispy, fragrant, impossible to stop eating. Start your guests here and they'll trust everything that follows.

2Mains — Pho, Bun Bo Hue & Banh Mi

Mains philosophy: The Vietnamese mains menu is anchored by two things: the bowl and the baguette. Pho is the soul — 12 hours of bone broth, star anise, cinnamon, the sweetness of charred ginger and onion. The banh mi is the identity — French colonial baguette meets Vietnamese street ingenuity. These two together define your restaurant. Master the broth first. Everything else follows.

Method

1
Pho broth (batch): Roast beef bones at 220°C / 425°F 45 min until dark brown. Char ginger and onion directly on flame. Combine with star anise, cinnamon, clove, cardamom, coriander seed. Simmer 12 hours minimum. Strain, skim, season with fish sauce and rock sugar.
2
To order: soak noodles 30 min in cold water. Blanch in boiling water 30 sec. Drain, place in pre-warmed bowl.
3
Layer brisket slices and raw eye round on noodles. Ladle boiling broth over — the heat cooks the raw beef to medium-rare. Green onion on top.
4
Serve with herb plate: bean sprouts, basil, sawtooth, lime, chilli. Guest builds their own bowl. Table sauces: hoisin and sriracha.
💡 Chef's tip: The broth is everything. It should taste of bones, spice and time. If your broth lacks depth, it hasn't cooked long enough. Clear golden-amber colour means clean bones and careful skimming. Never rush it — make it overnight if needed.

5Kitchen Design — The Pho & Banh Mi Bar Kitchen

Kitchen design philosophy: A Vietnamese bar kitchen is built around two heroes: the broth station (the soup side) and the grill + assembly station (the sandwich and bowl side). The pho stock pot runs 24/7 — it never stops. The grill needs high heat and speed. These two systems define your kitchen layout. Keep them close to the pass, keep the cold assembly station between them.

10Chef's Lens — The Vietnamese Pho & Banh Mi Bar in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Vietnamese Bar in the United States
Vietnamese food is the most underrated cuisine in the American restaurant landscape. Pho is already the fourth most Googled food in the United States. Vietnamese restaurants are consistently among the highest-rated on Yelp in every major city. And yet the category is dominated by family operations that haven't evolved their branding or systems in twenty years. That gap between what Vietnamese food is and what the American market thinks it is — that is your opportunity.

The pho broth is the foundation of everything. If you get the broth right — charred ginger and onion, the bloom of star anise, cinnamon, clove, the long slow extraction from properly roasted bones — you will have regulars within two weeks of opening. People return to great pho the way they return to great coffee. It becomes part of their weekly rhythm.

The banh mi is your growth engine. It's portable, fast to assemble, deeply satisfying, and wildly photogenic. A great banh mi with house-made pickles, a good pate, and a properly crispy baguette is one of the greatest sandwiches in the world. Americans who discover it for the first time are evangelical. They bring their friends. Social media does the rest.

The Vietnamese iced coffee program is your margin engine. Ca phe sua da at 10% food cost, egg coffee at 9.8% — these drinks tell the story of the restaurant while generating exceptional returns. The phin filter dripping at the table is living hospitality. It says: we are not in a hurry, and neither should you be.

The herbs are your brand statement. Every pho bowl should arrive with a generously loaded herb plate — Thai basil, bean sprouts, lime, fresh chilli. That plate of aromatics, brought to the table still cold and crisp, signals quality and care before a single spoon has touched the broth.

One thing: never serve bad pho. The broth tells the guest everything about your standards. A pale, thin, under-seasoned pho is not a bad batch — it is a broken promise. Keep the stock pot running. Taste it every hour. Adjust. Season. This is not optional — it is the whole job.
The broth is the clock. The banh mi is the handshake. The herbs are the invitation. — KitchenFounder
