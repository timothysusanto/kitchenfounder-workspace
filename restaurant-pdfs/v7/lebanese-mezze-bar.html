1Mezze — Lebanese Street Starters

Mezze philosophy: Lebanese mezze is not a starter — it is a way of life. The table should feel abundant: small plates arriving together, sharing encouraged, conversation flowing. Every mezze dish should be made with obsessive care. The hummus must be silky. The fattoush must be crisp. The kibbeh must be golden. Mezze is how Lebanese hospitality says "we are glad you are here." Build your mezze list around these six classics and you will have a table that speaks for itself.

2Mains — Grill, Stew & The Lebanese Table

Mains philosophy: Lebanese mains are anchored by two cooking methods: the charcoal grill and the slow stew. The grill produces kafta, shish tawook, and lamb chops with that irreplaceable smoky char. The stew produces the deeply comforting dishes — slow-braised lamb, chickpea-tomato, rice and vermicelli. Together they cover every craving. Build your mains list to honour both traditions.

Method

5Kitchen Design — The Lebanese Mezze Bar Kitchen

Kitchen design philosophy: A Lebanese mezze kitchen is built around three heroes: the charcoal grill (kafta, shish tawook, lamb chops), the cold mezze station (hummus, labneh, salads, dips), and the fry station (kibbeh, falafel, arayes). These three stations must flow efficiently to a central pass. The cold mezze station should be visible from the dining room if possible — the act of assembling a mezze table is theatre.

6Service Training Module

Service philosophy: Lebanese service is warm, generous and personal. The host culture is deeply embedded — in Lebanon, a guest is treated like royalty. Your FOH team should embody this. They should know every dish by heart, be able to explain the cultural context behind the food, and offer recommendations with genuine enthusiasm. A mezze table is a communal experience — train your team to facilitate sharing, not just take orders.

Opening Scripts

// Welcome + Ahweh offer
"Welcome — please sit down. Can I start you with some Arabic coffee and dates while you look at the menu? It's how we always begin at a Lebanese table."
Arabic coffee as a welcome costs almost nothing and immediately signals authentic hospitality. Sets the tone before any food arrives.
// Mezze explanation for first-timers
"Lebanese mezze is all about sharing — the idea is to order several small plates and put them all in the centre of the table. I'd suggest starting with the hummus, fattoush and kibbeh, and then we can add from there. The kafta and shish tawook from the grill are our most popular mains."
Guides first-time guests through the format without condescension. Gives a clear recommendation they can act on immediately.
// Arak introduction
"Have you had arak before? It's Lebanon's national spirit — made from grapes and anise, it's been made there for over 500 years. It turns milky white when you add ice or water — it's quite a thing to see. We have it on its own or I can make you our Arak Sour cocktail."
Educates and creates curiosity. Guests who try arak for the first time almost always remember it and come back for it.
// Dessert table close
"For dessert — can I bring you some knafeh? It's our warm shredded pastry with sweet cheese and rose water syrup — you should eat it while it's hot. And it pairs perfectly with Arabic coffee."
Creates urgency (eat it hot), provides a pairing, increases dessert + coffee revenue in one sentence.

10Chef's Lens — The Lebanese Mezze Bar in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Lebanese Mezze Bar in the United States
Lebanese food is one of the most beloved cuisines in the world, and one of the most underrepresented in the American restaurant market outside of New York, Los Angeles and a handful of other cities. That is changing rapidly. The hummus revolution that began a decade ago was just the beginning — Americans are now ready for the full Lebanese table.

The opportunity is real. Lebanese food occupies a space that almost no other cuisine can — it is simultaneously exotic and comforting, health-conscious and indulgent, vegetable-forward and meat-centered, ancient and contemporary. A well-executed Lebanese mezze bar can attract health-conscious lunchers at midday and a celebration-dining crowd at dinner. Few cuisines cover both with equal conviction.

The hummus is your front door. If your hummus is silky, pale, fragrant and served with a pool of the best olive oil you can afford, every guest who tastes it will trust everything else that follows. If your hummus is thick, grey and underseasoned, nothing else on the menu will rescue the meal. Make the hummus your obsession. Blend it for five minutes. Use ice water. Use the best tahini. Never stop improving it.

The charcoal grill is your soul. Kafta and shish tawook cooked over real charcoal have a smoky, caramelised quality that cannot be replicated on a gas grill. If you are serious about Lebanese food, invest in charcoal. The ventilation is expensive and the maintenance is demanding — but the flavour difference will be the most consistent piece of positive feedback you receive from guests every single day.

The hospitality is your identity. Lebanese culture is one of the most hospitable in the world — guests are fed until they cannot move, offered more, offered again, and sent home with something extra. You cannot fully replicate this in a commercial setting, but you can gesture toward it: Arabic coffee on arrival, generous portions, a complimentary piece of baklava with the bill. These gestures cost almost nothing and create loyalty that no marketing budget can buy.

One thing: never put bad hummus on the table. It signals everything about your standards. Make it right, make it daily, make it with pride.
The hummus is the front door. The grill is the soul. The hospitality is the identity. — KitchenFounder
