*The Dim Sum & Yum Cha Bible

What is Yum Cha? "Yum cha" (飲茶) means "to drink tea" in Cantonese — the full ritual of ordering tea and eating small dishes (dim sum) with family and friends, traditionally on Sunday mornings. "Dim sum" (點心) means "touch the heart" — small bites designed to delight rather than fill. The tradition originated in teahouses along the Silk Road where travellers would rest and eat small snacks with tea. In Cantonese culture, yum cha is a social institution as significant as Sunday dinner in an Italian family. Your teahouse does not just serve food — it hosts a weekly ritual.
The Cantonese Pantry: Aromatics: Ginger (ginger + prawn filling is non-negotiable), garlic, green onion, sesame oil (finish, not cook), Shaoxing wine (rice wine for marinades).
Sauces: Oyster sauce (the Cantonese umami backbone), light soy (seasoning), dark soy (colour + sweetness), hoisin sauce (char siu), chilli oil (condiment, always on table), XO sauce (premium condiment — dried seafood + chilli).
Proteins: Pork (siu mai filling), prawns/shrimp (har gow filling — must be fresh, not frozen), chicken (cheung fun + congee), char siu (BBQ pork — make in-house or source from Chinatown BBQ supplier).
Starches: Wheat starch + tapioca starch (har gow dough — creates translucent wrapper), rice flour (cheung fun rice rolls), glutinous rice (lo mai gai — lotus leaf sticky rice).
Specialty items: Bamboo steamers × 30+ minimum; dim sum pushcarts (optional but authentic for larger venues); bamboo leaves (lo mai gai wrap); dried shrimp (lo bak go turnip cake); Chinese sausage (lap cheong).
Tea programme: Jasmine (most popular), pu-erh (earthy, aids digestion — traditional pairing), chrysanthemum (floral, caffeine-free), longjing/Dragon Well (green, premium), oolong (aged, complex). The tea is not an afterthought — it is the centrepiece of the experience.
Steamer Science: Dim sum is almost entirely steam-cooked. The steamer is your most important piece of equipment. Bamboo steamers (not metal) are traditional and create a slightly absorbent steam environment that prevents water droplets from falling onto the dim sum surface. Stack up to 3 layers per wok. Each steamer basket holds approximately 4–6 pieces. Average dim sum service: 2–3 steamers ordered per person in a group. A table of 4 orders 8–12 baskets. The service rhythm is continuous refill of steamers and tea — not a single order taken at the start. This is the heart of the dim sum service model.

1Starters — The Yum Cha Opening Baskets

Starter philosophy: In a traditional dim sum service, there are no "starters" in the Western sense — dishes arrive continuously throughout the meal. However, for a modern dim sum teahouse targeting the US market, a curated opening flight of the most iconic pieces serves as both an introduction to the cuisine and an immediate visual statement. These six items represent the essential Cantonese dim sum canon. If you can make these six correctly, you can make everything else.

SHELLFISHGF (wheat starch — no gluten)

Method — The Benchmark Technique

1
The wrapper: Mix wheat starch and tapioca starch. Pour in boiling water all at once, stir quickly to form a dough. The dough must be worked while still very hot — use a folding motion rather than kneading. Wrap in plastic and rest 5 minutes. Divide into small balls (10g each). Flatten with the flat side of a Chinese cleaver — the specific technique creates the smooth, slightly shiny surface of a proper har gow wrapper. Never use a rolling pin — the cleaver technique creates the right thinness at the edges and thickness in the centre.
2
The filling: Mix chopped and whole prawn pieces (the combination creates texture — uniform chop is flat). Season with ginger, sesame oil, white pepper, light soy, sugar. The filling should taste slightly sweet, fresh, and clean — not heavily seasoned. The prawn flavour is the point. The pleating: Fold the wrapper in half over the filling, then create 7–9 pleats on one side only (the "fan" shape is the har gow trademark). Steam over boiling water in a bamboo steamer, lined with perforated baking paper or oiled steamer cloth, for 6–7 minutes until the wrappers become translucent.
💡 Har gow is the dim sum dish that dim sum chefs are judged on. A traditional evaluation checklist: wrapper thin and translucent (not gummy), 7+ pleats on one side, filling plump and visible through the wrapper, prawn bright pink not grey, wrapper not sticking to steamer. A skilled har gow maker can pleat 10 pieces per minute. Your dim sum chef's speed on har gow determines your kitchen throughput capacity. Audit this specifically during the interview.

Method

2Mains — The Dim Sum Table

Mains philosophy: Dim sum "mains" are the heartier dishes that anchor the table — the lo mai gai in lotus leaf, the turnip cake on the griddle, the congee that warms the soul, the char siu that defines Cantonese BBQ. Method — The Glaze is Everything

1
Marinate pork strips minimum 24 hours (48 hours preferred). The maltose/honey combination creates the distinctive sticky, slightly lacquered glaze. If you can only make one Cantonese BBQ item in-house, it should be this.

GLUTENDAIRYEGGS

Method — Two Styles

1
Hong Kong style (puff pastry shell): Use a lard-and-butter water dough laminated with a fat paste — the Hong Kong egg tart shell is distinctly flaky, with visible layers, and shatters when bitten. Macanese/Portuguese style (shortcrust shell): Dense, buttery, crumbly. Both are legitimate. The HK style is more theatrical; the Macanese style is more forgiving to make. Custard: strain egg + sugar + evaporated milk + fresh milk + vanilla. Pour into blind-baked shells at 160°C until just set with a slight wobble in the centre. The custard should be smooth as silk, pale golden, and fragrant with vanilla. A properly set egg tart wobbles in the centre and is opaque rather than glassy — it still has a slight tremor when the oven is opened, and sets firm as it cools.
💡 Egg tarts are the most-ordered dessert at every Cantonese restaurant in the US. The HK egg tart, when made correctly, is one of the most elegant and precise pastry items in any Asian cuisine. Your egg tarts should be warm at service — the combination of a just-warm flaky shell and a silky, fragrant custard is irreplaceable. At $5.99 for 3 with 6.7% FC, egg tarts are your highest-margin dessert and your highest-volume dessert.

Premium single-origin teas ($8.99–$14.99) are your highest-margin beverage category. Your tea list should have at least 8 varieties with brief tasting notes for each.

Dim Sum Folding Station

  • Dedicated dim sum prep table — separate from hot production
  • Cool environment (air-conditioned or separate cool area) — dough dries out at high temperature
  • 1 dim sum chef can produce 200-250 pieces/hour at full speed
  • Har gow pleating benchmark: 7+ pleats in under 30 seconds per piece
  • Pre-portioned filling trays — weighed portions prevent size inconsistency
  • Parchment paper squares pre-cut for steamer lining
Cool workspace | Pre-portion scale | Bamboo steamer × 30 | Cleaver for har gow

High-BTU Wok Station

  • Minimum 100,000 BTU per burner — the wok hei flavour requires this heat
  • Wok hei is the Cantonese "breath of the wok" — the slight char, caramelised edges, and smoky aroma only achievable at extreme heat
  • Never use a domestic cooktop for fried rice or stir-fry in a commercial setting
  • Wok ladle + wok spatula always together — tossing motion is the technique
  • Cold rice for fried rice in the walk-in: day-old rice only, never freshly cooked
100k BTU wok range × 2 | Wok ladle × 2 | Cold rice supply

Tea Service Station

  • Hot water urn at 95°C (not boiling — boiling water scalds green and white teas)
  • Tea selection display: loose leaf in labeled jars with tasting notes on display cards
  • Per-table teapots (ceramic preferred — heat retention) + tea strainers
  • Gongfu ceremony set: small clay pot + 6 small cups + brewing board
  • Refill policy: unlimited hot water for the base tea price — this is the tradition
95°C urn | Tea jar display | Ceramic teapots × 20 | Gongfu set × 2

Char Siu Roasting

  • Dedicated BBQ oven with rack and drip tray for char siu
  • The char siu aroma from the oven fills the dining room — this is a feature, not a problem
  • Baste every 10 minutes during final 30 minutes for glaze build-up
  • Rest char siu 5-10 minutes before slicing — juices redistribute
  • Char siu strips in walk-in ready for bao, cheung fun, lo mai gai throughout service
BBQ roasting oven | Basting brush | Rack + drip tray | Slicing board

6Service Training — The Yum Cha Table Scripts

Service philosophy: Yum cha service is circular and informal by Cantonese tradition — not a linear three-course Western meal. Guests order continuously, dishes arrive as they are ready, and the tea is refilled indefinitely. For a Western market teahouse, the key is to maintain the warmth and informality of the yum cha ritual while providing enough structure for guests who are unfamiliar with the format. The service briefing should always begin with tea — not menus, not specials, not the wifi password. Tea first.
// Opening Script — Table Arrival
"Welcome — we start with tea. What do you like? We have pu-erh, which is the traditional dim sum pairing — earthy, rich, helps with digestion. Jasmine if you want something floral and light. Or Dragon Well green tea from Hangzhou if you want to try something special. Once you've chosen your tea, the menu is just for guidance — you're welcome to order as much or as little as you like, whenever you like. More baskets arrive throughout the meal. There's no rush here."
Leading with the tea question before the food menu resets the guest's expectation from "Western restaurant" to "teahouse." The "no rush" reassurance signals the yum cha service model without requiring a lengthy explanation. The tea recommendation names specific varieties with brief descriptions — educating without overwhelming.
// Har Gow Introduction Script
"The har gow is our dim sum chef's signature piece — it's the dish every dim sum chef in Cantonese cuisine is specifically trained and judged on. The wrapper is made from wheat starch and tapioca — no gluten — which is why it's translucent when steamed. You should be able to see the prawn inside. There should be at least seven pleats on one side — that's the technical standard. Go ahead and check. The filling is just prawn, bamboo shoot, ginger, and sesame. Nothing else. The wrapper is the skill."
The invitation to "check the pleats" turns a food explanation into a participatory experience. Guests will count the pleats, show their table companions, take a photo. This creates engagement and a personal story attached to the dish. The "nothing else" explanation of the simple filling signals confidence in technique over sauce complexity.
// Tea Refill Script
"Traditionally, when you want more hot water, you leave the teapot lid slightly ajar — that's the signal in Cantonese teahouses that you need a refill. But we'll also come by. If you want to order a different tea for the second pot, the pu-erh and oolong get better with more steepings — the flavour deepens each time."
Teaching the teapot lid signal gives guests a cultural piece of knowledge they will use for the rest of their lives. The upsell to "different tea for the second pot" is gentle and educational. The note about pu-erh improving with steeping increases the perceived value of the tea and encourages second tea orders.
// Egg Tart Close Script (at dessert)
"Before you wrap up — the egg tarts just came out of the oven. They're best warm, within about fifteen minutes. The custard is still slightly trembling in the centre. It's the version they make in Hong Kong — flaky pastry shell, not the shortcrust Portuguese version. $5.99 for three. Three people, one each."
"Just came out of the oven" creates urgency. "Fifteen minutes" creates scarcity. "Custard still trembling" is a sensory description that creates anticipation. The specific note about HK vs Portuguese style signals culinary knowledge. The three-for-three table math closes the table softly without pressure.

8Customer Retention System

Dim sum teahouses build the deepest community loyalty of any restaurant concept. The yum cha ritual is a weekly family gathering — once a family adopts a teahouse as "their place," they return every Sunday, bring extended family, celebrate birthdays, host New Year dinners. Building that loyalty requires consistency above all things: consistent har gow quality, consistent tea temperature, consistent service warmth. One bad visit breaks the bond. Ten consecutive perfect visits cement it for years.
// WeChat / SMS Template — Post-Visit
"Dear [Name], thank you for joining us for yum cha. We hope the har gow was everything you expected. Chinese New Year falls on [date] this year — our special menu is already filling up. Priority booking for returning guests opens one week early. Would you like us to reserve your table? 茶飯 [Restaurant Name]"
The "priority booking for returning guests" creates exclusivity and drives re-engagement. Chinese New Year is the highest-revenue single event of the year for any Chinese restaurant in the US — early booking drive maximises captures. The 茶飯 (tea and rice) closing signature is a Cantonese term of warmth equivalent to "bon appétit" — culturally resonant for the Cantonese diaspora and educational for non-Cantonese guests.
// Chinese New Year Feast Programme
SMS to all guests (first week of January): "新年快樂 Happy New Year 🧧 Chinese New Year falls on [date]. We're hosting a traditional Reunion Dinner on New Year's Eve — whole steamed fish (nian nian you yu — abundance every year), longevity noodles, turnip cake for prosperity, tang yuan for family togetherness. $88/person, table of 8. The specific food symbolism (fish = abundance, noodles = longevity, turnip cake = prosperity, tang yuan = family togetherness) turns the reservation invitation into a cultural education. The timing details (har gow from first table, egg tarts at 11am, roasting oven aroma) make the Sunday visit feel like a live event rather than just a restaurant opening. The tea choice gives the reader a micro-decision that increases mental commitment to attending. Posting Saturday evening captures the audience planning their Sunday morning.

// Chef's Lens — Opening a Dim Sum Teahouse Outside the Cantonese Diaspora
Dim sum is one of the most technically demanding restaurant concepts in the world. The har gow wrapper requires a specific non-gluten dough worked at a specific temperature in a specific way. The cheung fun rice sheet must be steamed to the exact moment when it is set but still pliable enough to roll. The bao dough requires the specific low-protein Hong Kong flour to achieve the characteristic white pillow texture. These are not approximations — they are precise techniques that take years of practice to master. The first question to answer before opening is not about location or funding. It is: do you have a dim sum chef who has made har gow? Not someone who can follow a recipe and try. Someone who has made har gow many hundreds of times. The gap between a first-attempt har gow (thick wrapper, uneven pleats, filling leaking) and a professional har gow (translucent wrapper, seven clean pleats, filling visible but contained) is a year of daily practice. That gap is visible immediately to anyone who has eaten dim sum in Hong Kong, in San Gabriel Valley, in Flushing. They will not return. If you cannot find a trained dim sum chef in your market, consider a smaller teahouse concept with bao, cheung fun, congee, and lo mai gai as the core items — and purchase your har gow and siu mai from a reputable Asian wholesale supplier while you build the in-house skill. Be honest with your menu: "Our har gow is made in collaboration with [supplier name], a traditional Hong Kong dim sum kitchen." That honesty builds trust. The alternative — poor quality har gow you pretend is house-made — destroys it. But when you do get it right — when the har gow wrapper is translucent and the prawn is visible through it, and when a Sunday morning table of eight Chinese grandmothers orders three rounds without comment because the comment IS the silence, because silence from a Cantonese grandmother who has eaten har gow every Sunday of her life means it is acceptable — then you have built something that will outlast you. Yum cha is not a restaurant format. It is a weekly family ritual that families entrust to you to host. That trust, once earned, is Sunday after Sunday for the rest of your business life. 茶飯 (Tea and rice.)
— The har gow pleats take a year. That's the point.
