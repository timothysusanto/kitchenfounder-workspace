*The Poke Bible & Hawaiian Pantry

What is poke? Poke (pronounced poh-KAY) is a Hawaiian dish of raw, cubed fish — traditionally ahi tuna — marinated in soy sauce, sesame oil, and sea salt, mixed with limu (seaweed) and inamona (roasted kukui nut paste). It has been eaten by Native Hawaiians for centuries, originally as a way to use every part of the fish caught that day. The word "poke" in Hawaiian means simply "to slice" or "to cut crosswise." Modern poke bars evolved from Hawaiian plate lunch culture, absorbed Japanese immigrant influences (shoyu, sesame, rice bowls), and exploded nationally in the 2010s into one of the most prolific fast-casual formats in the US.
The Hawaiian Pantry — Non-Negotiables:

Sushi-grade ahi tuna (yellowfin): The cornerstone of every poke bar. "Sushi-grade" is a marketing term, not a USDA designation — what it means in practice is: previously frozen at -20°C (-4°F) for 7+ days (which kills parasites), then thawed under refrigeration, handled with strict cold-chain discipline, and sourced from a licensed sushi-grade supplier. Never use fresh, never-frozen tuna for raw consumption. Your supplier's parasite-control documentation must be on file. This is a legal and food safety requirement, not a preference.

Shoyu (Hawaiian soy sauce): Aloha Brand shoyu is the authentic choice — slightly sweeter and less salty than Japanese shoyu, with a rounder flavour. In a restaurant context, Kikkoman regular soy sauce is an acceptable substitute but should be slightly diluted with mirin to approximate the sweetness of Hawaiian shoyu.

Sesame oil (toasted): Used in small quantities — 3-5ml per serving. Toasted sesame oil is intensely aromatic and becomes unpleasant when overused. It should be a background note in the marinade, not the dominant flavour. Korean or Japanese brands (Kadoya, Ottogi) are reliably consistent.

Limu seaweed: Traditional Hawaiian poke uses limu kohu (a native red seaweed) or limu wawae'iole. In the US mainland, dried wakame (Japanese seaweed) or agar seaweed is the practical substitute — rehydrated, it provides the oceanic, slightly mineral flavour of traditional limu.

Inamona (kukui nut paste): Roasted and salted kukui nut, ground to a paste — used in traditional Hawaiian poke as a seasoning. The kukui nut (candlenut) has a slightly bitter, oily flavour that adds depth. Source from Hawaiian specialty suppliers or substitute with a small amount of toasted macadamia nut paste blended with sea salt.

Sushi rice: Short-grain Japanese rice (Koshihikari or Calrose variety), cooked and seasoned with rice vinegar, sugar, and salt. The rice must be at room temperature — never hot (melts the fish proteins) and never cold (becomes hard and unpleasant). A poke bowl with poorly made rice is a failed poke bowl regardless of the fish quality.

Avocado: Hass variety, perfectly ripe — soft to pressure but not mushy. An under-ripe avocado in a poke bowl is a texture problem that undermines the entire dish. Order 3-4 days ahead of service and ripen at room temperature. Never refrigerate uncut avocados — cold stops the ripening process.

Macadamia nuts: Hawaiian macadamias — toasted and roughly chopped. The richest, most buttery nut in the world, and a genuinely Hawaiian flavour. Used as a garnish and a textural element. Never skip — macadamias are one of the few ingredients that signal authenticity to guests who know Hawaiian food.

Nori (roasted seaweed): Cut into fine julienne strips or torn into small pieces as a garnish. Adds umami depth and visual contrast. Must be stored airtight — nori absorbs moisture rapidly and becomes chewy and stale within hours of opening if not stored correctly.
The Poke Bowl Format — Build Logic: Every poke bowl is assembled in layers, and the order of assembly is both practical and aesthetic:

1. Base — sushi rice (most popular), mixed greens, brown rice, or cauliflower rice (keto)
2. Protein — ahi tuna, salmon, octopus, tofu, shrimp, scallop
3. Toppings — avocado, edamame, cucumber, mango, pickled ginger, pickled daikon, wakame, corn
4. Crunch — crispy shallots, toasted macadamia, tempura flakes, furikake
5. Sauce — spicy aioli, ponzu, shoyu sesame, yuzu kosho, mango habanero
6. Garnish — nori strips, sesame seeds, microgreens, lime wedge

The assembly must be done cold, quickly, and served immediately. Poke is a live product — the moment the fish hits warm rice it begins cooking. Bowl service from refrigerated fish to guest in under 90 seconds.

Method — The 10-Second Sear

1
Press sushi-grade ahi block firmly into sesame seed mix on all sides — the seeds form a crust. Heat a cast iron pan or heavy-based skillet until smoking. Add a small amount of neutral oil. Method — The Lomi Technique

1
Salt cure (overnight): Cover salmon fillet generously with Hawaiian sea salt — about 40g per 200g fish. Refrigerate 8-12 hours. The salt draws out moisture and partially cures the flesh, firming it and intensifying the flavour. Rinse well, pat dry, dice into small 1cm cubes. The cured salmon should be slightly firmer than raw salmon, with a concentrated, deeply savoury flavour.
2
"Lomi lomi" means "to massage" in Hawaiian — the original dish was made by massaging the salt-cured salmon with tomato and onion by hand, breaking down the ingredients slightly and mixing the flavours together. In a restaurant context: gently combine diced cured salmon, tomato, sweet onion, and green onion. Season sparingly with additional salt — the salmon is already salty from the cure.
3
Serve in a chilled bowl on a bed of crushed ice — the cold is part of the dish's identity. Garnish with taro chips for crunch. The dish should be served cold enough that the bowl feels cold to the touch.

Method — Kalua Pork (Batch Cook)

1
Kalua pork (cook day before): Traditional kalua pork is cooked in an imu — an underground oven with hot lava rocks. Restaurant version: rub pork shoulder generously with Hawaiian sea salt and liquid smoke (alaea red salt is traditional if available). Wrap tightly in ti leaves or banana leaves if available — this steams the pork and adds a faint vegetal note. Wrap in foil. Method — Bowl Assembly Protocol

1
The marinade (batch): Combine 200ml shoyu (Hawaiian-style soy), 30ml toasted sesame oil, 3 sliced green onions, 20g sesame seeds, 10ml mirin. This is your base shoyu marinade — made fresh daily. Toss diced ahi in marinade maximum 5 minutes before service (not hours ahead — over-marinating makes the tuna mushy and "cooked" by the soy salt).
2
Bowl assembly order (fast, cold, precise): Start with sushi rice as the base — spread evenly. Working clockwise: avocado slices, edamame, cucumber, wakame, pickled ginger. The tuna goes in the centre last — it is the hero and should have the most visual prominence. Scatter crispy shallots over everything. Lay nori strips across the top. Sesame seeds scattered. Drizzle of spicy aioli in a thin zigzag pattern — never a blob.
3
The bowl must be served cold — every component except the rice should be refrigerator-cold at assembly. The rice should be room temperature (cold rice is hard and unpleasant; hot rice "cooks" the tuna). Total assembly time from refrigerator to guest: under 90 seconds. Speed and cold temperature are both food safety and quality requirements.
💡 The most common poke bowl mistake: over-marinating the tuna. Marinating ahi in shoyu for more than 15 minutes begins the salt-denaturation process — the proteins start to firm and "cook," turning the tuna from silky and tender to chewy and grey at the edges. Toss tuna in marinade fresh for each order, or marinate batch quantities no more than 10 minutes before service begins. Your fish supplier cannot fix over-marinated tuna — only your timing can.

Method — The Shave Ice Ritual

1
Equipment required: A proper shave ice machine — not a blender or ice crusher. Hawaiian shave ice is made with a rotating blade that shaves block ice into feather-thin, snow-like ribbons. The texture is fundamentally different from crushed ice — it is soft, light, and absorbs syrup evenly rather than having syrup pool at the bottom. Invest in a quality shave ice machine (Hatsuyuki or Gold Medal brands are industry standard). This is the non-negotiable equipment for this dessert.
2
House syrups (batch): Do not use artificial commercial syrups — the flavour is inferior and guests who have eaten real shave ice in Hawaii will notice immediately. Make house syrups by simmering fruit juice or purée (lilikoi, guava, strawberry, coconut) with equal weight of sugar until dissolved. Cool. Store refrigerated up to 2 weeks. The difference between house-made and commercial syrup is the entire quality proposition of your shave ice.
3
Place azuki bean paste at the base of the bowl (hidden under the ice — guests discover it as they eat down). Mound shaved ice high above the bowl rim — Hawaiian shave ice always overflows the container significantly. Pour syrups from top in stripes or zones — the colour gradient as syrups meet is the visual signature. Drizzle condensed milk over the top. Add mochi balls. Serve with a flat spoon and a wide straw.
💡 Matsumoto's Shave Ice in Haleiwa has had a queue down the block since 1951. The secret: real house-made syrups, proper shave ice machine texture, and the hidden azuki bean surprise at the base. Position the condensed milk and azuki as "the Hawaiian way" — guests who don't know about them will feel they've learned something, which creates a memory and a return visit.

EQUIPMENT LIST:
● Refrigerated cold rail (bain marie, 3m) — most critical piece of equipment
● 3x Commercial rice cookers (10-cup Zojirushi or Tiger) — staggered batches
● Shave ice machine (Hatsuyuki or Gold Medal) — block ice only, not cube
● Plancha/flat grill (60x40cm) — for hot proteins
● Fryer (2-basket) — tofu, malasadas, crispy shallots
● Walk-in refrigerator — fish must be stored separately from other proteins
● Food thermometer — mandatory, check fish temp every 2 hours

❄️ Cold Assembly Station — The Poke Line

  • Refrigerated cold rail maintained at 0–4°C at all times during service
  • Each ingredient in labelled stainless steel inserts — change inserts every 2 hours
  • Fish inserts held on top of crushed ice inside the rail — additional layer of cold
  • Assembly order trained and standardised: base → protein → toppings → crunch → sauce
  • Bowl assembly target: 90 seconds from order to counter
CRITICAL: Cold rail is your food safety line. Never let fish exceed 4°C. Check with probe thermometer every 2 hours.

🍚 Rice Station

  • Three commercial rice cookers running staggered 30-minute batches
  • Sushi rice must rest 20 minutes after cooking before service — too hot damages fish
  • Season with sushi vinegar immediately after cooking, fold gently, fan to cool
  • Target temperature for serving: 18-22°C (room temperature, not cold, not hot)
  • Never refrigerate sushi rice — cold hardens the starch irreversibly

🐟 Fish Handling Protocol

  • All fish received and checked by head chef personally — inspect colour, smell, firmness
  • Separate cutting board (blue) for fish only — never shared with other proteins
  • Fish portioned cold, kept on crushed ice in cold rail inserts at all times
  • Tuna diced maximum 2 hours before service — longer makes it mushy
  • Daily fish log: supplier, delivery time, temp on arrival, use-by date

🧃 Drinks + Shave Ice Station

  • Shave ice machine: block ice, not cubed — must be maintained correctly for correct texture
  • House syrups: 6-8 flavours minimum (lilikoi, guava, coconut, strawberry, mango, blue coconut)
  • Fresh juices pressed daily: POG, pineapple, coconut water
  • Bar station separate from food assembly to avoid cross-contamination
  • Cocktail prep: batched syrups, fresh citrus squeezed daily

6Service Scripts — Aloha Hospitality

Script philosophy: Hawaiian hospitality culture is built on aloha — genuine warmth, welcome, and care. The service style at a poke bar should feel like being welcomed into someone's home, not processed through a fast-food line. Even in a quick-service format, the aloha energy is the differentiator.
OPENING SCRIPT — Welcome to the Poke Bar
"Aloha! Welcome in — I'll walk you through how we work. You pick your base: sushi rice, mixed greens, brown rice, or half and half. Then your protein — our ahi is the classic, or if you want something really special, the Rainbow Bowl has all five proteins at once. Then toppings and sauce — I'll build it for you or you can customize. First time here?" [If yes: "Okay, start with the Classic Ahi — it's what most people order and it's what we're known for."]
Why this works: Poke bars can be intimidating for first-timers who don't know the format. Immediate guidance reduces decision anxiety, increases confidence, and almost always results in a higher spend because the guest trusts the recommendation.
RAINBOW BOWL UPSELL SCRIPT
"Can I mention the Rainbow Bowl? It's our showpiece — ahi, salmon, octopus, scallop, and Wagyu beef all in one bowl with five different sauces. It's $29.99 but it's genuinely the best way to try everything we do. A lot of people order it on their second visit once they've found out which proteins they love — you can skip that learning curve."
Why this works: Pre-empts the "I'll come back and try the other ones" response. Presents the premium item as a shortcut, not an upsell. The phrase "skip that learning curve" is particularly effective — it reframes the higher price as an efficiency.
FISH FRESHNESS SCRIPT — The Trust Builder
"Quick note on our fish — everything is sushi-grade, which means it was frozen at -20°C to eliminate any parasites, then thawed fresh this morning. We follow the same protocols as a Japanese sushi restaurant. The tuna and salmon are from [supplier name] who delivers to us and to [well-known local sushi restaurant] in the neighbourhood. Freshness is the only thing we won't compromise on."
Why this works: Proactively addressing the "is the raw fish safe?" concern builds trust and differentiates you from competitors. Mentioning a well-known local sushi restaurant as a shared supplier is powerful third-party credibility. Guests who hear this script are significantly more likely to order raw fish rather than defaulting to a cooked option.
MAI TAI SCRIPT — The Story Sell
"For drinks — have you tried a Mai Tai made properly? It was invented in 1944 in Oakland, California, by a guy called Trader Vic, for two friends who'd just come from Tahiti. He gave it to them and they said 'Mai Tai — Roa Ae' — Tahitian for 'out of this world.' That's why it's called the Mai Tai. Ours has aged rum, fresh lime, orange curaçao, orgeat almond syrup, and a dark rum float. It's $14.99 and it's worth every cent."
Why this works: The 1944 origin story is genuinely interesting and takes 20 seconds to tell. Guests who learn it always order the cocktail rather than just a beer. The detail ("out of this world" in Tahitian) is the memorable anchor that makes the story stick.

11The Chef's Lens — A Michelin Perspective on the Hawaiian Poke Bar

Essay — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder

The Hawaiian poke bar is, in my view, the most important restaurant format of the last decade in the United States. Not because of fine dining innovation, molecular gastronomy, or chef celebrity — but because it solved a problem that every working person faces at lunchtime: how do I eat something genuinely good, genuinely healthy, genuinely interesting, in under 10 minutes, for under $20? The poke bar answered all four simultaneously. No other format has managed that combination as cleanly.

The origin of poke is worth understanding properly, because it changes how you build the restaurant. This is not a trend invented in San Francisco in 2015. This is ancient Hawaiian fishing culture — the practice of fishermen eating the freshest part of their catch immediately on the boat, seasoned simply with sea salt and limu seaweed. When Japanese sugar cane workers arrived in Hawaii in the 1860s and 1870s, they brought shoyu, sesame oil, and rice. The two food cultures merged over generations into what we now call poke. What you are serving in a US poke bar is the product of 150 years of cultural exchange, not a trend that will pass.

The critical insight for an operator is this: the poke bowl is deceptively simple. A bowl of rice, some fish, some vegetables. But there is no kitchen that reveals the quality of its ingredients more nakedly than a poke bar. There is nowhere for a low-grade tuna to hide. There is no sauce that masks an under-ripe avocado, no cooking process that salvages a mushy scallop. Everything is served cold, raw, and exposed. Your food cost is your food quality, which is your reputation, which is your business. This is not a format where you cut corners on the fish. The fish is the product. The rice is the frame. The sauces and toppings are the details. In that order of importance, in that order of investment.

The shave ice is the detail that most mainland US operators miss or shortchange. A snowcone machine producing crushed ice with commercial neon syrup is not shave ice. It is a counterfeit. Real Hawaiian shave ice — razor-thin, feather-light, absorbing house-made fruit syrups evenly throughout — is a genuinely different eating experience. Guests who have been to Hawaii know the difference immediately. Guests who haven't experienced it will discover it with you. The shave ice machine is a $2,000-4,000 investment that pays for itself in the story it tells about your commitment to the real thing.

Here is what the US market is missing right now, and why this guide exists: most poke bars in the continental US have commoditised the format. Online ordering, pre-packed bowls, frozen fish, commercial sauces. They have taken a format built on freshness and authenticity and optimised it for throughput and cost savings. The result is a product that is nutritionally fine and experientially forgettable. The opportunity is to do the opposite — to build a poke bar that feels like Hawaii, that treats the fish with the respect it deserves, that makes the shave ice properly, that tells the story of Giovanni's garlic shrimp truck and Matsumoto's shave ice and the Polynesian fishermen who created poke in the first place. That bar, in almost any US city, has no competition.

Fresh fish, correct temperature, the right story — everything else follows. — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder
