✦Argentine Beef Cut Bible — What Your Team Must Know

Why cut knowledge is mandatory: Argentine asado uses cuts that most American diners and many American chefs do not know by name. Tira de asado (cross-cut short ribs — the iconic Argentine rib presentation) is not the same as American-style short ribs. Entrañas (skirt steak) are not the same as fajita meat despite the same cut. Mollejas (sweetbreads) are the most prized offal in Argentine culture and must be presented with confidence. Every server in your restaurant must be able to explain every cut on the menu to a curious guest without hesitation.
Tira de Asado
Cross-cut beef ribs — thin, bone-in slices cut across the ribs. The most Argentine cut in existence. Long, slow grill over low fire. Deeply caramelised, fatty, unmistakable.
Entrañas
Skirt steak (diaphragm muscle). The most flavourful cut on the animal. High heat, fast cook, served medium-rare only. Never well done — it becomes tough and fibrous.
Bife de Chorizo
Strip loin — thick, bone-in. The Argentine version of a NY Strip. Not the same as Spanish chorizo (zero relation). Served rare to medium-rare, never sauced.
Lomo
Beef tenderloin. The most tender cut. Less flavour than bife de chorizo but prized for its texture. Lomo al trapo = wrapped in chimichurri-soaked cloth and placed in the coals.
Mollejas
Sweetbreads (thymus gland of young beef). The most prized offal — tender, creamy, slightly crispy when grilled. A test of a parrilla's seriousness.
Chinchulines
Small intestine. Washed, braided, slow-grilled until crispy outside. Deeply savoury, slightly funky, completely unique. Only for adventurous diners — never force-sell.
Morcilla
Blood sausage — spiced with cumin, paprika, oregano. Grilled until the casing is tight and slightly charred. Sliced at the table. Rich, iron-forward, complex.
Cordero Patagónico
Patagonian lamb — whole or half, butterflied on a cross and slow-roasted over open fire. The most dramatic presentation in Argentine cooking.
Provoleta
A whole disk of semi-aged provolone grilled directly on the parrilla grate until the exterior chars and the interior melts. Topped with dried oregano + chilli + olive oil.

1Starters — Empanadas, Provoleta & Parrilla Bites

Starter philosophy: Argentine starters are designed to arrive while the asado is cooking — because the asado takes time. In Argentina, a proper asado does not begin for at least 90 minutes after guests arrive. The empanadas, chorizo, morcilla, and provoleta serve as both hospitality and entertainment while the main event is being prepared. In a restaurant setting, your starters are doing the same job: managing the expectation that the main grill items take time and are worth waiting for. Never rush the parrilla.

Method — The Repulgue Fold

1
The filling (picadillo): sauté diced onion in beef fat until soft, add spiced beef mince, cook until coloured. Cool completely. Add sliced green olive and hard-boiled egg only when the meat is cold — never fill empanadas with hot filling. Overfilling causes burst seams during baking.
2
The repulgue (folded edge): this is the signature Argentine empanada seal — a twisted, rope-like edge fold that seals the pastry and is the visual identifier of a handmade empanada. Train every prep chef on the repulgue: press edges together, fold the back edge over the front in a series of 12-14 small tucks working around the curve. This takes 3-4 weeks of practice to master. A machine-sealed flat edge is acceptable but signals industrial production.
3
Bake at 200°C / 390°F for 18-20 min until deep golden with slightly darker crimped edges. Egg wash gives the surface a deep, lacquered colour. Serve immediately — empanadas held more than 15 minutes after baking lose the flaky-crisp exterior that makes them exceptional.
💡 The Michelin detail: Empanadas are Argentina's most universally eaten food — every region has its own version, distinguished by filling, fold type, and whether they are baked or fried. In Salta province, the empanadas are small, spicy, and fried. In Mendoza, they are large, baked, and seasoned with more cumin. In Buenos Aires, the standard is the baked variety with olives and egg in the beef filling. The repulgue fold is not just aesthetic — different fold patterns historically identified different fillings in bakeries so customers could tell them apart without cutting. The olive and egg in the beef filling is what makes a Porteño (Buenos Aires-style) empanada — serve these with chimichurri verde for dipping.

Method

1
Place the provolone disk directly on the hot parrilla grate. Do not move it for the first 3-4 minutes — let it char on the bottom and begin melting. The bottom should develop a deep brown, slightly crispy crust while the interior becomes molten. Carefully slide a spatula under and flip — cook 2-3 more minutes until the second side is also coloured. The disk should hold its shape but feel completely soft when pressed.
2
Transfer to a serving dish. Top immediately with dried oregano, chilli flakes, and a drizzle of olive oil. Serve with crusty bread for scooping the molten edges. The cheese must be served immediately — provoleta cools and sets within 4 minutes.
💡 Provoleta is Argentina's most universally beloved starter and the most important non-meat item on the asado menu. At an outdoor asado, the provoleta goes on the grill first — before any meat — because it takes time and everyone is hungry. The slightly smoky, deeply savoury, molten-then-solid cheese texture is completely unlike fondue or grilled halloumi. Source semi-aged provolone specifically (not fresh provolone) — the semi-aged version holds its shape during grilling while developing a proper crust; fresh provolone melts too fast and becomes liquid.

2Mains — The Parrilla & Asado Table

Mains philosophy: The Argentine asado is not a menu of individual dishes — it is a procession. At a traditional asado, the parrillero (grill master) manages the fire and decides the order and timing of everything. First the offal (mollejas, chinchulines, morcilla — these cook fast and whet the appetite). Then the sausages (chorizo). Then the ribs and steaks (long, slow, these finish the meal). In a restaurant setting, you replicate this procession through your ordering system and service pacing. Never rush the asado procession — it is the experience, not just the food.

Method — The Argentine Grill Commandments

1
Season with salt only — and apply only at the start. Argentine beef needs nothing else. No marinade, no rubs, no sauces. The quality of the meat, the wood smoke, and the patience of the parrillero is the complete recipe. Apply coarse salt to both sides 30 minutes before grilling.
2
Low and slow is mandatory. The tira de asado grills over low-medium charcoal heat for 45-60 minutes. The Argentine way is: place the ribs bone-side down first, leave for 20-25 minutes until the bone side is deeply coloured and the fat is rendering. Flip. Cook bone-side up for the final 20-30 minutes. The internal fat should render completely — not a single white fat vein should remain unrendered when you cut through.
3
Serve on a wooden board, sliced between the bones. Chimichurri verde in a small bowl on the side — never pour over the meat. The guest applies it themselves. The colour of the cross-section when cut: deep red interior surrounded by a caramelised exterior with rendered fat seams.
💡 The Michelin detail: The tira de asado is the most Argentine single item in world food culture — more than the steak, more than the dulce de leche. When cross-cut beef ribs are cooked correctly over hardwood charcoal for nearly an hour, the result is something that cannot be achieved any other way: completely rendered fat, caramelised exterior, deeply smoky, and still juicy at the bone. The patience required is the technique. Argentine parrilleros measure their skill by the quality of their tira — it is the dish that separates a grill cook from a parrillero. Warn your guests at ordering: "The tira takes 50-60 minutes. This is correct. Can I suggest starting with the empanadas, provoleta, and chorizo while you wait?"

Method

1
Salt entrañas generously. Grill over HIGH heat — skirt steak must be cooked fast at high temperature, unlike the ribs. 3-4 min per side for medium-rare. In the US, outer skirt is sometimes marketed as fajita meat — it is not the same experience when sliced thin against the grain on a restaurant board with chimichurri. Describe it as "the cut the grill master eats." At $28.99 with 17.1% FC, it is your best-margin steak.

Parrilla Grill — The Identity

  • Commercial charcoal parrilla: V-shaped grate design channels fat away from coals — prevents flare-ups that burn the meat and create bitter flavour
  • Charcoal management: dedicated charcoal chimney starter always loaded; never let the parrilla get cold mid-service
  • Temperature zones: far left = low/slow for tira de asado (60-90 min zone); centre = medium for chicken and sausages; far right = high heat for entrañas and fast items
  • Visible to dining room if possible — the smoke and glow is your best marketing tool; guests watch it cook and it creates appetite
  • Parrillero = the most important hire. This person IS the restaurant.
THE IDENTITY

Empanada + Pastry

  • Repulgue training: every prep cook must be trained — 2-3 weeks minimum to reach consistent quality; budget training time in pre-opening
  • Empanada filling batch: make twice weekly; fill and refrigerate raw; bake to order (18-20 min from frozen)
  • Dulce de leche: make weekly in large batches (2L milk → 700g DDL); store in sealed jars up to 3 weeks
  • Alfajor production: biscuits baked weekly in batches; assemble daily with DDL; dip in chocolate same day as service
  • Flan: bain-marie bake in bulk, 16-24 portions at once; refrigerate overnight, unmould at service
THE CRAFT

Chimichurri Programme

  • Verde (parsley, garlic, olive oil, oregano, red wine vinegar, chilli): make daily — fresh chimichurri loses its brightness after 24 hours
  • Rojo (red pepper, paprika, tomato): can hold 3 days
  • Both served in small clay bowls or ramekins — never in squeeze bottles (visual quality signal)
  • Chimichurri is served alongside every meat item — it is never cooked onto the meat during the grill, always served separately for the guest to apply
  • Volume: plan 40ml per cover per service minimum
THE ACCOMPANIMENT

Mate + Drinks Bar

  • Gourd collection: minimum 30 calabaza gourds + bombillas; wash after each use
  • Mate water: electric kettle dedicated to mate only; set to 80°C — never boiling
  • Fernet display: Fernet-Branca bottle visible on bar counter; the visual alone generates orders
  • Argentine wine: full list Argentine-only; display wine map of Mendoza visible to guests
  • Quilmes: stock imported if available; display the green Quilmes glass as part of the table setting for Argentine-feeling atmosphere

Preemptively explaining the asado procession prevents the most common Argentine restaurant complaint (long wait times for mains) by reframing patience as technique. 10Chef's Lens — The Argentine Asado in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening an Argentine Restaurant in the United States
Nearly half of all Argentines have Italian ancestry. This is not a footnote in Argentine food history — it is the explanation for dulce de leche (born from Italian immigrants adapting New World milk), for sambayón (Argentine zabaglione), for pasta nights at Argentine restaurants, and for the deep Italian influence on Argentine bread, pastry, and ice cream culture. A chef opening an Argentine restaurant in the US should understand Argentina not as a Spanish-speaking country with beef, but as the meeting point of Spanish colonialism, Italian immigration, indigenous Andean food culture, and European gentlemen farming culture — all expressed through the medium of charcoal and grass-fed beef.

The parrillero occupies a position in Argentine culture that has no direct equivalent in any other food culture. The grill master at an asado is simultaneously the host, the entertainer, the showman, and the craftsperson. In Argentina, men will wait 90 minutes for the asado to be ready, drinking wine and talking, and nobody complains — because the waiting and the socialising is part of the experience. In a restaurant context, this translates to a very specific challenge: your guests need to understand that the wait is intentional, that it is the technique, and that the patience is what makes the result extraordinary. Your service team must be able to articulate this with genuine conviction.

Three technical non-negotiables: First, the charcoal. Argentine parrilla cooking requires hardwood lump charcoal — the smoke from wood is part of the flavour of tira de asado and Patagonian lamb. Gas parrilla can produce good results but not the same results. If you are serious about the concept, invest in proper charcoal infrastructure and budget for the cost. Second, the chimichurri must be made fresh daily. Jarred chimichurri exists and is fine for home use. In a restaurant presenting Argentine cuisine with authority, fresh chimichurri is non-negotiable. Third, the dulce de leche must be made in-house. The words "homemade dulce de leche" on your menu carry more weight with Argentine-American guests than any other three words in your kitchen's vocabulary.

The wine programme is your biggest revenue opportunity and your biggest differentiator. Argentine Malbec remains one of the world's greatest value propositions in wine — the same quality available at $12-15 per glass would cost $20-25 if it were French. Build a wine list that is exclusively Argentine, educate your service team to talk about Mendoza, Luján de Cuyo, and Patagonian wine with real knowledge, and price your Malbec to move. At a table average of 1.8 glasses per cover, your wine revenue can equal or exceed your food revenue. That is the Argentine asado advantage.
The patience is the technique. The fat is the flavour. The chimichurri is always fresh. — KitchenFounder
