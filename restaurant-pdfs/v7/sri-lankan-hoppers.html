The Concept

Hoppers opened in Soho in 2015. No reservations. Instant cult following. The hopper — a bowl-shaped fermented rice and coconut milk pancake — became London's most talked-about dish overnight. This guide is inspired by that model: a Sri Lankan concept built around one iconic vessel, deeply aromatic curries, and some of the most underrepresented flavours in the Western restaurant world. The US market has almost no serious Sri Lankan restaurants. That is the opportunity.
1Entrées — Small Plates
Entrées philosophy: Sri Lankan small plates are built on contrast — the crisp papadum against the cool coconut sambol, the hot devilled cashews against the cold king coconut water. These six dishes introduce guests to the flavour vocabulary of the island before the curries arrive. Most US guests have never tasted pol sambol or kottu roti. Your team's job is to make that discovery feel exciting, not intimidating.

Method

2Mains — Curries & Rice
Mains philosophy: Sri Lankan curries are built on three foundations: roasted curry powder (darker, more complex than raw), coconut milk (added at the end, never boiled aggressively), and tempering (mustard seeds, curry leaves, dried chilli bloomed in hot oil). Master these three techniques and you can cook any Sri Lankan curry. The recipes below represent the full breadth of the island — from the dry, sour ambulthiyal of the south to the rich, black pork curry of Colombo.

The Hopper Iron — Deep Dive

The appa chatty is the soul of your kitchen. It is a small, hemispherical cast-iron pan, roughly 6 inches in diameter, with steep curved sides. Batter is poured in, the pan is rotated to coat the walls, and the result is a bowl-shaped crisp with thin, lacy, almost-fried walls and a soft, steamed centre. It is one of the most technically specific cooking vessels in the world. Treat it with respect — a well-seasoned iron is irreplaceable.
1
Seasoning a new iron: Wash once with mild soap and dry completely. Place on medium heat. When hot, rub with a cut half-onion dipped in coconut oil — this builds the initial seasoning layer. Wipe clean. Repeat 3–4 times before first use. Never use soap again. After each service, wipe with a dry cloth and apply a thin film of coconut oil.
2
Temperature — the critical variable: The iron must be hot enough that batter immediately starts to crisp on contact (approximately 190–210°C surface temp), but not so hot that the batter scorches before the centre sets. Test by dropping a small amount of water — it should evaporate in under 2 seconds. The edge should sizzle immediately when batter contacts the iron. Too cold: hopper sticks. Too hot: edge burns before centre cooks. You will develop a feel for this within a week of daily cooking.
3
Batter consistency — the foundation: Hopper batter is made from raw rice (soaked 4–6 hours), coconut milk, a small amount of cooked rice (for softness), salt, and a fermentation starter (a small amount of toddy or active yeast). It should be the consistency of thin crêpe batter — it must flow freely when you swirl the pan, coating the walls in a thin, even film. Too thick: walls will be doughy and won't crisp. Too thin: the centre won't hold. Ferment overnight (8–12 hours) at room temperature. Batter peaks in the morning — make it fresh daily.
4
The swirl technique: Add one ladle of batter to the centre of the hot iron. Immediately pick up the iron with both hands using cloth handles and swirl in a continuous circular motion — the batter climbs the walls by centrifugal force. Return to burner. Drop egg in centre if making egg hopper. Cover with lid. Cook 90 seconds. The edges will be golden and pulling away from the iron naturally. Slide a thin spatula under the edge and lift out — it should release cleanly. If it sticks, the iron needs more seasoning or the temperature was wrong.
5
Common mistakes:
• Batter not fermented enough — hopper is dense and doughy, no airiness in the centre. Fix: longer ferment, or add a small pinch of sugar to accelerate yeast.
• Iron not hot enough — batter doesn't climb the walls, hopper is flat and soft. Fix: increase flame, wait 30 more seconds before next batch.
• Iron too hot — edges burn before the lid goes on. Fix: reduce flame slightly, work faster.
• Insufficient seasoning — hopper tears when removing. Fix: re-season with coconut oil and onion method, do not use soap.
• Batter too cold from fridge — doesn't flow properly. Fix: always bring batter to room temperature 30 minutes before service.
6
End of service iron care: While still warm, wipe interior with a dry cloth. Apply a thin film of coconut oil with a folded paper towel. Store upside-down on a clean dry surface. Never stack cast iron without a cloth between. A properly cared-for appa chatty will last 20+ years and season better with every use.

Daily Prep Schedule

1
7:00am — Hopper Batter Mix MUST DO FIRST
Mix hopper batter: rice flour, coconut milk, a touch of sugar, salt, and yeast (or toddy). Cover and leave in a warm spot to ferment for 8 hours. This batch covers lunch service. No shortcuts — the fermentation window is fixed. Assign Sous Chef to own this task every single morning. If yeast-based: water temp 38–40°C to activate. If toddy-based: use 2 tbsp toddy per batch, room temp.
2
8:00am — Curry Base Prep
Sweat down the aromatic base: sliced red onion, crushed ginger, garlic, and green chilli in coconut oil until deeply golden (20–25 min). Add roasted curry powder, black pepper, mustard seeds, curry leaves, and pandan. Build the paste that becomes the backbone of black pork curry, devilled chicken, and lamb curry. Batch 3–4 litres — enough for full day. Grate or blend fresh coconut for sambol and curry finishing.
3
9:00am — Slow Curry Batch Begins 2+ hours required
Add black pork and lamb pieces to the curry base. Brown well in batches, then slow simmer covered. Black pork curry needs a minimum 2 hrs to tenderise and develop its dark, almost-caramelised sauce character. Lamb curry: 90 min minimum, 2.5 hrs ideal. Do not rush with coconut milk — add it only in the final 20 minutes of cooking. These are your most expensive menu items; give them the time they need.
4
10:30am — Coconut Milk Curries, Dhal & Pol Sambol
Start the faster curries: coconut milk fish curry, devilled potato, jackfruit curry. Add coconut milk to the slow curries in their final stage. Cook parippu (dhal): red lentils simmered with turmeric, tempered with mustard seeds, curry leaves, and dried chilli. Make pol sambol: freshly grated coconut, red chilli flakes, lime juice, onion — this must be fresh daily for texture. Rice cooker on for samba or red rice.
5
11:30am — Mise En Place Check & Irons Ready
Full mise en place check: all curries in bain marie at 75°C+, pol sambol portioned, dhal ladled into holding pots, rice cooked and held. Hopper irons: season with coconut oil and heat over medium-high flame. Confirm batter has fermented — it should have risen slightly and smell faintly tangy. Test one hopper before service: it should peel cleanly from the iron with crisp lacy edges. 10-min team briefing — covers, specials, any 86s.
6
12:00pm — Lunch Service
Full kitchen on. Hopper station: one CDP per 2 irons. Target ticket time 10–14 min. String hoppers steamed to order (3–4 min). Egg hoppers: crack egg into iron after batter sets on edge, cover with lid to steam egg white. Monitor batter consistency throughout service — add 1–2 tbsp coconut milk if thinning. Never refrigerate active batter; it kills fermentation.
7
2:30pm — Break & Evening Prep Begins
Post-lunch: clean irons while warm (never cold), re-season with coconut oil, store covered. Assess curry stock levels for dinner. Mix second batter batch for dinner service now — 8 hours fermentation means it's ready for 10:30pm last orders. Restock pol sambol (must be fresh), refresh curry garnishes, cook second rice batch. Prep lamprais banana leaf packets if on the menu (banana leaf soaked, portioned, folded parcels assembled).
8
6:00pm — Dinner Service
Full team back on. Re-heat slow curries gently — never boil, especially coconut milk curries (they split). Re-season irons. Confirm dinner batter is bubbly and ready. Watalappam (coconut custard) must be portioned and plated cold — ensure enough portions for full dinner service. Bar: kirala (arrack-based) cocktails, arrack syrups ready. Expect higher covers than lunch — dinner is your revenue service.
9
10:30pm — Last Orders
Call last orders to FOH. Batter at this stage should still be active from the evening batch — use it through. Begin cooling curries not needed for last covers: spread into shallow hotel pans, cool to <70°F within 2 hrs, refrigerate immediately. FIFO labelling on all containers: item name, date, time cooled.
10
11:00pm — Kitchen Close & Iron Clean
Cool irons completely before storage. Wipe with dry cloth — never wash with water or soap (destroys seasoning). Rub lightly with coconut oil, cover with cloth or paper. Deep clean bain marie, pots, and surfaces. Prep first morning task card for tomorrow's opener: batter time, curry base quantities, any specials requiring long prep. Sign off on waste log and fridge temp log.

Section 10 — Chef's Lens: Why This, Why Now

A considered perspective on the Sri Lankan opportunity in America

The Most Underrepresented Great Cuisine in the US

Sri Lankan food is, by any objective measure, one of the world's great cuisines — and it is almost entirely absent from the American dining landscape. In a country where Thai, Vietnamese, Korean, and even Burmese restaurants now anchor neighborhoods in every major city, Sri Lanka remains a blank on the map. This isn't because the food is difficult, or the diaspora is small (there are over 50,000 Sri Lankans in the US), or the flavours are too challenging for Western palates. It's simply a matter of timing, critical mass, and the absence of a breakout moment. That moment is coming. The question is whether you'll be in front of it or behind it.

The Hopper as a Commercial Vessel

In twenty years of watching concepts succeed and fail, few dishes have the convergence of properties that hoppers do. They are visually dramatic — a crisp-edged bowl of fermented rice batter, served hot from a curved iron, with an egg pooled in the centre. They are inherently Instagram-native: the shape, the colour contrast, the steam. They carry theatre — the appa chatty is worked live on the line, and a good cook flipping hoppers in an open kitchen is a show. They are portion-controllable and cost-efficient. They are novel enough to drive destination dining but familiar enough (think: crispy pancake with egg) to convert the mildly adventurous. You will not find another dish that checks every box.

Ceylon Arrack — The Spirits Story Nobody Is Telling

Mezcal had its moment. Cachaça had its moment. Shochu is still waiting for its. Ceylon arrack — distilled from the fermented sap of coconut flowers, aged in Halmilla wood, with a terroir unlike anything in the Western spirits canon — has not had its moment yet. In Sri Lanka, it's the national drink, consumed daily, made by multi-generational distilleries. In London, a handful of bars have started building arrack cocktail programs. In the US: almost nothing. The founder who builds a genuine arrack program — flights, cocktails, a short education card on the back of the menu — is sitting on a differentiator with press-ready narrative and genuine depth.

The Hoppers London Effect

In 2015, a small restaurant opened on Frith Street in Soho, London. It was called Hoppers. It was 40 seats. It had a short menu, loud music, no reservations, and a months-long waitlist within weeks of opening. It went on to win awards, open a second location in King's Cross, and inspire a generation of Sri Lankan cooks to take their cuisine seriously in a professional context. More importantly: it created global demand for a cuisine that didn't have a restaurant identity before. People who ate at Hoppers went home wanting more — and found nothing. The US is still in the pre-Hoppers era. The restaurant that defines Sri Lankan cuisine for American diners doesn't exist yet. It could be yours.

The Honest Challenge: Sourcing

Goraka (a dried tamarind-adjacent souring agent), fresh pandan, curry leaves, dried Maldive fish, raw coconut toddy for fermentation — these are not available at Restaurant Depot. This is the real operational challenge of Sri Lankan cuisine in the US, and it's worth being honest about. The solution is threefold: build relationships with South Asian and Southeast Asian grocery distributors in your market (most now carry curry leaves and pandan); source goraka and Maldive fish through Sri Lankan and South Indian online importers (Diaspora Co., iShopIndian, local Sri Lankan grocers who will often sell wholesale to restaurants); and adapt where necessary — not compromising flavour, but substituting intelligently where a fresh ingredient isn't viable. The chefs running the best Sri Lankan restaurants in London solved this five years ago. Their playbook is available.

The Opportunity: First-Mover Advantage

In most American cities — Boston, Denver, Nashville, Portland, Austin, Atlanta, Chicago outside of Devon Avenue — there is no Sri Lankan restaurant. Not a bad one. Not a great one. None. You would not be competing for share of an existing market. You would be creating the market, owning the search terms, earning every local press story about "the best new restaurant" and "have you tried Sri Lankan food yet?" The first-mover premium in emerging cuisine categories is real, measurable, and durable. The Tamil-inflected, coconut-rich, hopper-centred cuisine of Sri Lanka is ready. The only missing ingredient is a founder willing to build it.
