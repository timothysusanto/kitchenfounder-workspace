A Letter from the Maestro

My grandfather made gelato in Palermo with a hand-cranked machine, rock salt, and instinct honed by generations. My father carried that instinct to Sydney, and I — after four years training under a master artigiano in Bologna — brought it back as something new. Not fusion. Not novelty. Just rigour applied to memory.

This guide is every recipe we run at the bar: from the absolute purity of our Stracciatella — nothing but fior di latte and hand-drizzled dark chocolate — to the layered drama of a Cassata Siciliana, assembled with the patience of a cathedral builder. Gelato is not ice cream. It is lower in fat, lower in overrun, higher in density, served between -11°C and -8°C to stay paddle-soft. Once you understand the physics, you understand the taste.

I have trained my team to treat every scoop as a statement. Technique is love made visible. Read every note. Follow every temperature. Never rush the riposo.

— Gelato Maestro, Guide #45

Signature Gelato Flavours

Six house-made gelato flavours churned fresh daily. Served at -11 deg C to -8 deg C. All bases made in-house from whole milk, fresh cream, and egg yolks.

Recipe 01 of 28 — Signature Gelato
Stracciatella
Fior di latte base, hand-drizzled dark chocolate shards
$7.50
Food cost: $1.45 AUD
GF NF
The purest expression of Italian gelato craft. A silken fior di latte base — nothing but whole milk, fresh cream, sugar, and egg yolks — drizzled mid-churn with a thread of molten 70% dark chocolate that shatters into irregular shards. The word stracciatella means "torn apart" in Italian. Every bite should feel like both destruction and revelation.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg gelato

  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)600 g
  • Fresh cream (35% fat)150 g
  • Egg yolks (fresh)80 g (4 yolks)
  • Caster sugar150 g
  • Skim milk powder30 g
  • Dextrose20 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g
  • Dark couverture 70% (Valrhona Guanaja)100 g
  • Neutral cooking oil (for chocolate)5 g
  • Fleur de sel (finishing)pinch

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Combine milk, cream, and skim milk powder in a heavy saucepan. Heat to 45°C, whisking to dissolve powder completely.
  2. Whisk egg yolks with sugar and dextrose in a bowl until pale and ribbon-like, about 2 minutes.
  3. Temper hot milk mixture into egg mixture in a slow stream, whisking constantly to avoid scrambling.
  4. Return custard to saucepan. Add locust bean gum, whisking vigorously. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly with a heat-safe spatula, until mix reaches 85°C (pasteurisation). Do not boil.
  5. Immediately strain through a fine-mesh sieve into a clean container. Cool rapidly over an ice bath to below 4°C. Cover and ripen in refrigerator for minimum 4 hours (overnight preferred) — this is the riposo, critical for texture.
  6. Churn base in a batch freezer or gelato machine set to standard programme. Target internal temperature of -6°C at extraction.
  7. While churning is in final 2 minutes, melt couverture with oil to 40°C. Load into a squeeze bottle.
  8. Open machine lid and drizzle molten chocolate in a thin, continuous stream directly into the churning gelato. It hits the cold mass and shatters instantly into irregular shards — stracciatella effect.
  9. Extract immediately into a pre-chilled steel pan. Blast freeze or cover with parchment directly on surface. Harden at -20°C for 1 hour minimum before serving.
  10. Serve between -11°C and -8°C. Scoop with a Parisienne paddle dipped in warm water.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Fior di latte should achieve 20–25% overrun (air incorporation) — dramatically less than commercial ice cream at 100%. Lower overrun means higher density and more intense flavour. The locust bean gum (LBG) stabilises the base during freezing, preventing large ice crystal formation. The 4-hour riposo allows the stabiliser to fully hydrate and the fat to homogenise. Never skip it.
Fail-Proof Tip
The chocolate for stracciatella must be at exactly 40°C when drizzled — too hot and it pools rather than shatters, too cool and it seizes before dispersing. The small addition of neutral oil lowers the chocolate's setting temperature by 2°C, giving you a slightly wider window. Use a thermometer, not intuition, on this step.
Plating and Serving Note
Two generous quenelles on a chilled white ceramic cup. The chocolate shards should be visible in the scoop — if they're not, the drizzle was too fast. Finish with a pinch of fleur de sel on the peak of each scoop. At the bar, serve with a small pirouette biscuit. No sauce — the chocolate IS the sauce.
Recipe 02 of 28 — Signature Gelato
Pistachio — Pistacchio di Bronte
100% Bronte Sicilian pistachio paste, zero artificial colouring
$8.50
Food cost: $2.20 AUD
GF
This is my signature. If you order one gelato, let it be this one. We import 100% pure pistachio paste from Bronte, the small town on the slopes of Mount Etna where pistachios grow in volcanic soil and taste unlike anything else on earth. The colour is a natural khaki-green-grey — not the garish emerald of cheap artificially coloured product. That is how you know it is real. This recipe has not changed since I first made it in Bologna at age 22.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg gelato

  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)550 g
  • Fresh cream (35% fat)100 g
  • Egg yolks (fresh)100 g (5 yolks)
  • Caster sugar140 g
  • Dextrose20 g
  • Skim milk powder25 g
  • Bronte pistachio paste (100% pure)120 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g
  • Sea salt1 g
  • Chopped roasted pistachios (garnish)30 g

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Warm pistachio paste in a small bowl over a double boiler to 35°C to loosen. Set aside.
  2. Heat milk, cream, and milk powder to 45°C in a heavy saucepan, whisking to dissolve. Add LBG and whisk vigorously.
  3. Whisk egg yolks with sugar and dextrose in a bowl until pale. Temper warm milk mixture in slowly while whisking constantly.
  4. Return to saucepan. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly, to 85°C. Strain through a fine sieve.
  5. While custard is still warm (above 60°C), whisk in the loosened pistachio paste and sea salt until fully incorporated. A stick blender creates a smoother emulsion — recommended.
  6. Cool rapidly over ice bath to below 4°C. Cover and ripen for minimum 6 hours (overnight ideal) — longer riposo than other flavours because pistachio fat needs extra time to integrate.
  7. Churn in batch freezer to -6°C extraction temperature.
  8. Extract into pre-chilled pan. Sprinkle half the chopped pistachios through the pan as you layer. Cover directly and harden at -20°C for 1 hour.
  9. Serve at -11°C to -8°C with remaining chopped pistachios as garnish on top of the scoop.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Pistachio paste is high in natural fat (approx. 50% oil content). This affects the gelato's freeze point depression — the mix will feel slightly softer at the same serving temperature compared to fior di latte. We compensate by reducing the cream ratio and increasing milk. The result is a gelato that is dense but not heavy, with clean nut flavour unmasked by dairy fat. Overrun target: 22–25%.
Fail-Proof Tip
Cheap pistachio paste is cut with almonds or artificially coloured. Real Bronte paste should be roughly-textured, oily, and smell intensely of pistachio — not sweetness, not marzipan. The colour will be an un-glamorous brownish-green. If your paste is bright green, it is not authentic. Source from a trusted Italian importer and pay the premium — it is the entire flavour of the dish.
Plating and Serving Note
Scoop into a ceramic cup or cone. The natural colour will surprise new customers — brief the front-of-house team to set expectations: "The natural colour of Bronte pistachio is not bright green. That muted tone is your guarantee of authenticity." Finish with a pinch of chopped roasted pistachios on top and a very small drizzle of high-quality pistachio oil if available.
Recipe 03 of 28 — Signature Gelato
Salted Caramel — Caramello Salato
House dry-method caramel, Tasmanian sea salt flakes
$7.50
Food cost: $1.60 AUD
GF NF
We make our caramel the old way — dry, direct, dangerous, and deeply flavoured. No water, no corn syrup, just caster sugar in a dry pan taken to a mahogany amber. The smell of proper caramel should make you slightly nervous. If it doesn't, you haven't gone far enough. The Tasmanian salt flakes go in at three points: the caramel, the custard base, and the final scoop. Salt is not a garnish here. It is architecture.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg gelato

  • Caster sugar (for caramel)200 g
  • Unsalted butter40 g
  • Fresh cream 35% (for caramel)80 g (warm)
  • Tasmanian sea salt flakes4 g + extra
  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)520 g
  • Fresh cream 35% (for base)80 g
  • Egg yolks80 g (4 yolks)
  • Caster sugar (for base)60 g
  • Skim milk powder30 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Make dry caramel: place 200 g sugar in a heavy stainless or copper saucepan over medium-high heat. Do not stir — swirl the pan gently once the edges begin to melt. Cook to deep mahogany amber (175°C on a sugar thermometer).
  2. Remove from heat. Add butter immediately — it will bubble violently. Whisk. Add warm cream in three additions, whisking between each. Add 2 g of the salt. Whisk until smooth. Set aside to cool slightly.
  3. Heat milk, cream (base portion), and milk powder to 45°C. Add LBG and whisk.
  4. Whisk egg yolks with 60 g sugar until pale. Temper warm milk in slowly, whisking constantly.
  5. Return to saucepan and cook to 85°C, stirring continuously. Strain.
  6. Whisk the caramel sauce into the hot custard base. Add remaining 2 g salt. Blend with a stick blender for 30 seconds for smoothness.
  7. Cool over ice bath to below 4°C. Ripen 4–6 hours minimum.
  8. Churn to -6°C. Extract into chilled pan. Fleck sea salt flakes through the layers as you pan. Harden at -20°C for 1 hour.
  9. Serve at -11°C to -8°C. Finish each serving with 2–3 large salt flakes on top.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
The dry caramel introduces sucrose breakdown products (fructose, glucose, plus Maillard compounds) that lower the freezing point. The finished gelato will be noticeably softer than fior di latte at the same serving temperature. We compensate by reducing the dextrose to zero — caramelised sugar already provides antifreeze effect. Salt lowers freezing point very slightly but its primary role is flavour — it suppresses bitterness and amplifies sweetness at the sensory level.
Fail-Proof Tip
The most common failure with dry caramel is stopping too early out of fear. Pale caramel produces pale flavour — sweet without complexity. You need the Maillard and pyrolysis compounds that form above 160°C. Take it to 175°C but do not exceed 180°C or it turns bitter. Keep a bowl of ice water nearby to dip the pan base if needed to stop cooking. Practice makes the window familiar.
Plating and Serving Note
Serve in a dark ceramic cup to contrast the warm amber tone of the gelato. Two scoops. Drizzle the cup with a small ribbon of leftover salted caramel sauce (kept warm in a squeeze bottle). Three large Tasmanian salt flakes placed on top — not ground salt, not table salt, specifically the large flat crystals. They provide a visual cue and a burst of crunch that resets the palate between bites.
Recipe 04 of 28 — Signature Gelato
Espresso — Caffè
Single origin double ristretto, dark roast intensity
$7.50
Food cost: $1.35 AUD
GF NF
Coffee gelato in most places is a disappointment — diluted, sweet, vague. Ours uses a double ristretto from a single-origin Ethiopian Yirgacheffe, dark roasted to bring forward chocolate and blackcurrant notes. We brew 200 g of ristretto for every kilogram of base. It is intensely, unapologetically coffee. The caffeine is real. Order it at night at your own risk.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg gelato

  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)480 g
  • Fresh cream (35% fat)100 g
  • Double ristretto (freshly brewed, cooled)200 g
  • Egg yolks80 g (4 yolks)
  • Caster sugar155 g
  • Skim milk powder25 g
  • Dextrose15 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g
  • Instant espresso powder (intensifier)8 g
  • Dark chocolate shavings (garnish)20 g

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Brew double ristretto and allow to cool to room temperature. Do not refrigerate yet as the oils need to stay integrated.
  2. Dissolve instant espresso powder into the cooled ristretto. This double-extraction technique maximises coffee solids without over-diluting the base.
  3. Heat milk, cream, and milk powder to 45°C. Add LBG and whisk vigorously to dissolve.
  4. Whisk egg yolks with sugar and dextrose until pale and thick.
  5. Temper hot milk into eggs slowly, whisking. Return to saucepan and cook to 85°C, stirring constantly.
  6. Strain custard. Immediately stir the coffee-ristretto mixture into the hot strained custard. The heat keeps coffee oils in suspension.
  7. Blend with stick blender for 20 seconds. Cool over ice bath to below 4°C. Ripen minimum 4 hours.
  8. Churn to -6°C. Extract into chilled pan. Harden 1 hour at -20°C.
  9. Serve at -11°C. Garnish with dark chocolate shavings and a light dusting of espresso powder.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Coffee contains numerous compounds (chlorogenic acids, melanoidins, volatile aromatics) that affect gelato texture and freeze behaviour. Coffee also contains dissolved CO2 from the espresso extraction that must degas before churning — hence the cooling step. The instant espresso powder adds coffee solids (melanoidins) that contribute both colour and extra body to the mix. Overrun target: 20–22% — coffee gelato should be dense.
Fail-Proof Tip
Never add hot espresso to hot custard and then reheat — the volatile aromatics responsible for fresh coffee flavour flash off above 70°C. Always let your ristretto cool to room temperature before incorporating into the strained (not still-cooking) custard. If you add coffee too early in the cooking process, you will have coffee-flavoured milk custard with no coffee aroma. The aroma is half the flavour.
Plating and Serving Note
Serve in a small espresso cup or a dark ceramic cup to reinforce the coffee identity. One large oval scoop. Dust the top lightly with fine espresso powder through a small fine-mesh sieve. Scatter dark chocolate shavings. The scoop should look like a crema-topped espresso. For the affogato service (Recipe 07), use this gelato as the base — the flavour combination is transcendent.
Recipe 05 of 28 — Signature Gelato
Hazelnut — Nocciola IGP Piemonte
100% Piedmont hazelnut paste · NF version: sunflower seed paste
$8.00
Food cost: $1.90 AUD
GF NF*
Hazelnuts from the Langhe hills of Piedmont are to Italian gelato what truffles are to the same region's cooking — the defining luxury ingredient. Our nocciola paste is 100% roasted Piedmont hazelnuts, ground to a smooth butter without added sugar or oil. It tastes like the air in a Piemontese autumn. The nut-free version uses a premium sunflower seed paste, roasted and ground the same way. It is not identical, but it is remarkable.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg gelato

  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)540 g
  • Fresh cream (35% fat)90 g
  • Egg yolks100 g (5 yolks)
  • Caster sugar140 g
  • Dextrose20 g
  • Skim milk powder25 g
  • Piedmont hazelnut paste (100% pure)130 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g
  • Sea salt1 g
  • Whole roasted hazelnuts (garnish)20 g

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Warm hazelnut paste to 35°C in a double boiler to loosen the oils. This ensures even distribution through the custard.
  2. Heat milk, cream, and milk powder to 45°C, whisking to dissolve. Add LBG and whisk well.
  3. In a bowl, whisk egg yolks with sugar and dextrose until pale and slightly thickened.
  4. Temper warm milk into eggs slowly. Return to saucepan and cook to 85°C, stirring constantly. Strain through fine sieve.
  5. While custard is still warm, whisk in loosened hazelnut paste and sea salt. Blend with stick blender for 30 seconds — the emulsion should be completely smooth with no paste streaks.
  6. Cool over ice bath to below 4°C. Ripen minimum 6 hours. The hazelnut oils need extended time to integrate into the water phase of the mix.
  7. Churn to -6°C extraction temperature. Extract into pre-chilled pan.
  8. Press halved roasted hazelnuts into the surface of the panned gelato. Harden at -20°C for 1 hour.
  9. Serve at -11°C. For NF version: replace hazelnut paste with sunflower seed paste at same quantity. All other steps identical.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Hazelnut paste (like pistachio) is naturally high in unsaturated fats which remain liquid at colder temperatures than saturated dairy fat. This gives nocciola gelato its characteristic soft, yielding texture even straight from the blast freezer. Overrun target: 22%. The extended riposo is not optional — under-rested nocciola gelato will exhibit fat separation at the surface (visible as a slightly greasy sheen) after churning.
Fail-Proof Tip
NF (nut-free) version: use a roasted sunflower seed paste made in-house or sourced from a dedicated nut-free facility. Standard sunflower butter has a slightly green, raw flavour — look specifically for roasted versions. If making in-house, roast hulled sunflower seeds at 170°C for 12 minutes until golden, cool completely, then blend in a high-powered blender for 8–10 minutes to a smooth paste. Allergen protocols must be strictly followed for the NF version — dedicated equipment.
Plating and Serving Note
The natural colour is a warm medium brown — not the pale beige of inferior product. Serve in a round ceramic cup. Two scoops, slightly offset. Place 3–4 whole roasted hazelnuts at the base of the scoops. For NF version, substitute toasted sunflower seeds (explicitly labelled for the customer). The hazelnut gelato pairs exceptionally well with the Caffè Shakerato (Recipe 26) — suggest the combination.
Recipe 06 of 28 — Signature Gelato / Sorbet
Mango Sorbet — Sorbetto di Mango Alphonso
Alphonso mango puree, lime zest, no dairy — DF · VG · NF · GF
$7.00
Food cost: $1.50 AUD
GF VG NF DF
A sorbet, not a gelato — but made with the same rigour. Alphonso mangoes from Maharashtra (India) are the world's finest: saffron-orange, intensely aromatic, with a honey-richness that needs no embellishment. We use 60% pure Alphonso puree, a careful invert sugar syrup to control freeze point, and fresh lime zest added post-churn to preserve the volatile citrus oils. Completely dairy-free. Completely vegan. Completely not a compromise.

Ingredients — Yields 1 kg sorbet

  • Alphonso mango puree (100%)600 g
  • Filtered water185 g
  • Caster sugar120 g
  • Dextrose30 g
  • Trehalose20 g
  • Invert sugar (trimoline)25 g
  • Lime zest (fresh Tahitian lime)3 g (2 limes)
  • Fresh lime juice20 g
  • Locust bean gum2 g
  • Sea salt0.5 g

Method — Serves 8–10 scoops per batch

  1. Combine water, caster sugar, dextrose, trehalose, and invert sugar in a saucepan. Heat to 85°C, stirring until fully dissolved. This is the simple syrup base.
  2. Whisk in LBG while still warm. The stabiliser requires heat to hydrate properly in a dairy-free mix.
  3. Cool syrup to below 30°C. Do not add mango to hot syrup — heat destroys the volatile Alphonso aromatics.
  4. Combine cooled syrup with mango puree, lime juice, and sea salt. Blend with stick blender until perfectly smooth.
  5. Taste for balance: the refractometer reading should be 28–32 Brix. If below 28, the sorbet will freeze rock-hard. If above 32, it will not freeze firm enough for scooping.
  6. Ripen in refrigerator for 4 hours minimum.
  7. Churn in batch freezer or sorbet setting. Sorbet churns faster than gelato — watch the machine. Extract at -5°C (slightly warmer than gelato as there is no dairy fat).
  8. Extract into chilled pan. Add lime zest by folding gently through the freshly churned sorbet — do not churn the zest, fold it in post-extraction.
  9. Harden at -20°C for 1 hour. Serve at -10°C to -8°C.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Sorbet freezing physics differ fundamentally from gelato: there is no fat to create creaminess, so texture depends entirely on sugar management (freeze point depression) and stabiliser use. The combination of sucrose, dextrose (lower molecular weight = more freezing point depression per gram), trehalose (provides body and anti-crystallisation), and invert sugar (prevents re-crystallisation on storage) is carefully balanced. Overrun target for sorbet: 25–30% — sorbets need slightly more air than gelato for a scoopable texture.
Fail-Proof Tip
Use a refractometer. It is the single most important tool for consistent sorbet. Alphonso puree varies in natural sugar content between batches and seasons (typically 14–18 Brix). Adjust your added sugars based on the actual Brix reading of your puree. Without this measurement, your sorbet will be inconsistent in texture from batch to batch. A basic refractometer costs $25 AUD and pays for itself in the first week.
Plating and Serving Note
Serve in a chilled white ceramic cup — the saffron-orange colour of Alphonso sorbet is spectacular against white. One generous quenelle (oval scoop with a spoon dipped in warm water). Micro-grate additional lime zest directly over the top tableside for aroma. Optionally add 3–4 small pieces of fresh mango alongside. The sorbet should look like sunshine in a bowl. This is your best seller to customers who cannot eat dairy.

Recipe 07 of 28 — Main Dessert Plates
Gelato Affogato
Double ristretto poured tableside over house gelato — theatre on a plate
$14.00
Food cost: $2.80 AUD
GF NF
"Affogato" means drowned. The gelato is drowned in espresso — but the genius is that neither component fully wins. The hot coffee melts the gelato at the surface, creating a warm-cold, bitter-sweet emulsion in the bowl that changes with every spoonful as the customer eats it. This is the most beautiful dish on our menu and costs us almost nothing to make. The theatre is everything: pull the espresso tableside and pour it yourself.

Ingredients — Per serving

  • House gelato (fior di latte or espresso)2 scoops (120 g)
  • Double ristretto (single origin, just pulled)60 ml (2 shots)
  • Small pirouette biscuit1 piece
  • Cocoa powder (for dusting)small pinch

Method — Per serving, 2 minutes

  1. Pre-chill a small ceramic tulip bowl in the freezer for at least 30 minutes before service.
  2. Scoop two generous portions of gelato into the chilled bowl. Work quickly — the bowl must reach the table before the gelato starts to melt.
  3. Carry the bowl and a freshly-pulled double ristretto to the table in separate vessels.
  4. Present the gelato to the guest. Pour the hot ristretto directly over the gelato tableside. The hiss and steam are the dish's opening chord.
  5. Dust lightly with cocoa powder. Place the pirouette biscuit at the edge of the bowl.
  6. Instruct the guest: "Start immediately. The contrast lasts about 90 seconds."
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
The affogato is a live lesson in phase transitions. Hot espresso (93°C) hits cold gelato (-8°C) and creates an immediate thermal gradient. The fat globules in the gelato begin to melt from the outside, releasing the milk proteins that were holding the structure. The customer experiences this as a rapidly changing texture from firm-cold to cool-creamy to warm-liquid. The emulsification of melted gelato and espresso produces an extraordinarily smooth mouthfeel that neither component achieves alone.
Fail-Proof Tip
Temperature control is everything. If the gelato is too warm when the coffee hits it, it collapses immediately and the guest gets soup. If the bowl is too warm, same result. Always use a chilled bowl (not just room-temp ceramic), and always pour coffee immediately upon table delivery. Brief your floor staff on the timing — the affogato waits for no one. Flavour choice: vanilla fior di latte gives the most contrast; espresso gelato gives the deepest coffee intensity.
Plating and Serving Note
The presentation is two pristine scoops in a chilled white tulip bowl, carried to the table with the espresso in a separate small pitcher. The pour is done by the server, not the kitchen — this is a service moment. The flourish of steam rising from cold gelato is the dish. Never pre-pour. Never assemble in the kitchen. The tableside theatre justifies the price point and creates a moment that customers photograph and share.
Recipe 08 of 28 — Main Dessert Plates
Semifreddo al Torrone
Nougat semifreddo, Sicilian honey, orange zest, wafer
$18.00
Food cost: $3.60 AUD
GF*
Semifreddo is Italy's other frozen dessert — not churned, but frozen, lighter and more mousse-like than gelato. This version is built around torrone (Italian nougat) — a confection of honey, egg white, and almonds with roots in Arab-Sicilian culinary history. The semifreddo is rich with nougat, fragrant with orange zest, and finished with a thin ostia wafer on top. It is cut at the table with a hot knife for drama.

Ingredients — Yields 1 loaf (8 portions)

  • Fresh egg yolks120 g (6 yolks)
  • Caster sugar150 g
  • Water (for sugar syrup)40 g
  • Fresh egg whites120 g (4 whites)
  • Thickened cream (35% fat)400 g
  • Torrone (Italian nougat, chopped)200 g
  • Sicilian orange blossom honey40 g
  • Orange zest (fresh, Navel orange)5 g (2 oranges)
  • Ostia wafer sheets2 sheets
  • Flaked almonds (toasted, for garnish)30 g

Method — Serves 8, prep 45 min, freeze 6 hrs

  1. Make Italian meringue: cook sugar and water to 121°C (hard ball stage). Meanwhile whip egg whites to soft peak. Stream hot syrup into whites while machine is running. Whip to cool, stiff, glossy meringue.
  2. Make pate a bombe: whip yolks over a double boiler with honey until pale, thick, and tripled in volume. Remove from heat, continue whipping until cool.
  3. Whip cold cream to soft peaks — do not over-whip to stiff peaks as it will make the semifreddo grainy after freezing.
  4. Fold the pate a bombe into the Italian meringue gently with a large rubber spatula, maintaining as much volume as possible.
  5. Fold in whipped cream in two additions, cutting through the centre to avoid deflating.
  6. Fold in chopped torrone and orange zest. The nougat pieces should be irregularly distributed.
  7. Line a 10 x 20 cm loaf pan with plastic wrap, leaving overhang on all sides. Pour in the semifreddo mixture. Smooth the top.
  8. Lay ostia wafer sheets on top, pressing gently to adhere. Fold plastic wrap over. Freeze for minimum 6 hours or overnight.
  9. To serve: unmould onto a cutting board. Heat a knife in hot water and wipe dry. Cut slices 2.5 cm thick. Plate immediately.
Chef Technique Note — Gelato Science
Semifreddo achieves its creamy texture without churning through a combination of three foamed elements: Italian meringue (air in egg whites), pate a bombe (air in yolks), and whipped cream (air in fat). The fat from cream and the sugar from nougat both contribute to freeze point depression.
Fail-Proof Tip
The most common failure is using purchased torrone that is too hard — it shatters into jaw-cracking shards in the frozen semifreddo. Use a soft or medium-soft torrone (morbido). If your torrone is very firm, warm it briefly in an oven at 50°C for 5 minutes to soften before chopping. Cut into irregular pieces of 1–1.5 cm — too small and they disappear, too large and they impede clean slicing. Taste your torrone before using — sweetness levels vary enormously by brand.
Plating and Serving Note
Serve a 2.5 cm slice on a chilled white rectangular plate. The cut face should show beautiful marbling of white semifreddo and golden torrone pieces. Drizzle the plate with a thin line of Sicilian honey. Scatter toasted flaked almonds. Add a small sprig of fresh mint or a candied orange peel curl. The ostia wafer top gives a rustic crunch. Serve immediately — semifreddo softens quickly on a warm plate.

  • Full cream (35% fat)600 g
  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)400 g
  • Caster sugar120 g
  • Tahitian vanilla beans (split, scraped)2 pods
  • Titanium-grade gelatine leaves7 g (3.5 sheets)
  • Strawberries (fresh, hulled)400 g
  • Caster sugar (for compote)50 g

  • Shortbread biscuits (crushed)80 g (contains gluten)
  • Unsalted butter (melted)25 g

The most common failure: panna cotta that will not unmould cleanly. This is almost always caused by insufficient greasing of the moulds. Tiramisu Classico

Ingredients — Yields 1 tray (12 portions)

  • Mascarpone cheese (250g, room temp)500 g
  • Fresh egg yolks120 g (6 yolks)
  • Caster sugar130 g
  • Fresh egg whites150 g (5 whites)
  • Savoiardi (Italian ladyfinger biscuits)300 g (approx 24)
  • Double espresso (freshly brewed, cooled)400 ml
  • Dry Marsala wine80 ml
  • Dutch-process cocoa powder (unsweetened)30 g (for dusting)
  • Dark chocolate (70%, for garnish)20 g

Method — Prep 40 min, set overnight

  1. Brew espresso and allow to cool to room temperature. Mix with Marsala. Set aside.
  2. Make pate a bombe: whisk egg yolks and 80 g of the sugar in a bowl over a simmering double boiler. Whisk vigorously until the mixture reaches 60°C (kills raw egg risk), then whip off heat until cool, pale, and tripled in volume.
  3. Beat mascarpone in a stand mixer until smooth and lump-free. Fold the yolk mixture into mascarpone gently with a rubber spatula until just combined.
  4. In a clean bowl, whip egg whites to soft peaks. Add remaining 50 g sugar gradually and whip to firm, glossy Italian meringue.
  5. Fold meringue into mascarpone mixture in three additions using a large spatula. Cutting strokes — do not stir or deflate.
  6. Quickly dip each savoiardo into the espresso-Marsala mixture — one dip on each side, no more than 2 seconds total. The biscuit should be damp throughout but not so wet it disintegrates.
  7. Layer soaked savoiardi in a deep tray. Cover with half the mascarpone cream. Dust generously with cocoa through a fine sieve.
  8. Add second layer of soaked savoiardi. Cover with remaining cream. Smooth top. Cover tightly with plastic wrap. Refrigerate overnight — minimum 8 hours.
  9. To serve: dust fresh cocoa over the top tableside. Cut portions with a warm, dry knife. Add dark chocolate shavings on each plate.
Chef Technique Note
The pate a bombe over a double boiler (rather than just whisking cold) is a food safety and texture step. Raw egg yolks at 60°C are pasteurised while simultaneously creating a cooked-sugar coating on the yolk proteins that gives the mascarpone cream its characteristic stability without gelatine. This is the technical difference between a tiramisu that holds its structure when cut and one that slumps. Do not skip the temperature verification.
Fail-Proof Tip
The "dipping" step is where most tiramisu fails. Too brief: the centre of the biscuit stays dry and powdery — you feel it in the finished dish as a hard core. Too long: the biscuit becomes a soggy mush that collapses the layers. The correct result is a savoiardo that is uniformly damp — flexible, not breaking — all the way through. Test your espresso temperature and biscuit brand before service. Different brands absorb at different rates.
Plating and Serving Note
Cut a 7 x 4 cm rectangular portion. Use a warm dry palette knife to transfer cleanly to a chilled plate. Dust the top of the portion with a fine layer of Dutch cocoa from 20 cm height for even coverage. Place one dark chocolate curl alongside. Add a small espresso-dipped savoiardo piece as a visual cue. The cross-section should show clean alternating layers of cream and biscuit — this requires the overnight set. Never serve the same day it is made.

Sfogliatella

Food cost: $2.80 AUD

The sfogliatella is one of the great technical achievements of the Italian pastry tradition. The riccia (curly) version requires dozens of tissue-thin layers of dough separated by lard — when baked, they shatter explosively into shards. There is a legend that the nuns of a Neapolitan convent invented it to avoid wasting a semolino porridge. Whether true or not, the result is a pastry of improbable complexity from humble ingredients. We make the riccia version only. The frolla is easier but not the same thing.

Ingredients — Yields 8 pieces

  • Strong flour (00, 12% protein)500 g
  • Water (cold)175 ml
  • Sea salt5 g
  • Honey10 g
  • Lard (for lamination)250 g (softened)
  • Sheep's milk ricotta (drained)250 g
  • Fine semolino (cooked, cooled)100 g
  • Caster sugar80 g
  • Candied orange peel (fine dice)30 g
  • Whole eggs (for filling)2
  • Ground cinnamon3 g
  • Icing sugar (for dusting)30 g

Method — Prep 2 hrs, rest overnight, bake 25 min

  1. Make pasta: combine flour, water, salt, honey. Knead 10 minutes until very smooth and elastic. Wrap tightly. Rest 1 hour at room temperature — the gluten needs to relax fully for the thin rolling to follow.
  2. Divide dough into 4 portions. Use a pasta machine to roll each to the thinnest setting (setting 9). The sheets should be almost transparent.
  3. Lay one sheet on the bench. Spread a thin, even layer of softened lard over the entire surface using an offset palette knife. Lay the second sheet on top, spread with lard again. Repeat with all sheets to build a four-layer stack.
  4. Roll the lard-layered stack into a tight log, working from the short end. Refrigerate the log overnight — this is essential for the lard to firm and the layers to set.
  5. Make filling: cook semolino in 200 ml water with a pinch of salt until thick porridge. Cool completely. Combine with drained ricotta, sugar, eggs, cinnamon, and candied orange. Mix until homogenous.
  6. Remove the dough log from refrigerator. Slice into 1 cm rounds. Cup each round in your palm and use your thumb to push the centre out, forming a cone shape. Rotate as you thin the edges — this is a skill that takes practice.
  7. Fill each cone with 2 tablespoons of ricotta filling. Pinch the open edge closed firmly.
  8. Bake at 200°C for 22–25 minutes until deeply golden, shatteringly flaky on the outside. Dust with icing sugar immediately after removal from oven.
Chef Technique Note
The sfogliatella's shattered layers are the result of steam-lamination during baking. Water trapped between the lard-coated dough layers vaporises at 100°C, forcing the layers apart. The lard (high melting point, plastic texture) coats each layer preventing them from rebonding as they separate. Butter cannot replicate this effect — it has too much water content and too low a melting point. Lard is not negotiable for authentic sfogliatella. The overnight refrigeration ensures the lard is firm enough to keep the roll's shape during slicing.
Fail-Proof Tip
The shaping step — pushing the centre of the slice into a cone — is the most technically demanding part. Work with cold dough from the refrigerator. Warm dough tears instead of stretching. Keep a bowl of cold water nearby to cool your hands if the heat from your palms begins to melt the lard. If a cone tears, press the tear closed firmly and bake anyway — small tears in sfogliatella are not visible after baking as the layers puff and hide imperfections.
Plating and Serving Note
Serve two sfogliatelle on a plain white plate, tips pointing toward the guest. Dust generously with icing sugar while still warm — sugar that melts into the hot pastry looks better than sugar dust that slides off a cold surface. Add a small espresso sauce or orange blossom honey pool. Sfogliatella is best served hot — serve within 10 minutes of leaving the oven. Brief the floor: this dish cannot be held and re-served.

Method — Prep 1 hr, assemble to order

  • Caster sugar80 g
  • Bitter almond extract2–3 drops (careful — powerful)
  • Sea salt0.5 g
  • Brioche bun (house made, see note)1 per serve
  • Thickened cream 35% (for Chantilly)100 ml

  • Vanilla bean seeds (for Chantilly)quarter pod

Method — Granita: start 6 hrs ahead

  1. Blanch almonds: pour boiling water over almonds, stand 2 minutes, drain, and slip off skins. The white inner nut is the raw material.

Chef Technique Note — Granita Science
Unlike sorbet, granita intentionally develops large ice crystals — this is the defining texture. The scraping technique manually breaks the growing ice matrix into fragments before they form the large interlocking crystals of a solid block of ice. The result is icy, light, and refreshing rather than smooth. Almond milk has very low dissolved solids compared to a sugar syrup, meaning it freezes more readily — the first scrape should happen earlier (40–45 min) than for a fruit-based granita.
Fail-Proof Tip
Bitter almond extract is extraordinarily powerful — 2 drops is genuinely sufficient for 500 ml of liquid. Add it drop by drop and taste between drops. Too much produces a cough-syrup medicinal quality that cannot be corrected. The authentic Sicilian almond flavour is subtle and floral, not aggressive. Also: use a metal tray, not glass or plastic — metal conducts cold more efficiently and freezes the edges faster, giving better scraping results.

The granita goes in a pre-frozen ceramic cup (store cups in the freezer). The brioche arrives warm, split, toasted golden. The Chantilly is piled generously on top of the brioche half or piped alongside. The temperature contrast — icy granita, warm bread, room-temp cream — is the Sicilian morning in a serving. A final dusting of very finely ground almonds over the granita is an optional traditional touch. Serve the components separately so the guest can combine them at their own pace.

  • Pistachio gelato (R02)350 g

The espresso is our opening chord, our closing note, and everything in between. We use a single-origin Ethiopian Yirgacheffe, dark roast, pulled as a ristretto — 30 ml in 25 seconds at 9 bar, 93°C. The crema should be hazelnut-brown, dense, and persist for 90 seconds without breaking. We do not offer milk in the espresso. Milk is available separately. This cup is designed to be experienced as it was made: alone, small, and without interruption.

Ingredients — Per espresso

  • Ground Ethiopian Yirgacheffe (dark roast)18 g (double basket)
  • Filtered water (93°C, pre-heated)30 ml yield
  • Extraction time25–28 seconds
  • Pressure9 bar (machine set)
  • Pre-heated espresso cup (ceramic, 90 ml)1
  • Small square of dark chocolate (service)1 piece

Method — Per espresso

  1. Pre-heat everything: cup, group head (run a blank shot), and spoon. Cold equipment steals temperature from the extraction.
  2. Grind to order if possible. Freshly ground coffee degasses CO2 which creates the crema — pre-ground coffee is flat.
  3. Dose 18 g into the double portafilter basket. Distribute evenly with a finger or distribution tool. Tamp with 15–20 kg pressure, perfectly level. The tamp level is critical — uneven tamp causes channelling.
  4. Lock portafilter and pull shot immediately — do not allow the dry puck to absorb steam from the group head.
  5. Target yield: 30 ml in 25–28 seconds. Crema should be caramel-brown, not pale (under-extracted) or dark-brown with a white hole (over-extracted).

Chef Technique Note — Coffee Science
The crema in espresso is an emulsion of CO2 gas trapped in a matrix of emulsified coffee oils and melanoidin foam. It forms under 9-bar pressure during extraction. Single-origin Ethiopian Yirgacheffe at dark roast yields a crema with pronounced chocolate and blackcurrant notes, lower acidity than light roast, and the characteristic dense body required for affogato and the Shakerato. Light roast produces more acidity and fruit — interesting but wrong for this menu's direction.
Fail-Proof Tip
The three most common espresso failures: 1) Sour = under-extracted (too coarse grind, too short time, too cool water). 2) Bitter = over-extracted (too fine grind, too long, too hot). 3) Weak = channelling (uneven tamp creating water pathways through the puck). Each failure has one specific correction. Keep a tasting log of each day's first pull and adjust the grinder by 0.5 notch increments. The environment — humidity, temperature, barometric pressure — affects grind size daily.
Plating and Serving Note
White pre-heated ceramic espresso cup (90 ml, 60 ml fill). White saucer with a small teaspoon. One square of dark 70% chocolate on the saucer edge. No sugar on the table unless requested — our espresso at the correct extraction is balanced without sugar. Serve immediately upon pulling. A two-second gap between the shot completing and the cup reaching the customer is acceptable. Longer, and the crema begins to deteriorate. Speed and temperature are the two non-negotiables.

The almond milk provides a liquid medium and reinforces the almond flavour without diluting it. The simple syrup allows adjustment for the natural sweetness variation of different batches of almonds. Production efficiency: the granita base (R15) serves double duty for the Granita con Brioche plated dessert and this drink — make in larger batches to reduce per-unit labour.
Fail-Proof Tip
The granita crystals must be stored in a wide shallow container in the blast freezer, not a deep tub — deep storage compresses the bottom crystals under weight, fusing them into a solid block. If this happens, use a fork to re-scrape the block before service. Ideally, the granita is scraped fresh each morning and replenished hourly during service on busy days. The crystals deteriorate in texture after 4–5 hours at service temperature due to re-crystallisation — fresh is always better.
Plating and Serving Note
Present in a squat, wide-mouthed glass (bicchiere basso) so the guest can get a wide spoon in without difficulty. The visual is icy white crystals with almond milk pooling at the base — clean, pale, and pure. Two whole almonds on top. No straw — this is a spoon drink. Serve on a small saucer with a wide dessert spoon. In summer, keep the serving glasses in the freezer at all times. A warm glass will melt the granita before it reaches the customer.

Cremino Hot Chocolate
Valrhona single-origin, steamed whole milk, edible gold leaf finish
$12.00
Food cost: $2.10 AUD
GF NF
This is not hot cocoa. It is a dense, almost pudding-thick European-style hot chocolate made with Valrhona Caraibe 66% and steamed whole milk — no water, no milk powder, no shortcuts. The cremino refers to the initial texture: thick, barely pourable, with the density of a loose custard. It becomes slightly thinner as you drink it as the chocolate cools and the cocoa butter resolidifies marginally. Finished with a fragment of edible 24-karat gold leaf — because sometimes a Tuesday needs ceremony.

Ingredients — Per cup

  • Valrhona Caraibe 66% dark couverture50 g
  • Whole milk (3.5% fat)200 ml
  • Thickened cream (for richness)20 ml
  • Caster sugar (optional, adjust to taste)10 g
  • Sea salt (pinch)0.5 g
  • Edible 24-karat gold leaf1 fragment
  • Pre-heated tulip cup (240 ml)1

Method — Per cup, 5 minutes

  1. Chop couverture very finely. The smaller the chop, the faster and more even the melt.
  2. Heat whole milk and cream in a small saucepan to 70°C. Do not boil — boiled milk develops a skin and loses sweetness from caramelisation.
  3. Remove from heat. Add chopped couverture all at once. Wait 90 seconds without stirring to allow the heat to penetrate.
  4. Begin stirring from the centre outward in small circles, gradually expanding — classic Valrhona ganache emulsification technique. Continue until perfectly smooth and glossy.
  5. Add sugar (if using) and sea salt. Return to low heat and whisk vigorously for 2 minutes until the mixture thickens slightly and a gentle foam develops on the surface.
  6. Pour into a pre-heated tulip cup.
  7. Lay one fragment of edible gold leaf on the surface of the hot chocolate using a dry, fine brush — do not use fingers as body moisture dissolves gold leaf instantly.
  8. Serve immediately with a small teaspoon and a pirouette biscuit on the saucer.
Chef Technique Note
The emulsification technique (stirring from centre outward) creates a smooth, unbroken chocolate emulsion where the cocoa butter droplets are uniformly dispersed throughout the milk. If you stir incorrectly (large sweeping strokes from the start), the fat and water phases can separate, leaving a grainy texture. The final 2 minutes over heat whisking thickens the drink through partial starch gelatinisation from the cocoa solids and protein denaturation from the milk — this is what creates the cremino texture distinct from regular hot chocolate.
Fail-Proof Tip
Edible gold leaf is the most volatile garnish in professional kitchens. Store in its booklet away from air movement (never open near a vent or fan). Use a dry, fine artist's brush to transfer — any moisture dissolves it instantly. The leaf should be applied last, after the cup reaches the customer if possible. One fragment (approx. 3 x 3 cm) is sufficient and visually striking. Confirm your supplier is selling food-grade 24-karat leaf, not craft gold which contains nickel. The flavour is neutral — the effect is entirely visual.
Plating and Serving Note
White tulip cup (240 ml) on a matching white saucer. One small teaspoon. One pirouette biscuit at 4 o'clock on the saucer (classic Italian caffe presentation). The gold leaf fragment sits on the surface of the dark chocolate — a startling visual luxury for $12. This drink is your highest-margin item with the highest perceived value. It photographs beautifully. In winter, it is a reason to stay. Brief your floor: "The gold leaf is edible, 24-karat, and completely tasteless. It is purely for beauty."

Caffe Shakerato
Double espresso, ice, caster sugar — shaken to a cold foam
$9.00
Food cost: $0.90 AUD
GF DF VG NF
The shakerato is Italy's summer espresso: hot coffee shaken violently with ice and sugar until a cold, aerated foam forms on top. The foam is the espresso crema transformed — the proteins and melanoidins that create crema under pressure instead create a cold foam under mechanical agitation. The result is cold, slightly sweet, intensely caffeinated, and topped with a foam that persists for several minutes. Served in a chilled martini glass. No milk, no cream — just coffee and ice and physics.

Ingredients — Per shakerato

  • Double espresso ristretto (just pulled)60 ml
  • Caster sugar10 g
  • Ice (crushed or small cubes)6–8 pieces
  • Cocktail shaker (tin and glass)1
  • Pre-chilled martini glass (150 ml)1

Method — Per shakerato, 90 seconds

  1. Pre-chill martini glass in the freezer for minimum 15 minutes.
  2. Pull a double ristretto. Add sugar immediately while the espresso is hot — the heat helps dissolve the sugar instantly.
  3. Pour hot sweetened espresso into the cocktail shaker. Add ice immediately.
  4. Seal shaker firmly. Shake vigorously for 15 seconds — hard, consistent shakes. You should hear the ice fragments reducing in size. Your hands should get very cold.
  5. Double-strain through the fine-mesh strainer of the shaker into the pre-chilled martini glass. The fine strain removes ice shards while allowing the cold foam to pass.
  6. The foam should be at least 1–1.5 cm deep on top of the drink. A light brown, aerated crema-like foam. If foam is thin or absent, the shaking time was insufficient.
  7. Serve immediately without garnish — the foam is the presentation. Do not add anything that would disturb or sink through the foam.
Chef Technique Note — Coffee Science
The shakerato foam forms because espresso contains surface-active compounds (melanoidins, proteins, phospholipids) that stabilise air bubbles at the liquid surface. Vigorous mechanical agitation incorporates air while the rapid cooling (from ice contact) lowers surface tension, trapping the air. The foam is structurally similar to the hot crema but contains different compounds — more melanoidins, less CO2. Sugar increases the viscosity of the liquid phase, providing additional foam stability. Without sugar, the foam collapses in 30 seconds.
Fail-Proof Tip
Two common failures: 1) Thin or no foam — you are not shaking hard enough or long enough. 15 seconds at full effort should be enough to create crema-level foam from fresh espresso. 2) Foam collapses on pouring — your glass is warm or the double-strain is too slow. Pre-freeze the glass and pour through the strainer in one continuous motion. Also: use fresh espresso only. Coffee that has been sitting even 2 minutes has lost the CO2 and surface-active compounds needed for foam formation. Pull to order, always.
Plating and Serving Note
The shakerato arrives in a chilled martini glass with a deep, stable cold foam on top — no garnish, no decoration. The elegance is in its purity. Place on a small white doily or a folded napkin (the chilled glass will sweat and leave a ring on the bar top). This is the drink for an Italian summer afternoon — serve it ice-cold, serve it immediately, and do not serve it to anyone who asks for milk. That is a different drink and a different conversation.

Limoncello Service
30ml house limoncello, frozen ceramic shot, lemon biscotti
$14.00
Food cost: $1.80 AUD
DF NF
We make our limoncello in-house from Amalfi lemon zest, pure grain neutral spirit, and spring water. It macerates for 30 days in glass — no shortcuts, no commercial extract. The result is a viscous, intensely aromatic limoncello with a natural yellow-gold colour from the lemon oils, not added colouring. It is served at -18°C in a ceramic shot glass that has been stored in the blast freezer — it should arrive at the table with a faint frost on the exterior of the glass. This is the closing ceremony of every meal.

Ingredients — House Limoncello (yields 1 litre)

  • Amalfi lemons (unwaxed) — zest only10 large lemons
  • Pure grain spirit (95% ABV)500 ml
  • Spring water600 ml
  • Caster sugar400 g
  • Glass jar (1.5 litre, airtight)1
  • Per serve — house limoncello30 ml
  • Lemon biscotti (house baked)2 pieces
  • Ceramic shot glass (pre-frozen)1

Method — Maceration: 30 days

  1. Zest the lemons using a Microplane or vegetable peeler — avoid the white pith entirely. The zest should be thin, fragrant, and bright yellow.
  2. Combine zest with grain spirit in the glass jar. Seal airtight. Store in a cool, dark place for exactly 30 days. Do not agitate or open.
  3. After 30 days, the spirit will have turned deep gold-yellow and will be intensely aromatic. Strain through cheesecloth, squeezing the zest firmly to extract maximum oil.
  4. Make simple syrup: combine water and sugar, heat to dissolve, cool completely.
  5. Combine strained spirit with cooled syrup. Stir well. Bottle. Refrigerate for 24 hours minimum — the flavours knit together during this rest period.
  6. Transfer to blast freezer or freeze at -18°C for service. The high sugar content prevents freezing solid — it will remain pourable at -18°C.
  7. Serve: pour 30 ml from the frozen bottle into a ceramic shot glass that has been stored in the blast freezer. The glass should have a light frost when served.
  8. Accompany with 2 lemon biscotti. Place on a small white saucer. Bring to the table after the main dessert is cleared.
Chef Technique Note — Limoncello Science
Lemon essential oils (d-limonene, linalool, alpha-terpineol) dissolve readily in high-ABV ethanol during maceration — they are lipophilic compounds that extract efficiently into alcohol but not water. This is why dilution timing matters: combine the strained spirit with the sugar syrup only after maceration is complete. Adding water before maceration would lower the ABV and significantly reduce the extraction efficiency of the aromatic compounds. The final ABV of house limoncello is approximately 32% — high enough for good flavour preservation, moderate enough for responsible service.
Fail-Proof Tip
The 30-day maceration is not arbitrary — peak aromatic extraction occurs between days 25–35. Under 15 days and the limoncello tastes thin; over 45 days and bitter terpene compounds begin to dominate, making the limoncello harsh. Mark your jar with the date started and date to strain. Also: use unwaxed lemons only. Waxed lemons have a food-grade coating that can extract into the spirit, affecting flavour and clarity. Organic Amalfi lemons are ideal — source from Italian importers when available.
Plating and Serving Note
The limoncello service is the final punctuation of the meal. The frosted ceramic shot, the golden liquid, the two biscotti — it arrives unbidden as a complimentary digestivo gesture, or is ordered specifically. In either case, it signals that the meal is ending and ending well. It is the Italian equivalent of mints with the bill — but one that is actually worth looking forward to. Brief the floor: when a table orders coffee and tiramisu after a long meal, ask if they would like to close with the limoncello service. The conversion rate is very high.

Understanding Gelato — A Technical Primer

Gelato and ice cream are both frozen dairy emulsions but they differ in four critical ways: fat content (gelato: 4–8%, ice cream: 10–16%), overrun (gelato: 20–35%, ice cream: 50–100%), serving temperature (gelato: -8°C to -11°C, ice cream: -14°C to -18°C), and texture at serving (gelato: dense and pliable, ice cream: lighter and firmer). These differences are not merely stylistic — they reflect fundamentally different approaches to flavour delivery. In gelato, lower fat means the flavour compounds are not masked by fat, and lower overrun means each mouthful is denser in flavour. The slightly warmer serving temperature ensures the gelato is paddle-soft at service, yielding immediately on the tongue.

— From the Bologna training notes, 2011

Key Gelato Terms

Term Definition
Overrun % increase in volume from air incorporation during churning. Gelato: 20–35%. Ice cream: 50–100%.
Riposo The resting period (4–12 hours, 4°C) for the base before churning. Allows stabilisers to hydrate and fat to homogenise.
Mantecatura The churning phase in the gelato machine. The paddles simultaneously freeze and incorporate air into the base.
LBG Locust Bean Gum — a natural stabiliser extracted from carob seeds. Prevents large ice crystal formation during storage.
Fior di Latte Literally "flower of milk" — a plain gelato base of whole milk, cream, egg yolks, and sugar with no added flavouring. The purest expression.
Dextrose A monosaccharide (glucose) used to lower the freezing point of gelato bases more efficiently per gram than sucrose.
Brix A measure of dissolved sugar content. Measured with a refractometer. Sorbet target: 28–32 Brix. Gelato base target: 22–26 Brix.
POD (Sweetening Power) The perceived sweetness of a sugar relative to sucrose (sucrose = 100). Dextrose = 75, fructose = 170, trehalose = 45.
AFP (Antifreeze Power) The freezing point depression effect of a sugar. Dextrose AFP = 190 (vs sucrose = 100). Invert sugar AFP = 190.

Weekly Prep Schedule
Weekly Prep Schedule

All prep tasks required to keep a 45-seat gelato and dessert bar at production standard across a 7-day trading week. Gelato bases demand daily attention; pastry prep can batch across 2-day windows where quality allows.

Task Frequency Time Role Notes
Milk gelato base production (fior di latte, custard bases) Daily 14:00 (for next-day churn) Gelato Maestro Pasteurise at 85°C, homogenise, cool to 4°C. Minimum 4h riposo — overnight preferred. Label with batch date/time. Yield: 1kg base per flavour per day.
Egg yolk custard base (pistachio, hazelnut, salted caramel, espresso) Daily 14:00 (for next-day churn) Gelato Maestro 4 egg yolks per kg base. Temper yolks into hot milk at 60°C. Do not exceed 85°C or yolks scramble. Strain through fine chinois. This base requires scrupulous hygiene — check temperature logs daily.
Fruit sorbet base (mango, seasonal citrus, blood orange) Daily or every 2 days (by demand) Flexible — 2h before service Gelato Maestro / Pastry Cook Fruit puree + sucrose + dextrose + stabiliser (locust bean gum). No dairy — allergen safe. Check Brix of fresh mango (min 18° Brix for depth). Adjust sugar if fruit is low-Brix.
Nut paste preparation — pistachio Twice weekly (Mon, Thu) 1.5h Gelato Maestro Bronte pistachios (raw, unroasted) blanched, skinned, dried at 60°C for 30 min, then ground in Robot Coupe with a touch of neutral oil until paste forms. No added sugar in paste — sugar is in the base. Yield: ~400g paste from 500g nuts. Store vacuum-sealed.
Hazelnut paste preparation (nocciola) Weekly (Wednesday) 1h Pastry Cook Use Piedmont (Tonda Gentile) hazelnuts where available. Roast at 160°C for 12 min — skin slips off. Process while warm. Paste should be glossy, not grainy. Cool to room temp before adding to base.
Cannoli shells — fry preparation Daily (fresh per service) 30 min (morning) + to order Pastry Cook Cannoli dough sheeted thin (pasta machine setting 5), cut, wrapped on metal tubes, fried at 180°C for 2 min until golden. Cool on rack. Fill to order only — pre-filled shells go soggy within 20 min. Fry batch of 24 shells AM; replenish as needed. Never pre-fill more than 4 at once.
Ricotta sourcing and quality check Daily (delivery check) or 3x weekly (pickup) On delivery Gelato Maestro Use fresh sheep's milk ricotta (from Sydney Italian deli network) where possible; cow's milk ricotta acceptable for volume. Quality check: smell (fresh, slightly sweet — never sour), texture (creamy but not wet), colour (white, not grey). Drain excess whey through muslin 30 min before use. Never freeze.
Valrhona chocolate delivery and stock check Weekly (Tuesday delivery) 15 min Gelato Maestro Guanaja 70% for stracciatella, Ivoire 35% for white chocolate elements, Caraïbe 66% for cassata coating. Store at 15–18°C, away from light. Check temp of delivery vehicle — rejection threshold is any delivery above 22°C. Minimum 3kg Guanaja on hand at all times.
Limoncello batch production Every 3–4 weeks (3-week maceration) 1h active (+ 3 weeks passive) Gelato Maestro Amalfi lemon zest (no pith) macerated in 95% grain alcohol for 21 days. Add simple syrup (1:1) to dilute to 30% ABV. Strain, bottle, freeze. Stagger batches: start a new batch every 3 weeks so supply is continuous. Track start date on bottle. Yield: approx 2L per batch from 1L alcohol + 10 lemons.
Brioche baking for granita service Daily (batch of 12) 2.5h (incl. proof) Pastry Cook Enrich brioche (butter-heavy, egg-enriched, Sicilian style — slightly sweeter than French brioche). Proof overnight in cool room. Bake at 180°C for 16 min. Best served same-day, sliced horizontally, toasted lightly before granita service. Max 2-day shelf life at room temp (seal in paper bag).
Seasonal flavour development and testing Weekly (Thursday, post-service) 1.5–2h Gelato Maestro (+ Pastry Cook as taster) R&D session for rotating seasonal flavour. Test 1–2 concepts. Evaluate: flavour intensity, texture, overrun, colour, shelf life, allergen profile, cost. Document recipe immediately if approved. Notify FOH of upcoming launch 1 week ahead for return driver script update.
Panna cotta set and amarena cherry stock Every 2 days (batch of 12) 20 min active (+ 4h set) Pastry Cook Panna cotta sets in 150ml glasses in cool room. Top with amarena cherries and syrup just before service — not before. Check set: should hold shape when tilted but tremble gently. Over-set (rubbery) is a failure; under-set (liquid centre) is a failure.
Tiramisu assembly Every 2 days (tray of 9 portions) 45 min Pastry Cook Savoiardi soaked in fresh espresso (not pre-made mixture — pull espresso per batch). Mascarpone cream layered immediately. Must rest minimum 4h before service — 12h preferred for flavour meld. Portion at service; dust with Valrhona cocoa powder to order.

Prep Quality Standard

Every prep task above has a product that fails if rushed. Gelato bases that skip riposo produce a coarser texture — perceptible to any trained palate. Cannoli shells filled in advance become leathery within the hour. Tiramisu portioned too early loses its clean layer edge. The schedule is not bureaucracy — it is the architecture of quality. Do not compress it.

Customer Retention
Customer Retention System

Gelato is not a treat. In the Italian tradition, gelato is a ritual — a weekly punctuation mark in family life. Building that ritual is the highest-leverage thing you can do for long-term revenue.

The Ritual Mindset

In every Italian city, the Sunday-afternoon gelateria walk is non-negotiable. It is not a special occasion — it is a standing date. Grandparents and grandchildren at the same cabinet. The same flavour ordered every week by the same person, and something new tried by the adventurous one. Your goal is to become that place for your Sydney neighbourhood. Not the gelateria for special days. The gelateria for every Sunday.

The families who come every Sunday spend less per visit and more per year than any occasional visitor. A family of four ordering $40 of gelato every Sunday generates $2,080 annually — from one table. Build 30 of those families and you have $62,400 in recurring baseline revenue before a single tourist walks through the door.

Weekend Family Tradition Strategy
🍨 Recognise Regulars by Name
From the third visit, FOH should know the regular family's name and their usual orders. "The Pistachio for Dad, Stracciatella for Mum, Mango Sorbet for the kids?" is not just good service — it is identity confirmation. You are now part of their ritual. This costs nothing.
🗓️ Sunday Morning Tasting Ritual (10:00–11:00)
Every Sunday, offer a free 1-scoop tasting of the seasonal or new flavour for dine-in guests who arrive before 11:00. This is not a formal programme — it is a cultural habit. FOH mentions it in the farewell of every Saturday visit: "Come early Sunday — we'll have the new [flavour] out for tasting." Costs 30–40 scoops. Creates 30–40 reasons to return.
👨‍👩‍👧 Kids' Loyalty Card (Analogue)
Simple stamp card: 10 scoops = 1 free scoop. Exclusively for children. Make it illustrated — a small gelato cart with flavour icons to stamp. The child holds the card and reminds the parents to return. ROI: cost of 1 scoop for every 10 sold. Conversion effect: the child creates the return visit decision.
Seasonal Flavour Launch Events

Run 8–10 times per year. Zero external cost. Maximum community impact. These events are your highest-converting retention tool.

📋 The Tasting List
Keep a simple notebook (or phone note) of regular customers who have expressed interest in new flavours. When a seasonal launch is confirmed, send a personal Instagram DM or SMS to your tasting list: "New flavour launching Saturday — Fig & Marsala. You're on the list for a first taste Thursday 5–7pm if you want to come in." This is not a mass email. It is a personal invitation. Maximum 20 guests per preview.
🎉 Preview Evening Format
Thursday 17:00–19:00. Invite 15–20 regulars. Serve the new seasonal flavour as a tasting flight alongside a glass of house Prosecco ($0 cost — included in the experience). Maestro presents the flavour story in 3 minutes: the ingredient origin, the technical challenge, the decision. Guests feel like insiders. 90% of preview attendees order the flavour on their next standard visit and recommend it to others.
Birthday Cake Pre-Order Program

Gelato cakes are your highest-margin single transaction ($65 retail, ~$18 food cost = 72% GP) and one of the strongest return drivers in the business. Every sold cake is a future visit.

Stage Action Notes
1. Order Collection Guest enquires about birthday cake (triggered by Script 05 or direct enquiry). FOH collects: flavour preferences for each layer (max 3 layers), dietary requirements, collection date/time, name and contact number. Minimum 48h notice required. 72h preferred for custom decoration. Payment in full at order — gelato cakes are non-refundable once production begins.
2. Production Booking FOH logs order in the pre-order book (physical + digital). Notifies Maestro and Pastry Cook same day. Pastry Cook confirms slot in the 18:00 assembly window. Never accept more than 3 gelato cake orders on the same collection day — production capacity constraint.
3. Collection Experience Cake presented in branded box with personalised card from Maestro. FOH gives a brief handling instruction: "Keep flat in the freezer. Remove 5 minutes before cutting. Dip knife in hot water, wipe clean, one smooth cut." Gift-wrap optional ($5 premium). The collection is a service moment, not a handover. Spend 2 minutes with the guest. Every cake collected creates a referral opportunity — "They made this for my daughter's birthday" is your best marketing.
4. Follow-Up Next time the guest visits (usually within 2 weeks — they come back to tell you how it went), FOH asks: "How was the cake? Did people love the pistachio layer?" Note preference. Offer to pre-book next year's birthday. Pre-booking next year's cake at collection converts ~25% of buyers into annual recurring orders. Birthday families are the most loyal cohort in the business.

Gross Profit Mechanics — Why Gelato Works

Gelato overrun is the silent margin booster. A 1kg base at -11°C churned to 20% overrun yields 1.2kg of finished gelato. You are selling 20% more volume than you produced by weight — with zero additional ingredient cost. This is why gelato food cost percentages look lower than comparable pastry items even when using premium ingredients like Bronte pistachio paste. The physics of the product work in your favour. Protect overrun discipline: if staff scoop too generously or cabinet temperature drifts above -8°C and product softens, you lose portion control and margins compress immediately.
