0The Caribbean Pantry Bible — Pepper, Roti & the Soul of the Islands

What is a roti shop? The roti shop is the Caribbean equivalent of the French brasserie — a casual, high-turnover, deeply loved neighbourhood institution where the food is honest, the portions are generous, and the price is always right. Born in Trinidad from the Indian indentured labourers brought to the island after emancipation in 1838, the roti shop fuses Indian flatbread tradition (the roti, the dhal puri, the paratha) with Caribbean stew cooking, Creole spice traditions, and local ingredients like callaloo, breadfruit, plantain, and scotch bonnet pepper. The result is one of the most joyful, complex, and deeply misunderstood cuisines in the world.
The Scotch Bonnet & Wiri Wiri Bible — heat with flavour:

Scotch Bonnet (Capsicum chinense): The defining pepper of the Caribbean. Named for its resemblance to a Scottish bonnet hat. Heat level: 100,000–350,000 Scoville units (10–20× hotter than a jalapeño). But the heat is only half the story: the scotch bonnet has a distinctly fruity, floral, almost tropical aroma when raw that is completely lost if the pepper is cooked in too early or at too high a temperature. The professional technique: add scotch bonnet whole to stews and remove before serving — the heat infuses into the sauce without the full searing intensity. For guests who want maximum heat: slice and serve as a garnish tableside. For maximum flavour extraction: toast a whole pepper directly on a gas flame until charred, then add to the stew.

Wiri Wiri (Capsicum frutescens): Specifically Guyanese. A small, round, red pepper with a more complex, almost berry-like flavour profile underneath intense heat. Essential for authentic Guyanese pepper pot. Less available in US markets than scotch bonnet — source from Caribbean specialty suppliers or grow from seed (they fruit prolifically in containers).

Shadow beni (Eryngium foetidum): Also called culantro or long coriander. The Caribbean's most important herb — used in everything from doubles to stewed chicken to rum punch. It looks like a serrated flat leaf and smells like an intense, slightly more earthy version of coriander. Not interchangeable with coriander — the flavour is deeper and more pungent. Source from Caribbean or Latin grocery stores; grows readily in warm climates.

Green seasoning (the foundational Trinidadian marinade): Blend shadow beni, coriander, chive/green onion, pimento peppers (or mild sweet peppers), garlic, and thyme with a small amount of water or oil. This is the base marinade for virtually every protein dish in the roti shop — chicken, goat, beef, shrimp. Makes 1L batches. Store refrigerated up to 5 days. The green seasoning is what makes Caribbean chicken taste Caribbean rather than merely spiced chicken.

Geera (whole cumin): The most important spice in Trinidadian cooking. Dry-toasted whole until fragrant, then ground fresh. Used in curry goat, curried channa (chickpea), and dhalpuri filling. Pre-ground cumin is an inferior substitute — the volatile oils in whole cumin toast differently and produce a deeper, more complex flavour.

Amchar masala: A Trinidadian spice blend specifically for mango and fruit-based chutneys. Contains roasted cumin, fennel, mustard seed, and fenugreek. Essential for the green mango chutney served with doubles.

Coconut milk (fresh or canned): Used in rundown fish, rice and peas, and many desserts. Fresh coconut milk (blended fresh coconut flesh + water, strained) produces a significantly richer, more flavourful result than canned for cooking applications that require the coconut flavour to be prominent.

Method — The Doubles Masterclass

1
The bara (fried flatbread): Mix plain flour (500g), instant yeast (7g), turmeric (1 tsp — this is the source of the bara's yellow colour), salt, cumin (ground, 1 tsp), and enough warm water to form a soft, slightly sticky dough. Rest 1 hour until doubled. Divide into 30g balls. Flatten each ball in an oiled palm to a thin disc approximately 12cm in diameter — the bara must be thin enough to be slightly translucent when held to light. Deep-fry in oil at 175°C / 350°F for 45–60 seconds, turning once, until golden and puffed. Drain. The bara should be soft, slightly chewy, with the turmeric giving a golden hue and the cumin adding warmth.
2
Curried channa (batch — yields 2kg): Soak dried chickpeas overnight. Drain. In a heavy pot: fry curry powder (3 tbsp — Trinidadian-style, not Indian; contains more cumin and less fenugreek) in oil until fragrant. Add finely minced onion (2 large), garlic (6 cloves), shadow beni (4 sprigs), and chadon beni (4 leaves). Add drained chickpeas. Add water to cover. Simmer 45–60 minutes until chickpeas are completely tender and the curry has thickened around them. Season with salt. The consistency should be thick, not soupy — the chickpeas should hold their shape but be fully yielding. Add a whole scotch bonnet in the final 15 minutes (remove before serving unless high heat is requested).
3
Assembly and the doubles ceremony: In Trinidad, ordering doubles is a ritual. "Slight" (a small amount of pepper), "small" (moderate), "extra" (significant), "slight slight" (almost none). Place a bara on your palm. Spoon curried channa over one side. Lay the second bara on top. Press gently. The customer specifies: chutney? tamarind? pepper? cucumber? The whole transaction takes 15 seconds. The speed is part of the experience. In a US restaurant context: serve as a small share plate with all chutneys alongside. The guest builds their own combination.
💡 Doubles originated in Trinidad in 1936 when Emamool Deen began selling the flatbread-chickpea combination from a cart in Princes Town. Today, hundreds of thousands of doubles are sold daily on the streets of Trinidad — it is the national breakfast. In the US, they are virtually unknown outside Caribbean diaspora communities, which makes them simultaneously the most confusing item to explain and the most explosively popular once a table tries them. Convert rate on tables that try doubles: approximately 85% re-order before leaving.
The bara: soft, slightly chewy, turmeric-yellow, with the warmth of cumin throughout. The curried channa: earthy, warm-spiced Trinidadian curry, chickpeas fully yielding but holding shape, sauce thick and clinging. The chadon beni chutney: green, intensely herby, slightly acidic — it is the brightness that lifts everything. The scotch bonnet pepper sauce: fruity heat, a slow build that arrives after the swallow. A doubles eaten correctly — chutney + tamarind + slight pepper — is a complete flavour composition in two bites.

Method — Desalting and Technique

1
Desalting the saltfish: This is the most important preparation step and the most commonly rushed. Salt cod must be soaked for 24 hours minimum, changing the water at least 3 times (morning, afternoon, evening). After soaking: taste the fish. It should be pleasantly savoury, not aggressively salty. If still too salty: poach in fresh water for 15 minutes. The goal is fish that retains its characteristic flavour and firmness while being comfortable to eat without thirst. Under-desalted accra makes guests drink twice as much water and complain about the next day's sodium intake.
2
Flake the desalted saltfish into small pieces — not minced, not shredded too fine. The fish pieces should be visible in the finished fritter, providing textural contrast to the batter. Combine with flour, green seasoning, scotch bonnet, spring onion, parsley, egg, baking powder, and enough cold water to create a thick dropping batter.
3
Drop tablespoon portions into oil at 175°C / 350°F. The baking powder creates internal bubbles as the fritter fries — the result should be irregular, slightly puffy, with a golden-brown crust. Fry 4 minutes until cooked through. Serve immediately — accra lose their crunch within minutes of coming out of the fryer.
💡 Accra (also called "stamp and go" in Jamaica) exists throughout the Caribbean with regional variations. The Trinidadian version uses green seasoning as the base flavour — the Jamaican version is simpler (saltfish + flour + scotch bonnet). Both are correct. The key quality indicator for accra: the saltfish flavour should be distinctive but not overwhelming, and the fritter should be light despite being fried (the baking powder and cold water are what keep it from being dense).
The exterior: golden, crunchy, slightly irregular. The interior: soft, with visible flakes of saltfish throughout the herb-spiced batter. The saltfish: firm, savoury, deeply flavoured — the desalting process concentrates and mellows the salt cure flavour without removing it entirely. Shadow beni throughout. The scotch bonnet: present as warm heat rather than fire. Serve with chadon beni sauce — the herb brightness amplifies the fish flavour.

Method — Three Textures, One Fruit

1
Tostones (smashed green plantain): Peel green plantain, slice into 2cm rounds. Fry at 160°C / 320°F for 3 minutes until slightly golden but not fully cooked. Remove. Using the flat of a heavy pan or a tostonera, smash each piece to 1cm thickness. Return to oil at 180°C / 360°F for 2 more minutes until crispy and golden. The double-fry creates a crispy exterior with a starchy, yielding interior that single-frying cannot achieve. Season immediately with salt and a squeeze of lime.
2
Maduros (sweet fried ripe plantain): The plantain must be fully ripe — black skinned, the flesh soft and beginning to caramelise internally. Slice diagonally 1cm thick. Fry in a small amount of oil at medium-low heat for 2–3 minutes per side until deeply caramelised and golden. The natural sugars of the ripe plantain caramelise and create a lacquered, sweet exterior. A pinch of sea salt on top immediately after frying amplifies the sweetness.
3
Plantain chips: Slice green plantain paper-thin on a mandoline. Fry immediately in oil at 170°C / 340°F for 90 seconds until crispy and slightly translucent. Season with salt and a pinch of cumin.
Three expressions of the same fruit: the tostones — starchy, slightly salty, with the crunch of the double fry and the lime brightness. The maduros — sweet, lacquered, caramelised, the natural fruit sugars dominant, the salt cutting through. The chips — thin, crispy, neutral starch with cumin warmth. Together they demonstrate why plantain is the most versatile starch in Caribbean cooking.

The bake: Not to be confused with baking in an oven — Trinidadian "bake" is fried bread dough. Mix flour (300g), baking powder (2 tsp), salt, butter (30g), enough water to form a soft, smooth dough. Method — The Curry Goat Masterclass

1
The green seasoning marinade (batch): Blend together shadow beni (culantro — 30g), coriander (20g), garlic (8 cloves), green onion (4 stalks), pimento pepper (2 — mild, sweet, distinctly Caribbean), fresh thyme (4 sprigs), and just enough water to blend. This is the foundational Trinidadian marinade. Coat bone-in goat pieces (shoulder or leg, cut into 5cm pieces) generously with the green seasoning. Add Trinidadian curry powder (2 tbsp — the curry powder is important: it contains more cumin and allspice than Indian curry powder, reflecting the Caribbean adjustment to local palate). Marinate overnight minimum, 24–48 hours preferred. The longer the marinade, the deeper the penetration of flavour into the goat.
2
Cooking the curry goat (batch — 2kg): This is the technique that separates authentic Trinidadian curry goat from generic curry: "burn the curry." Heat oil in a heavy pot over high heat. Add curry powder (3 tbsp) directly into the dry, hot oil — fry for 60–90 seconds, stirring constantly, until the curry blooms and becomes intensely fragrant and slightly darkened. This blooming process activates the fat-soluble volatile compounds in the curry spices that water-based cooking cannot extract. Add marinated goat immediately. Sear in the bloomed curry until the goat pieces are coated and the exterior begins to colour.
3
Add water to just cover. Add whole scotch bonnet (remove before serving for medium heat). Add potatoes (halved) in the final 20 minutes. Simmer covered at medium-low heat for 2–3 hours until the goat is completely tender and falling from the bone. The bone-in cooking is not negotiable — the bone marrow and collagen contribute to the sauce's richness and body. Boneless goat curry lacks the depth of the bone-in version. Season with salt.
4
Dhalpuri roti (batch — yields 8 rotis): The dhalpuri is the most technically demanding of the Caribbean flatbreads. Prepare a soft, smooth dough from flour (500g), baking powder (1 tsp), salt, and water. Rest 30 minutes. Separately: cook yellow split peas until very soft. Drain. Combine with ground geera (cumin, toasted and ground), garlic (minced), and scotch bonnet. Season. The split pea filling should be dry enough to be spread without moisture leaking. Roll each dough ball to a disc, spread split pea filling across the centre, fold the edges over the filling and pinch to enclose. Roll out again to a thin circle. Cook on a hot tawa (flat cast iron griddle) for 90 seconds per side, brushing with oil. The dhalpuri should be golden in patches, pliable, with the split pea filling creating a faint spotted pattern through the dough where the peas are pressed close to the surface.
💡 The key quality indicator for curry goat: the goat should be completely yielding when pushed with a fork — if there is any resistance, the braise is not complete. Goat collagen takes longer to convert to gelatin than beef or chicken. Rushing the braise produces chewy, tough meat that undermines everything. 2.5 hours minimum. Many roti shops cook their goat for 3–4 hours. The sauce should be thick, deeply coloured, rich with the rendered goat fat and bloomed curry.
The dhalpuri: warm, slightly chewy, with the split pea filling adding earthy, cumin-warm depth to every bite of the flatbread itself. The curry goat: the meat falls from the bone at the lightest touch, saturated with the bloomed curry, the green seasoning's herb complexity, and the goat's own gamey richness. The potato: soft, curry-soaked, providing starchy contrast. Wrapped and eaten by tearing sections of roti with your hands — the correct and only way.

Method — Understanding Cassareep

1
Cassareep: This is the single most important and most unusual ingredient in Caribbean cuisine. Cassareep is the reduced juice of the bitter cassava root — the raw juice is toxic (contains hydrogen cyanide) and must be cooked for hours until reduced to a thick, dark, molasses-like syrup. The cooking process destroys the cyanide compounds and produces a concentrated, bitter-sweet, deeply complex liquid that is simultaneously a preservative (due to high tannin content), a colouring agent (dark mahogany), and a flavouring agent of extraordinary depth. Cassareep is what gives Guyanese pepper pot its characteristic dark colour and indefinite shelf life — a properly maintained pepper pot pot can last literally years without refrigeration if brought to a boil daily.
2
The master pot principle: Traditional Guyanese pepper pot is cooked in a pot that is never fully emptied — new meat is added as old meat is consumed, cassareep is replenished, and the pot is brought to a boil every morning. Like the HK master soy stock or the Kau Kee beef broth, the pepper pot develops complexity over time. The pot on Christmas morning in a Guyanese household represents decades of accumulated flavour. For a restaurant: maintain a rolling batch (minimum 3L, topped up daily) rather than cooking from scratch. Source cassareep from Caribbean specialty stores (Goya and other Caribbean brands produce it) or online.
3
Sear meat in batches. In the pepper pot: combine seared meat, cassareep, wiri wiri peppers (whole — they perfume the pot without being consumed), cinnamon, cloves, brown sugar (balances the bitterness of cassareep), water to cover. Simmer minimum 2.5 hours. The meat should be extremely tender and deeply coloured from the cassareep — dark mahogany, almost black at the edges. Serve with white rice and bake for soaking.
💡 Pepper pot is the story dish of the roti shop — the cassareep origin story (toxic raw, complex cooked, ancient Amerindian technology adapted by Caribbean cooks) is extraordinary and guests will discuss it at the table and tell others about it. Describe it as "Guyana's national dish — a slow-cooked beef and pork stew in cassareep, which is a reduced cassava extract used by the Arawak people for thousands of years. The pot is never emptied — we add to it daily." The "pot that's never emptied" concept alone generates conversation.
Pepper pot: dark, intense, simultaneously sweet and bitter from the cassareep, warm-spiced with cinnamon and clove, the wiri wiri peppers leaving a fruity, complex heat throughout the broth. The meat: impossibly tender, dark at the edges, with the cassareep having penetrated every fibre. The rice absorbs the dark broth — each grain is coloured mahogany. This is a stew unlike anything else on earth, produced by a cooking technique that is genuinely ancient and genuinely irreplaceable.

CRITICAL EQUIPMENT:
● Cast iron tawa (60cm minimum) — the non-negotiable tool; must be seasoned and very hot
● Overnight braise oven (runs 140–150°C unattended for oxtail + goat water + pepper pot)
● Heavy-duty charcoal grill (outdoor or ventilated — for jerk)
● Bain marie bank (6 slots minimum) — multiple stews held simultaneously
● Deep fryer (2-basket) — accra, pholourie, plantain, festival, doubles bara
● Blender (commercial) — callaloo, peanut punch, sea moss, green seasoning batches

🫓 Tawa Station — The Heart of the Roti Shop

  • Cast iron tawa heated minimum 20 minutes before service at medium-high
  • Roti dough: made fresh daily, portioned and rested; never refrigerated dough (kills the gluten structure)
  • Dhalpuri: filled, rolled, and cooked per order — maximum 90 seconds per side
  • Buss up shut: clapping the roti while hot is a 10-second technique; practice until second nature
  • Bara (for doubles): flattened to a thin disc and fried immediately in adjacent fryer
  • Never wash the tawa with soap — season with oil after each service
THROUGHPUT: A skilled tawa cook makes 1 roti every 3–4 minutes. At peak service, 2 cooks on the tawa produces 30 rotis per hour — 30 roti wraps per hour is the throughput target.

🥘 Stew Station — The Batch Engine

  • All stews batch-cooked: goat (3–4hrs), oxtail (3–4hrs), pepper pot (rolling batch), stewed chicken (45min)
  • Bain marie holds all proteins at 75°C through service — never reheated per order in a pan
  • Channa: batch-made daily. Must be thick, not soupy. Consistency check before every service
  • Pepper pot: never fully emptied. Add new oxtail / beef daily to the rolling master pot
  • Quality test for every stew before service: can you break it with the back of a spoon? If not, it needs more time

🌶 Sauce & Chutney Station

  • Shadow beni (chadon beni) chutney: blended fresh every morning; maximum 2-day refrigerated shelf life
  • Pepper sauce: scotch bonnet, garlic, lime, mustard — blend weekly; develops better flavour after 3 days
  • Tamarind sauce: batch weekly; sweet-sour balance checked against a standard before each service
  • Mango sour: made morning of service from fresh green mango; never more than 4 hours old
  • Green seasoning: batch weekly (2L); the most important mise en place in the kitchen

🥃 Rum & Drinks Station

  • House ginger beer: rolling batch (new batch every 2–3 days); always taste before service
  • Sorrel concentrate: batch weekly; used for cocktails AND dessert (efficiency)
  • Rum flight boards: pre-set with sal de gusano — sorry, with lime wedge, tasting card, and small water glass
  • Sea moss: blend morning of service; serve within 24 hours
  • Peanut punch: blend to order — 60 seconds; do not pre-batch (separates)

11The Chef's Lens

Essay — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder

I grew up in a city where Caribbean food was invisible. There were dozens of Chinese restaurants, Thai restaurants, Vietnamese restaurants — and somewhere, occasionally, a "West Indian" place that served jerk chicken and rice and peas and called it a day. It took me years of cooking and travelling to understand that what I had been missing was one of the most extraordinary and most joyful food cultures in the world.

The roti shop is not one cuisine. It is the entire history of the Caribbean in edible form. The dhalpuri roti carries the story of indentured Indian labourers brought to Trinidad after the abolition of slavery — their flatbread tradition, their split pea filling technique, their geera (cumin) and curry culture, transplanted to a tropical island and transformed into something that is neither Indian nor not-Indian. The pepper pot carries the story of the Arawak people of Guyana — their cassareep, their bitter cassava alchemy, their preserved pot that outlasts every other cooking technique in the hemisphere. The jerk carries the story of the Maroons of Jamaica — escaped enslaved people living in the Blue Mountains, cooking pork over pimento wood in allspice-scented smoke to feed their communities through generations of resistance. Every dish is a survival story. Every dish is a triumph.

In the US market, this history is almost entirely unknown. The word "Caribbean" on a restaurant sign typically means jerk chicken and rum punch and a vaguely tropical aesthetic. It does not typically mean doubles, or cassareep, or buss up shut, or mauby, or sea moss, or curried crab. This is the opportunity for a first-time operator willing to do the work: be the restaurant that introduces your city to Caribbean food as it actually exists. Not the beach resort version. The street version. The grandmother version. The Saturday morning at the roti shop on the way to the market version.

The roti shop is the most democratic restaurant format in the world. It feeds everyone. It costs almost nothing to eat well there. A doubles and a peanut punch for under $17 is a complete, nourishing, culturally rich breakfast that will sustain you until dinner and leave you thinking about it for days. Build that experience. Tell those stories. The ingredients, the techniques, and the history are all waiting for you in this guide.

The roti shop feeds everyone. Build that. — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder
