1Starters — The Yum Cha Opening Baskets

Starter philosophy: In a traditional dim sum service, there are no "starters" in the Western sense — dishes arrive continuously throughout the meal. However, for a modern dim sum teahouse targeting the US market, a curated opening flight of the most iconic pieces serves as both an introduction to the cuisine and an immediate visual statement. These six items represent the essential Cantonese dim sum canon. If you can make these six correctly, you can make everything else.

SHELLFISHGF (wheat starch — no gluten)

Method — The Benchmark Technique

1
The wrapper: Mix wheat starch and tapioca starch. Pour in boiling water all at once, stir quickly to form a dough. The dough must be worked while still very hot — use a folding motion rather than kneading. Wrap in plastic and rest 5 minutes. Divide into small balls (10g each). Flatten with the flat side of a Chinese cleaver — the specific technique creates the smooth, slightly shiny surface of a proper har gow wrapper. Never use a rolling pin — the cleaver technique creates the right thinness at the edges and thickness in the centre.
2
The filling: Mix chopped and whole prawn pieces (the combination creates texture — uniform chop is flat). Season with ginger, sesame oil, white pepper, light soy, sugar. The filling should taste slightly sweet, fresh, and clean — not heavily seasoned. The prawn flavour is the point. The pleating: Fold the wrapper in half over the filling, then create 7–9 pleats on one side only (the "fan" shape is the har gow trademark). Steam over boiling water in a bamboo steamer, lined with perforated baking paper or oiled steamer cloth, for 6–7 minutes until the wrappers become translucent.
💡 Har gow is the dim sum dish that dim sum chefs are judged on. A traditional evaluation checklist: wrapper thin and translucent (not gummy), 7+ pleats on one side, filling plump and visible through the wrapper, prawn bright pink not grey, wrapper not sticking to steamer. A skilled har gow maker can pleat 10 pieces per minute. Your dim sum chef's speed on har gow determines your kitchen throughput capacity. Audit this specifically during the interview.

Method

1
Combine pork, prawns, shiitake, water chestnuts with seasoning. Mix thoroughly — the protein strands need to develop for proper binding. Refrigerate 30 minutes (the cold temp improves the texture). Place filling in the centre of each round wrapper, then gather the sides up around the filling, gently squeezing at the waist to form the distinctive cylindrical "open cup" shape with filling visible at the top. The waist of a siu mai should be gently pinched — enough to hold the shape, not so much that it splits. Garnish with a small dot of tobiko (flying fish roe) or a tiny piece of carrot for colour. Steam 8–9 minutes.

GLUTENDAIRY (milk)

Method

1
Bao dough: use low-protein flour (Hong Kong bleached flour is traditional — creates the characteristic bright white colour). Mix with sugar, yeast, shortening, milk. Knead 10 minutes until silky. Rest 1 hour. Divide into 30g portions. Flatten, fill with char siu mix, pinch closed. Place on square of parchment, pleat-side up. Proof 20 minutes. Steam over vigorous boiling water 12 minutes. The bao should have a slightly cracked top after steaming — this is a sign of proper fermentation. If the top is completely smooth, the dough was under-proofed.

Method

1

Method

1
Mix rice flour and tapioca starch with water to form a thin batter. Pour a thin layer onto an oiled tray or cheung fun tray, place filling at one end. Steam 3–4 minutes until the rice sheet is just set but still very soft and silky. Roll immediately. The rice sheet must be rolled while hot — a cold sheet will crack. Serve with sweet soy drizzled over, sesame oil, sesame seeds, and sliced green onion. Cheung fun is one of the most technically demanding dim sum items — the batter ratio, the steaming time, and the rolling speed all must be precise.
