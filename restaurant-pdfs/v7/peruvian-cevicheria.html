1Entradas — Peruvian Street Starters

Entrada philosophy: Peruvian starters are built around the concept of freshness and acidity. Ceviche is not cooked with heat — it is "cooked" by acid, a technique developed by pre-Inca civilisations over three thousand years. Every entrada should wake the palate: bright citrus, sharp heat from aji amarillo, the crunch of cancha corn. Start your guests here and the rest of the meal becomes a journey through one of the world's most exciting and underrated cuisines.

Method — Leche de Tigre

1
Cube sea bass. Season lightly with salt. Mix lime juice, aji amarillo paste, grated ginger, minced garlic, pinch of cilantro stems. Taste — should be aggressively sour and spicy.
2
Combine fish with sliced red onion and leche de tigre. Toss gently. Rest exactly 2 minutes — no more. The fish should be barely opaque on the outside, completely raw in the centre. This is correct.
3
Plate in a chilled bowl. Add choclo, cancha, roasted sweet potato slices. Fresh cilantro leaves on top. Serve immediately — ceviche waits for nobody.
💡 The 2-minute rule is sacred. Peruvian ceviche is not marinated — it is dressed and served. Over-marinating makes the fish tough and grainy. The cancha gives the essential crunch. The sweet potato balances the acid. These three elements — fish, crunch, sweet — are the holy trinity of ceviche.

2Mains — The Peruvian Kitchen

Mains philosophy: Peruvian cuisine is the result of one of the most extraordinary cultural collisions in culinary history — pre-Inca ingredients fused with Spanish, Japanese, Chinese, African and Italian influences over five centuries. The result is a cuisine unlike any other: the wok techniques of chifa (Peruvian-Chinese), the acid freshness of ceviche culture, the deep stew tradition of the highlands. Build your mains to honour all of these threads.

Method

1
Get wok screaming hot — no oil first. Season beef strips with salt. Add to dry wok in single layer, no movement for 60 sec. Heavy char on one side only. Remove beef — it is not fully cooked yet.
2
Add oil to hot wok. Add garlic and aji amarillo paste, stir fry 20 sec. Add onion wedges, high heat 60 sec. Add tomato wedges, toss 30 sec — they should soften slightly but hold shape.
3
Return beef. Add soy sauce and vinegar — they will sizzle dramatically. Toss together 30 sec max. Fresh cilantro. Remove from heat immediately.
4
Plate with hot crispy fries (placed half in, half alongside) and steamed rice. The fries absorb the sauce — this is intentional.
💡 Lomo saltado is the most ordered dish in every Peruvian restaurant in the world. It is Peru's national main course. The Chinese wok technique (the "saltado" = sauteed/tossed) came from the Chinese migrants who arrived in Peru in the 1840s. It must be cooked on maximum heat — the wok must smoke. If your wok isn't smoking, it isn't hot enough.

5Kitchen Design — The Peruvian Cevicheria Kitchen

Kitchen design philosophy: A Peruvian cevicheria kitchen is built around two opposing forces: the cold and the hot. The ceviche station is cold — pristine fish, ice contact, acid assembly. The wok and grill station is violently hot — lomo saltado at 300°C+, pollo a la brasa roasting, chowders simmering. These two stations must be separated but connected to a central pass. The ceviche station should ideally be visible — watching fish being dressed is theatre.

The Wok Temperature Rule: Lomo saltado cooked on an underpowered burner is not lomo saltado — it is a beef stew. The wok must be at such high temperature that the beef chars immediately on contact, the sauce steams and caramelises in seconds, and the dish is done in under 3 minutes. The story of Peru is embedded in every dish. Train your team to tell it. Guests who understand the history behind the food eat more, stay longer, and come back sooner.

10Chef's Lens — The Peruvian Cevicheria in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Peruvian Cevicheria in the United States
Peru is the most exciting food country on earth right now. Central in Lima has been ranked #1 restaurant in the world. Gastón Acurio has built a global restaurant empire from Lima. The New York Times calls Peruvian cuisine "the next great food revolution." And yet, outside of Miami, Los Angeles, and New York, there are almost no good Peruvian restaurants in the United States. That is your opportunity — and it is enormous.

The ceviche is your calling card. Every person who has ever eaten great ceviche — the fresh acid, the heat of aji amarillo, the crunch of cancha, the sweetness of choclo — becomes evangelical. They bring friends specifically to show them this dish. They take photographs. They post. Your ceviche, if it is excellent, is your best marketing tool and it costs nothing extra.

The lomo saltado is your anchor. It is the dish that proves Peru invented its own wok cuisine, that Chinese and Peruvian cultures fused in the 1840s and created something entirely new. The theatre of the screaming-hot wok, the caramelised beef, the steam, the chips glistening in the sauce — this dish should make noise in the kitchen and arrive hot enough to force immediate attention at the table.

The pisco is your story. Peru and Chile both claim pisco, but Peruvian pisco — made from Quebranta or Italia or Torontel grapes, distilled once to preserve the grape character — is distinct and magnificent. The Pisco Sour with its foam and Angostura bitters pattern is one of the world's most beautiful drinks. Train your bartenders to make it with obsessive care. Every one that leaves the bar should look and taste perfect.

One thing: aji amarillo is your soul. It is the most distinctive flavour in Peruvian cuisine — fruity, floral, moderately hot, with a depth that no other chilli can replicate. It appears in almost every dish. Source it well. Make your own paste if possible. The difference between house-made aji amarillo paste and commercial paste is immediately obvious to anyone who has eaten in Peru.
The ceviche is the calling card. The lomo is the anchor. The pisco is the story. — KitchenFounder
