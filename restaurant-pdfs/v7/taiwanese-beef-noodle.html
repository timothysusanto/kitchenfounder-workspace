1Small Plates — The Night Market Opener

Small plates philosophy: Taiwanese dining begins with sharing — small plates arrive at the table continuously, ordered throughout the meal rather than in a fixed sequence. This is the night market philosophy: everything comes hot, everything is meant to be shared, and the table should never feel empty. Each small plate should be a complete flavour experience — the scallion pancake is flaky and layered, the pork belly bao is a precise construction of textures, the oyster vermicelli is a study in silky umami. Begin here and set the kitchen's standard.

Method — Michelin Lamination Technique

1
The dough: Combine flour and salt. Pour boiling water over 70% of the flour, mix vigorously. Add cold water to remaining flour. Combine both portions — the mixed-temperature technique creates a dough that is simultaneously flaky and pliable. Rest covered 30 minutes minimum. This rest is critical — the gluten must relax completely or the pancake will spring back and lose its layers during rolling.
2
The lamination: Divide dough into 120g portions. Roll each into a very thin rectangle (2mm thickness — almost translucent). Brush generously with sesame oil mixed with softened lard. Scatter fine-chopped scallion across the entire surface. Season with salt. Roll tightly lengthwise into a log — the roll traps air and fat between layers. Coil the log into a snail shape. Press flat gently. Rest 10 min. Roll out again to 8mm thickness — the layers should be visible in cross-section.
3
The cook: Heat cast iron or heavy steel pan until smoking. Add oil — it should shimmer immediately. Cook pancake on medium-high heat 3–4 min per side until deeply golden with visible char spots. While still hot, clap the pancake between both palms to shatter the layers open — this creates the characteristic shaggy, flaky texture. Cut into 6 wedges. Serve immediately — scallion pancakes die within 2 minutes of sitting.
💡 The Michelin detail: The lard in the lamination is non-negotiable for competition-level texture. Lard has a higher fat content and lower water content than butter — it creates shorter, crispier layers without steam softening them. In a restaurant setting, laminate the dough in bulk at the start of each service day and refrigerate individual portions. The pan must be hot enough to create the Maillard reaction immediately — a medium-temperature pan produces a steamed, soft pancake rather than a shattered, flaky one.

Method — Hong Shao Pork Belly Braise

1
The Hong Shao braise (batch): Cut pork belly into 10cm x 5cm blocks (skin on). Blanch in boiling water 5 min — this removes blood and impurities. Remove, pat dry. In a wok or heavy-based pot, caramelise 80g rock sugar in 30ml oil until deep amber — this is the foundation of the red colour. Add blanched pork belly, skin side down. Coat in caramel 2 min.
2
Add: Shaoxing rice wine 150ml, light soy sauce 80ml, dark soy sauce 40ml (for colour depth), oyster sauce 30ml, 4 cloves garlic, 3 slices ginger, 2 star anise, 1 cinnamon stick, 1 tsp five spice, 2 dried chillies. Add water to just cover. Bring to boil, reduce to gentle simmer. Cook covered 2.5–3 hours at 150°C until a chopstick passes through the fat without resistance. The skin should be gelatinous and trembling.
3
Remove pork, refrigerate overnight — the cold makes slicing clean. Reduce braising liquid to thick, glossy sauce. Slice pork cold to 1cm thickness — the fat and meat layers should be distinct and beautiful. Reheat slices in braising liquid at service.
4
Assembly: Steam bao buns 2 min until pillowy. Brush interior with hoisin. Place pork belly slice, pickled mustard greens, generous crush of peanut, fresh cilantro, fried shallot. The balance of fat, acid, sweet, crunch and herb must be present in every bite.
💡 The Michelin detail: The overnight refrigeration and cold slicing is the difference between a professional bao and an amateur one. Hot pork belly crumbles when sliced — cold pork belly slices cleanly with distinct fat and meat layers that are visually beautiful. The braising liquid must be reduced to glaze consistency — thin sauce produces a wet bao that collapses. The pickled mustard greens (mei cai) cut the fat with precision — do not substitute.

Method

1
The gravy base: Bring pork or dashi broth to simmer. Add soy sauce, Shaoxing wine. Thicken with sweet potato starch slurry until the consistency of a loose nappe — it should coat the back of a spoon but flow slowly. This viscosity is the defining texture of the dish. These are the details that separate an authentic oyster vermicelli from a pale imitation.

SOYGLUTEN (soy)

Method

1
Marinade: Combine chicken with minced garlic, soy sauce, rice wine, five spice, white pepper. Marinate minimum 2 hours — overnight gives better flavour penetration. The soy and rice wine begin to cure the surface of the chicken, concentrating flavour.
2
The coating: Sweet potato starch (not cornstarch) creates a uniquely craggy, shatter-crisp coating. Toss marinated chicken pieces in dry sweet potato starch — the residual marinade moisture will cause the starch to adhere in irregular clumps. This is correct. Do not use a wet batter. Let coated pieces rest 5 min before frying — the starch absorbs surface moisture.
3
The fry: First fry at 160°C / 320°F for 4 min — cook through. Remove. Increase oil to 190°C / 375°F. Second fry 90 seconds until shattering crisp. In the last 20 seconds, throw Thai basil leaves directly into the hot oil — they will crisp instantly with a dramatic sizzle. Remove everything together, drain. Toss with fresh garlic powder and white pepper while hot.
💡 The Michelin detail: The double-fry technique is mandatory for competition-level crispness. The first fry cooks the chicken through at lower temperature without burning the coating. The second fry at high temperature drives the remaining moisture out of the coating, creating the shatter-crisp texture. The sweet potato starch coating is crispier and more irregularly textured than cornstarch — it is what makes Taiwanese fried chicken recognisably different from Japanese or Korean fried chicken.

2Mains — The Beef Noodle Kitchen

Mains philosophy: The centrepiece of any Taiwanese beef noodle bar is the bowl — and the bowl begins with the broth. The red-braised broth (hóng shāo niú ròu tāng) is cooked for 12 hours minimum, building layer upon layer of complexity from doubanjiang, spice, bone, tendon and time. Every main must be approached with the same discipline: the noodles are cooked to order, the broth is tasted before every service, the toppings are precise. This is not fast food — it is a craft that happens to be served in bowls.

Method — The 12-Hour Red Braise (Batch Recipe for 40 Portions)

1
Foundation — the beef preparation: 5kg beef shin and 2kg beef tendon. Blanch all beef in cold water brought to boil — this first blanch removes blood proteins. Discard blanching water completely. Rinse beef under cold water. This step is mandatory — skipping it produces a cloudy, bitter broth.
2
The aromatics: In a large wok or heavy pot, heat 60ml neutral oil until smoking. Fry 150g doubanjiang (Pixian brand — the best available) until the oil turns deep red and fragrant, 3–4 min. Add 80g minced garlic, 60g minced ginger, 4 whole star anise, 2 cinnamon sticks, 1 tbsp Sichuan peppercorn, 3 dried bay leaves, 5 dried chillies. Fry together 2 min. Add 200ml Shaoxing rice wine — it will steam dramatically. Add 100ml dark soy sauce, 60ml light soy sauce, 80ml doubanjiang (additional raw). Add blanched beef.
3
The long braise: Cover beef with cold water plus 20g rock sugar. Bring to rolling boil, skim aggressively. Reduce heat to the lowest possible simmer — the surface should barely move. Cook covered for 10–12 hours. The tendon takes longest — it is ready when it has a translucent, jelly-like quality throughout and offers no resistance to pressure. The shin is ready when it pulls apart along the grain but holds its slice shape.
4
Service assembly: Cook fresh noodles in boiling water 2.5 min. Drain. Blanch bok choy 45 seconds. In warmed bowl: noodles, 500ml strained and tasted broth (adjust salt and soy if needed), beef shin slices fanned, tendon portion, bok choy. Scallion, chilli oil, fried shallot. Chilli bean paste on the side — guests season to taste.
💡 The Michelin detail: Pixian doubanjiang (郫县豆瓣酱) is the single most important ingredient in this broth. It is fermented for a minimum of 3 years in Sichuan — the longer the fermentation, the deeper the flavour. Do not use other brands. The broth should be tasted by the head chef before every single service — it is a living product that changes as it concentrates during service. The fat floating on the surface of the broth is not a flaw — it is flavour. Serve it as it is.

Method — Crystal Clear Broth Technique

1
The clear broth (batch): 3kg beef bones and 2kg beef shin, blanched and cleaned as above. Place in cold water, bring to gentle simmer — never boil. Skim continuously for 20 min until no more foam rises. Add: 6 slices ginger, 4 scallion sections, 2 star anise, 1 cinnamon stick, 100ml Shaoxing wine, 30ml light soy. Simmer at 95°C / 203°F — the broth must never boil or it will turn cloudy. Cook 12 hours. The result is a golden, crystal-clear broth of extraordinary depth.
2
Strain through fine mesh and then through clean muslin cloth. The clarity should be pristine — you should see the bowl beneath. Season with salt and a small amount of light soy. Three drops of sesame oil at service — no more or it dominates.
3
Serve with sliced beef shin, noodles, scallion, pickled mustard greens on the side. The purity of this bowl communicates technique more than any sauce can.
💡 The Michelin detail: The Taiwanese clear broth noodle is the dish that separates serious kitchens from casual ones. The colour of the broth — a deep amber gold, completely transparent — is the visual communication of technical skill. It requires discipline: 95°C maximum throughout, continuous skimming in the first hour, quality bones, and patience. Never rush this broth. The guests who order it are the most discerning guests in your dining room.

10Chef's Lens — The Taiwanese Beef Noodle Bar in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Taiwanese Beef Noodle Bar in the United States
Taiwan is having its moment in the United States. Michelin has starred restaurants in Taipei. Kavalan whisky has beaten Scottish distilleries at the World Whisky Awards. Taiwanese bubble tea has become one of the fastest-growing food and beverage categories in America. And yet authentic Taiwanese cuisine — not Chinese-American, not pan-Asian, but specifically Taiwanese — remains rare outside of a handful of cities.

The beef noodle soup is the dish that defines the opportunity. There is a competition held every year in Taipei judging the best beef noodle in the city, with hundreds of entries. The dish is taken with an intensity that other countries reserve for wine or bread. This is the cultural weight behind a bowl of beef noodle — it is a national obsession, a point of pride, and a deeply technical craft. When you get it right in the United States, you have no competition.

The broth is where the discipline begins and ends. Every morning, as the broth comes to temperature, it must be tasted. Not by a commis — by the head chef. The doubanjiang from Pixian, the star anise balance, the reduction level, the salt — these must be assessed and adjusted before every single service. This is not optional routine. This is the daily act of craft that makes a great noodle bar.

The bubble tea program is your gateway. In the United States in 2025, bubble tea is how younger consumers discover Taiwanese culture. The brown sugar milk tea with tiger-stripe syrup is on TikTok constantly. The travellers who visited Taiwan in their 20s and tried their first fresh boba are now in their 30s and spending money in restaurants. These are your guests — curious, food-educated, willing to explore. Build your social media presence around the bubble tea visual and let the food discovery follow.

One final thought: serve the pineapple cake with the cheque. Every time. At $0.40 cost, it is the most meaningful small gesture in your service. It says: we know Taiwanese culture, we respect it, and we want you to leave with something. Guests who receive it for the first time always ask what it is. That question is the beginning of every return visit.
The broth is the discipline. The noodle is the craft. The bubble tea is the invitation. — KitchenFounder
