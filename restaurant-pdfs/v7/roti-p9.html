11The Chef's Lens

Essay — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder

I grew up in a city where Caribbean food was invisible. There were dozens of Chinese restaurants, Thai restaurants, Vietnamese restaurants — and somewhere, occasionally, a "West Indian" place that served jerk chicken and rice and peas and called it a day. It took me years of cooking and travelling to understand that what I had been missing was one of the most extraordinary and most joyful food cultures in the world.

The roti shop is not one cuisine. It is the entire history of the Caribbean in edible form. The dhalpuri roti carries the story of indentured Indian labourers brought to Trinidad after the abolition of slavery — their flatbread tradition, their split pea filling technique, their geera (cumin) and curry culture, transplanted to a tropical island and transformed into something that is neither Indian nor not-Indian. The pepper pot carries the story of the Arawak people of Guyana — their cassareep, their bitter cassava alchemy, their preserved pot that outlasts every other cooking technique in the hemisphere. The jerk carries the story of the Maroons of Jamaica — escaped enslaved people living in the Blue Mountains, cooking pork over pimento wood in allspice-scented smoke to feed their communities through generations of resistance. Every dish is a survival story. Every dish is a triumph.

In the US market, this history is almost entirely unknown. The word "Caribbean" on a restaurant sign typically means jerk chicken and rum punch and a vaguely tropical aesthetic. It does not typically mean doubles, or cassareep, or buss up shut, or mauby, or sea moss, or curried crab. This is the opportunity for a first-time operator willing to do the work: be the restaurant that introduces your city to Caribbean food as it actually exists. Not the beach resort version. The street version. The grandmother version. The Saturday morning at the roti shop on the way to the market version.

The roti shop is the most democratic restaurant format in the world. It feeds everyone. It costs almost nothing to eat well there. A doubles and a peanut punch for under $17 is a complete, nourishing, culturally rich breakfast that will sustain you until dinner and leave you thinking about it for days. Build that experience. Tell those stories. The ingredients, the techniques, and the history are all waiting for you in this guide.

The roti shop feeds everyone. Build that. — Timothy Susanto, KitchenFounder
