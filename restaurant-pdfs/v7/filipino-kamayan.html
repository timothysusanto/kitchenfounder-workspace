Mains philosophy: Kamayan means "eating with hands" in Filipino — the traditional feast where food is laid out on banana leaves and eaten communally without utensils. In a US restaurant, the kamayan feast is your highest-revenue table experience: a spread of lechon, adobo, kare-kare, grilled meats, and rice laid out on banana leaf-lined tables for groups of 4–10. SOYGLUTEN (soy sauce)GF (request)

Method — The Two-Stage Technique

1
Stage 1 — Braise (batch, 2.5 hours): Simmer whole pork belly slab skin-side down in water with soy sauce, garlic, bay leaf, black pepper for 2–2.5 hours until pork is completely tender but holds its shape. Remove, cool on rack in refrigerator overnight uncovered — the cold air dries the skin surface. This uncovered overnight drying is critical for the crackling step.
2
Stage 2 — Deep fry to order: Slice braised belly into 5–6cm thick slabs. Deep fry at 185°C / 365°F for 6–7 min until the skin blisters and crackles explosively. The skin should swell and become completely opaque white. Stand back when placing in oil — braised pork contains residual moisture that causes intense bubbling.
3
Drain on rack — never on paper (paper makes skin soggy). Chop or serve whole slab. Serve immediately with liver sauce, calamansi, and fried rice. The window from fryer to table is 3 minutes maximum — beyond this, the crackling softens.
💡 The Michelin detail: Lechon kawali's crackling is achieved through three separate drying mechanisms acting together: the overnight refrigerator drying removes surface moisture from the skin; the soy sauce braise adds a thin protein layer that blisters under heat; and the frying temperature causes the remaining moisture to flash-evaporate into steam bubbles that puff the skin. All three must happen. Miss any one step and you get soft, chewy skin rather than the signature shatter. The overnight drying step is the most commonly skipped — and the most important.

Method

1
Marinate chicken and pork in soy sauce, vinegar, garlic, bay leaf, peppercorns overnight if possible (minimum 2 hours). The vinegar acts as both flavouring and natural preservative — traditional adobo was developed in tropical Philippines before refrigeration.
2
Brown marinated meats in oil at high heat — the soy marinade caramelises and creates a dark, savoury crust. Add all marinade liquid + enough water to just cover. Braise covered at low simmer 45 min. Remove lid, increase heat — reduce sauce until thick and glossy, coating the meat. The sauce should cling, not pool.
3

Your version should be confident and specific — own your recipe, explain it with pride, and never apologise for it. The garlic cloves must be whole and crushed, not minced — whole cloves soften into buttery pillows that are one of the great small pleasures of eating adobo.

Method

1
Oxtail braise (overnight): Blanch oxtail in boiling water 10 min, discard water. Braise in fresh water with garlic, onion for 4–5 hours until meat is falling off bone and the collagen has completely released into the broth. The broth should be thick and gelatinous when cooled — this collagen is the base of the sauce.
2
Peanut sauce: Sauté onion and garlic in oil. Add oxtail broth, annatto water, natural peanut butter — whisk until smooth and combined. Add toasted ground rice to thicken. 3
Add braised oxtail pieces, blanched eggplant, long beans, banana blossom to sauce. Simmer 5 min. The vegetables must retain their integrity — not overcooked. Serve in clay pot or wide bowl with fried rice. The bagoong (fermented shrimp paste) arrives separately — guests stir it into the kare-kare to their own taste. 💡 The Michelin detail: Kare-kare is the dish that separates serious Filipino restaurants from casual ones. The overnight oxtail braise is non-negotiable — the collagen release from 4+ hours of cooking creates the sauce's body and richness that no shortcut can replicate. The bagoong served alongside is equally essential: its fermented, pungent saltiness cuts through the rich peanut sauce in a way that no other condiment can. Guests new to Filipino food will be cautious about the bagoong — train your team to say: "Start with a small amount and stir it in. It sounds unusual but it's the most important bite of the dish."

Method

1
Blanch ribs in boiling water, drain. Simmer in fresh water 1.5 hours until tender. Add tamarind paste dissolved in water — the broth should be notably, pleasantly sour. This sourness is the entire point of sinigang. Add onion, tomato. Simmer 10 min. Season with fish sauce.

Overcooked spinach destroys the freshness of the broth.

Method — The Sizzle Plate Technique

1
Pork preparation (batch): Braise pork cheek or belly until very tender. Cool, then grill or pan-fry until well caramelised and slightly crispy on the outside. Chop into rough 1cm pieces — the irregular chop creates texture variation. Mix with raw onion, chilli, soy sauce, vinegar, chicken liver.
2
The sizzle plate: Heat a cast iron sizzle plate until extremely hot (190°C+). Add a small amount of butter. Pile the sisig mixture onto the sizzle plate — it should sizzle loudly. Crack an egg directly on top of the sizzling mixture. Carry to table immediately — the egg continues cooking on the hot plate at the table.
3
At table: serve with calamansi halves. The guest squeezes calamansi over the sizzling sisig and mixes the egg in themselves. The mayo is a modern addition — offer on the side. The combination of pork fat, egg, calamansi, raw onion and chilli is perfectly balanced.
💡 The Michelin detail: Sisig was invented in Pampanga, Central Luzon — a province famous for its cuisine. The original recipe used pig's face (cheeks, ears, snout) which gave it a unique texture combination. Pork cheek is an excellent substitute — it has the collagen and fat content of the original without the operational challenge of sourcing whole pig faces. The tableside egg-cracking is essential theatre — it must happen in front of the guest, not in the kitchen. The sound of the sizzle when the plate arrives is your advertisement to the entire room.

5Kitchen Design — The Filipino Kamayan Kitchen

6Service Training Module

Service philosophy: Filipino hospitality is characterised by warmth, generosity and the concept of "kapwa" — shared identity, the feeling that you and the guest are not separate but connected. Filipino hosts go to extraordinary lengths to make guests feel welcome and fed. Your team should embody this: not simply taking orders, but genuinely ensuring every guest has enough food, is curious about what they're eating, and leaves feeling cared for. The word "po" (a Filipino honorific) reflects this — train your team to bring warmth that feels culturally informed, not performative.

8Customer Retention System

Retention philosophy: The Filipino-American community is the third-largest Asian-American group in the US (over 4 million people) and is known for intense loyalty to Filipino businesses. When a Filipino restaurant gets it right — the food is authentic, the ube colour is real, the leche flan wobbles — the word spreads through the community rapidly. Filipino community groups, church groups, and family celebrations drive significant event bookings. Your retention strategy should have two tracks: community (Filipino-American diaspora) and discovery (food-curious non-Filipino guests). Both require different messaging but both respond powerfully to authenticity and warmth.

Using "salamat" (a word all Filipino-Americans know and most non-Filipinos find delightful) is warm and culturally specific. The kamayan upsell is strongest when the guest has already experienced the food — they know the flavours and are now invited to upgrade the experience.

Whole pig lechon is the most powerful Filipino food experience available — it cannot be done at home easily, it requires a group, and it generates extraordinary social media content. Run monthly or quarterly. Price at $45pp minimum — it is worth it and guests know it.

10Chef's Lens — The Filipino Kamayan Restaurant in Context

// A Working Chef's Perspective on Opening a Filipino Kamayan Restaurant in the United States
Filipino-Americans have been in the United States in significant numbers since the 1960s — and for most of that time, Filipino food remained largely invisible to the broader American dining public. Pinoy restaurants served the community beautifully, but the food did not cross over. That changed sometime around 2015–2018, when ube went viral, when lumpia started appearing on non-Filipino menus, when Jollibee opened in New York and lines wrapped around the block.

What drove the crossover? Social media, partly. The purple colour of ube is undeniably photogenic. The image of hands reaching across banana leaves is immediately warm and human in a way that a plate on a white tablecloth simply is not. But more fundamentally, Filipino food crossed over because it is genuinely, deeply delicious — and America was finally paying attention.

The opportunity now is to build the Filipino fine-casual restaurant that serves both communities at once. The Filipino-American guest who grew up with their lola's lechon and adobo will test you immediately and fiercely. If the crackling shatters, if the leche flan wobbles, if the kare-kare is rich enough and the bagoong is present and uncompromising — you will have a loyal guest for life. The non-Filipino guest will arrive curious, order cautiously, eat adventurously, and leave evangelical.

A few things I would tell any chef opening a Filipino restaurant: The lechon kawali skin is your daily test of kitchen discipline. If you are skipping the overnight drying step, your guests will know, even if they cannot say why — the skin will be leathery rather than shattering. Do not skip it. The bagoong alongside kare-kare is non-negotiable — guests who have never tried it will be hesitant, but the combination of rich peanut stew and intensely fermented shrimp paste is one of the most extraordinary flavour contrasts in world cuisine. Sell it with complete confidence.

And finally: the banana leaves are not decoration. They are the meal. When warm food sits on banana leaves, the leaves gently steam and perfume everything on them with a faint green, tropical fragrance that is completely unique to Filipino and Southeast Asian communal eating. That fragrance is your restaurant's opening statement — and you cannot buy it with any amount of interior design.
The crackling is the discipline. The banana leaf is the invitation. The bagoong is the truth. — KitchenFounder
