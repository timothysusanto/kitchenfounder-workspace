*The Greek Pantry Bible

Why this section matters: Greek cooking is built on ingredient quality, not complexity. The olive oil, the feta, the dried oregano, the lemon — these are not supporting ingredients, they ARE the dish. A Greek restaurant that uses inferior olive oil, pre-crumbled feta, or dried parsley instead of oregano is not a Greek restaurant. Sourcing is everything. This pantry bible tells you what to source, why, and what to never substitute.
Extra Virgin Olive Oil (Greek)
The single most important ingredient. Greek EVOO (Kalamata, Crete) has the highest polyphenol content of any olive oil in the world. Use for everything — cooking, dressing, finishing. Never use vegetable oil as a substitute. Cost: ~$18–22/litre wholesale. Every cent is worth it.
Feta PDO
Protected Designation of Origin — by EU law, genuine feta is made only in specific Greek regions from sheep's milk (or up to 30% goat). It has a crumbly, creamy texture and salty-tangy flavour. Pre-crumbled "feta-style" cheese is a pale substitute. Source block feta in brine. Crumble in-house only.
Dried Greek Oregano
Greek mountain oregano (rigani) is more pungent, more floral, and more complex than Italian or Turkish oregano. It is not the same herb. Used in lamb marinades, horiatiki, grilled fish, and souvlaki. Source specifically Greek dried oregano — it is cheap and transformative.
Kalamata Olives
The DOP-protected olive from Messinia, Peloponnese. Dark purple-black, almond-shaped, rich and fruity with a subtle bitterness. Never substitute with generic black olives. Kalamata olives in a horiatiki salad are the flavour foundation of the dish — the generic canned black olive ruins it completely.
Kefalotyri / Graviera Cheese
Hard, salty Greek sheep's milk cheese used for saganaki (fried cheese). Must withstand high heat without melting through. Kefalotyri is the traditional choice; graviera (a milder, slightly sweet version) is more approachable. Both are correct. Never use halloumi — different texture, different result.
Mastiha (Mastic)
Pine resin from the mastic trees of Chios island — one of the world's most unique flavourings. Used in Greek sweets, liqueur (Mastiha liqueur), and some desserts. Adds a clean, resinous, slightly minty depth. A small jar lasts months. Use in loukoumades glaze, Greek coffee, and the mastiha cocktail.

1Starters — Saganaki, Spanakopita & Mezedes

Starter philosophy: Greek starters are mezedes — small dishes designed for sharing, grazing, and ordering more of as the evening progresses. The saganaki (flambéed cheese) is your most theatrical and most-ordered starter. The spanakopita is your most culturally anchored. Serve both with house-made pita bread and Greek olive oil — never charge for bread at a Greek taverna.

Method — The OPA Moment

1
Slice kefalotyri into a thick slab (2-3cm). Soak briefly in cold water (prevents burning). Pat dry. Dust very lightly with flour on both sides. Heat olive oil in a heavy skillet or small saganaki pan until very hot. Sear cheese on both sides until a deep golden crust forms — 2 min per side. The crust holds the melting cheese together.
2
The tableside flambé: Bring the hot pan to the table. Pour ouzo or brandy over the cheese. Using a long match or lighter, ignite the alcohol immediately — the blue flame engulfs the pan for 10-15 seconds. When the flame dies, squeeze fresh lemon over the cheese. The classic call is "OPA!" — your servers should say it at ignition. The table will applaud.
💡 The saganaki is the single most room-stopping moment in a Greek restaurant. When a server carries a flaming pan through the dining room, every other table looks up. It is your best free marketing — every guest who sees it wants one. Train every server on the flambé. Do it tableside without exception. A saganaki fired in the kitchen and walked out cold is a failed opportunity. The theatre IS the product.

Method — The Three Rules of Spanakopita

1
Rule 1 — Squeeze the spinach. After wilting, squeeze every drop of water from the spinach with your hands, then squeeze again in a clean cloth. Wet spinach makes soggy phyllo. The filling must be completely dry.
2
Rule 2 — Crumble, don't blend the feta. Feta should be crumbled into small but visible pieces — not mashed into a paste. The textural contrast between creamy feta pockets and cooked spinach is essential.
3
Rule 3 — Brush every single phyllo layer. 4-6 layers of phyllo, each brushed with melted butter or olive oil. The layers should separate when baked into distinct flaky sheets. Roll or fold into triangles. Bake at 185°C / 365°F for 20-25 min until deep golden. Cool 5 min before serving — the interior sets and the phyllo crisps fully.
💡 Authentic spanakopita uses dill, not parsley. This is the single most common mistake in non-Greek recipes. Dill + feta is the Greek combination that defines the flavour profile. Nutmeg adds depth that guests won't consciously identify but will miss if absent. Make in large batches, bake fresh daily, hold at room temperature (not refrigerated) — reheated refrigerated spanakopita loses its crispness.

2Mains — Souvlaki, Moussaka & the Open Fire

Mains philosophy: Greek taverna cooking is built on two foundations — the grill (the souvlaki, lamb chops, octopus, branzino) and the slow oven (moussaka, kleftiko, pastitsio, gigantes). Both are equally important. The grill is your highest-volume, fastest-throughput station. The slow oven produces your most impressive, most talked-about dishes. Staff both correctly and you have a kitchen that can handle volume without sacrificing quality.

Method — The Overnight Marinade

1
Marinate lamb cubes overnight minimum (24 hours is better) in olive oil, Greek oregano, lemon juice and zest, garlic, and salt. The acid in the lemon tenderises the outer surface while the oregano penetrates. The next day the lamb should be deeply aromatic before it even touches the grill.
2
Thread onto metal skewers (wood skewers lose their shape at the temperatures needed). Grill on a very hot charcoal or gas grill — the lamb must sear and char at the edges while the interior remains just-pink. Total grill time: 10-12 min, turning every 2-3 min. The char marks and slight char flavour are not a mistake — they are the correct result.
3
Rest 3 min. Serve on grilled pita with tzatziki, sliced tomato, onion, and chopped parsley. A squeeze of fresh lemon at service is not optional — it lifts every component.
💡 The single difference between great Greek souvlaki and average souvlaki is the overnight marinade and the very high heat at service. The marinade must be 24+ hours. The grill must be screaming hot. A properly made souvlaki has char marks, a slightly crispy exterior, a just-pink interior, and an oregano-lemon perfume that hits the entire dining room when it leaves the kitchen. At $24.99 with 13.2% FC, souvlaki is your highest-volume main and your lowest food cost protein dish.

Method — The Three Layers

1
Layer 1 — Potato: Slice potato 5mm thick, brush with olive oil, roast at 200°C / 392°F until just cooked and golden. This is the base layer — it absorbs the meat juices during the final bake.
2
Layer 2 — Lamb mince: Brown lamb mince with onion, garlic. Add crushed tomato, cinnamon (this is the defining Greek spice in moussaka — not optional), allspice, bay, salt. Simmer 30 min until completely reduced and thick. Then layer slices of roasted aubergine on top of the potato base, followed by the lamb mince.
3
Layer 3 — Béchamel: Make a thick béchamel (butter + flour + milk + generous nutmeg). Off the heat, stir in 2 egg yolks — this enriches the sauce and sets it to a custard-like consistency when baked. Pour generously over the lamb layer, smooth, grate cheese on top. Bake at 175°C / 347°F for 35-40 min until the béchamel is deeply golden and set. Critical: rest moussaka 20-30 min before portioning — it needs to set or it collapses when cut. Reheat per-portion. Never serve moussaka hot from the oven — it will fall apart.
💡 The cinnamon in moussaka is not a mistake and not subtle. Greek lamb mince for moussaka is quite heavily cinnamon-spiced — more than guests from non-Greek food backgrounds expect. This is correct and intentional. If guests comment on it, explain: "The cinnamon is how Greek grandmothers have always made moussaka — it's what makes it unmistakably Greek rather than Italian lasagne. The spice balance is very deliberate."

Method

1
Kleftiko means "stolen" — legend says Greek mountain rebels cooked lamb sealed in clay pits to hide the cooking smell from Ottoman soldiers. The sealed cooking vessel is the technique that makes kleftiko extraordinary. Stud the lamb shoulder deeply with garlic slivers and rosemary sprigs. Method — The Braise-then-Grill Technique

1
Never grill raw octopus — it will be rubbery and almost inedible. The correct technique is two-stage: braise first (simmer whole octopus in water with wine vinegar and bay for 45-60 min until tender — test with a skewer through the thickest part, it should meet no resistance). Cool in the braising liquid. Then grill the pre-braised tentacles on screaming-hot grill until charred and slightly crispy on the outside. The braise creates tenderness; the grill creates the char flavour and the visual drama.
💡 Grilled octopus on a white plate with capers, red onion, lemon, and a drizzle of olive oil is one of the most photographed dishes in Greek restaurants globally. The purple-black tentacles with char marks against the white plate is a striking visual. Put this on your Instagram consistently — it will drive more traffic than any ad spend. Always grill from the pre-braised state, always at maximum grill heat, always with visible char marks.

Method — The Five Rules

1
Rule 1: No lettuce. A real horiatiki has no lettuce. Ever.
Rule 2: Feta goes on top as a whole slab — never pre-crumbled into the salad. The guest breaks it themselves.
Rule 3: No vinegar dressing. Only Greek EVOO and a pinch of salt. The tomato and olive brine provide all acidity needed.
Rule 4: Ripe tomatoes only — if your tomatoes are not ripe and flavourful, do not put this on the menu.
Rule 5: Dried Greek oregano (rigani) sprinkled over the feta slab at the very end — not mixed in, not substituted with fresh oregano.
💡 The horiatiki is the most common Greek dish ordered in non-Greek restaurants and almost universally made incorrectly in non-Greek restaurants. Your version — with a whole slab of authentic PDO feta, ripe tomatoes, real Kalamata olives, and Greek EVOO — will taste completely different from the "Greek salad" your guests have had elsewhere. That difference is the conversation. Explain the rules to guests who are curious.

Method — The Hot Pan / Cold Syrup Rule

1

2

6Service Training — Greek Hospitality Scripts

Service philosophy: Greek hospitality — philoxenia (literally "love of strangers") — is one of the most deeply culturally embedded hospitality traditions in the world. In ancient Greece, Zeus himself was the protector of guests (Zeus Xenios). In a restaurant context, this means warmth is not optional, it is the table stakes. Every guest should feel they are being welcomed into a Greek home, not just a commercial restaurant. The food arrives with stories. The saganaki arrives with theatre. The wine arrives with education.
// Opening Script — Table Arrival
"Welcome — can I get you some pita bread and olive oil while you look at the menu? It's on us. Greek tradition — you never let a guest sit at the table without something in front of them. Now, a few things to know about our menu: the saganaki is our flamed cheese starter — I'll bring it to the table and light it myself, so if you see a small fire coming your way, don't worry. And our lamb souvlaki has been marinating since yesterday morning. Can I tell you about our wine? We have some really interesting Greek wines that most people haven't tried."
Sets the philoxenia tone (free bread = Greek tradition), explains the saganaki theatre in advance, signals the souvlaki quality, pivots immediately to the wine programme — the most important revenue item on the table.
// Saganaki Introduction Script
"Saganaki is kefalotyri cheese — Greek sheep's milk cheese that can take high heat. I'll sear it in the pan until it's golden, then pour ouzo over it and light it at the table. When the flame dies I'll squeeze lemon on it — the lemon stops the cooking. The Greek word at that moment is OPA — it means something like 'here we go' or 'hooray.' The whole table usually joins in. Shall I put one in for the table to share?"
Explains what the dish is, creates anticipation for the OPA moment, ends with a natural table-share sell. At $12.99 with 13.9% FC, every saganaki starter sold to a shared table is high-margin revenue.
// Assyrtiko Wine Introduction Script
"For wine — can I tell you about the Assyrtiko from Santorini? It's a white wine grown in volcanic ash, which gives it this incredible mineral, almost saline quality — people describe it like 'the taste of the Aegean sea.' The vines are over 200 years old and trained in a basket shape on the ground to survive the island wind. It's genuinely one of the most unique wines in the world and most people have never heard of it. It pairs perfectly with the octopus and the branzino. $15 a glass, or $55 the bottle."
The volcanic ash + 200-year-old vines + basket training story is memorable, educational, and creates genuine intrigue. This is the server who earns tips from wine — not from describing food people can already read on the menu.
// Kleftiko Pre-Sell Script
"If you're coming with three or more people — we can do the whole lamb shoulder. It's our kleftiko, which is a Greek mountain cooking method where the lamb is sealed inside foil or clay for five hours. The word kleftiko means 'stolen' — Greek mountain rebels used to cook lamb in sealed pits to hide the smell from Ottoman soldiers. We unwrap it at the table and the lamb just falls off the bone. It's $46.99, feeds two to three. Requires 24 hours notice to book. Are there more of you coming?"
Embeds the story (legend is memorable), sets the expectation of the reveal, explains the price before the ask. At $46.99 with 19.3% FC, kleftiko is a high-value group booking driver.
