1Starters — 6 Recipes

Starters philosophy: Caribbean starters are street food elevated — doubles, pholourie, accra (saltfish fritters), and festival are all roadside or market foods that have been perfected over generations by vendors competing for the same customer. In a roti shop context, these dishes are served as they are on the streets of Port of Spain or Georgetown: fast, hot, perfectly seasoned, and at prices that feel like a gift.

Method — The Doubles Masterclass

1
The bara (fried flatbread): Mix plain flour (500g), instant yeast (7g), turmeric (1 tsp — this is the source of the bara's yellow colour), salt, cumin (ground, 1 tsp), and enough warm water to form a soft, slightly sticky dough. Rest 1 hour until doubled. Divide into 30g balls. Flatten each ball in an oiled palm to a thin disc approximately 12cm in diameter — the bara must be thin enough to be slightly translucent when held to light. Deep-fry in oil at 175°C / 350°F for 45–60 seconds, turning once, until golden and puffed. Drain. The bara should be soft, slightly chewy, with the turmeric giving a golden hue and the cumin adding warmth.
2
Curried channa (batch — yields 2kg): Soak dried chickpeas overnight. Drain. In a heavy pot: fry curry powder (3 tbsp — Trinidadian-style, not Indian; contains more cumin and less fenugreek) in oil until fragrant. Add finely minced onion (2 large), garlic (6 cloves), shadow beni (4 sprigs), and chadon beni (4 leaves). Add drained chickpeas. Add water to cover. Simmer 45–60 minutes until chickpeas are completely tender and the curry has thickened around them. Season with salt. The consistency should be thick, not soupy — the chickpeas should hold their shape but be fully yielding. Add a whole scotch bonnet in the final 15 minutes (remove before serving unless high heat is requested).
3
Assembly and the doubles ceremony: In Trinidad, ordering doubles is a ritual. "Slight" (a small amount of pepper), "small" (moderate), "extra" (significant), "slight slight" (almost none). Place a bara on your palm. Spoon curried channa over one side. Lay the second bara on top. Press gently. The customer specifies: chutney? tamarind? pepper? cucumber? The whole transaction takes 15 seconds. The speed is part of the experience. In a US restaurant context: serve as a small share plate with all chutneys alongside. The guest builds their own combination.
💡 Doubles originated in Trinidad in 1936 when Emamool Deen began selling the flatbread-chickpea combination from a cart in Princes Town. Today, hundreds of thousands of doubles are sold daily on the streets of Trinidad — it is the national breakfast. In the US, they are virtually unknown outside Caribbean diaspora communities, which makes them simultaneously the most confusing item to explain and the most explosively popular once a table tries them. Convert rate on tables that try doubles: approximately 85% re-order before leaving.
The bara: soft, slightly chewy, turmeric-yellow, with the warmth of cumin throughout. The curried channa: earthy, warm-spiced Trinidadian curry, chickpeas fully yielding but holding shape, sauce thick and clinging. The chadon beni chutney: green, intensely herby, slightly acidic — it is the brightness that lifts everything. The scotch bonnet pepper sauce: fruity heat, a slow build that arrives after the swallow. A doubles eaten correctly — chutney + tamarind + slight pepper — is a complete flavour composition in two bites.

Method

1
Blend soaked split peas to a coarse paste (not smooth — the texture is important; some split pea pieces should remain). Combine with flour, yeast, turmeric, cumin, curry powder, garlic, shadow beni, scotch bonnet. Add enough warm water to create a thick batter that falls slowly from a spoon. Rest 30 minutes for the yeast to activate. The batter will become slightly puffy and lighter.
2
Deep-fry by dropping tablespoon-sized portions into oil at 170°C / 340°F. The pholourie should puff into uneven spheres — perfectly round pholourie indicate too much flour in the batter. Golden, slightly cracked, fragrant with turmeric and split pea. Fry 3–4 minutes until cooked through. The interior should be soft and slightly dense, not hollow.
3
Mango sour: Grate green (unripe) mango on the coarse side of a box grater. Combine with finely minced shadow beni, garlic, scotch bonnet (or habanero), lime juice, and salt. The mango sour should be raw, fresh, acidic, and herb-forward — it is the contrast element to the starchy, fried pholourie. Serve cold alongside hot pholourie.
Pholourie: crispy golden exterior, soft interior with split pea earthiness and turmeric warmth, the scotch bonnet providing background heat. The mango sour: cold, intensely acidic, pungent with raw shadow beni, the green mango's tartness cutting through the frying oil. This is a flavour contrast that is specifically Caribbean — hot starch against cold acid. It has no equivalent in any other cuisine.

Method — Desalting and Technique

1
Desalting the saltfish: This is the most important preparation step and the most commonly rushed. Salt cod must be soaked for 24 hours minimum, changing the water at least 3 times (morning, afternoon, evening). After soaking: taste the fish. It should be pleasantly savoury, not aggressively salty. If still too salty: poach in fresh water for 15 minutes. The goal is fish that retains its characteristic flavour and firmness while being comfortable to eat without thirst. Under-desalted accra makes guests drink twice as much water and complain about the next day's sodium intake.
2
Flake the desalted saltfish into small pieces — not minced, not shredded too fine. The fish pieces should be visible in the finished fritter, providing textural contrast to the batter. Combine with flour, green seasoning, scotch bonnet, spring onion, parsley, egg, baking powder, and enough cold water to create a thick dropping batter.
3
Drop tablespoon portions into oil at 175°C / 350°F. The baking powder creates internal bubbles as the fritter fries — the result should be irregular, slightly puffy, with a golden-brown crust. Fry 4 minutes until cooked through. Serve immediately — accra lose their crunch within minutes of coming out of the fryer.
💡 Accra (also called "stamp and go" in Jamaica) exists throughout the Caribbean with regional variations. The Trinidadian version uses green seasoning as the base flavour — the Jamaican version is simpler (saltfish + flour + scotch bonnet). Both are correct. The key quality indicator for accra: the saltfish flavour should be distinctive but not overwhelming, and the fritter should be light despite being fried (the baking powder and cold water are what keep it from being dense).
The exterior: golden, crunchy, slightly irregular. The interior: soft, with visible flakes of saltfish throughout the herb-spiced batter. The saltfish: firm, savoury, deeply flavoured — the desalting process concentrates and mellows the salt cure flavour without removing it entirely. Shadow beni throughout. The scotch bonnet: present as warm heat rather than fire. Serve with chadon beni sauce — the herb brightness amplifies the fish flavour.

Method — Three Textures, One Fruit

1
Tostones (smashed green plantain): Peel green plantain, slice into 2cm rounds. Fry at 160°C / 320°F for 3 minutes until slightly golden but not fully cooked. Remove. Using the flat of a heavy pan or a tostonera, smash each piece to 1cm thickness. Return to oil at 180°C / 360°F for 2 more minutes until crispy and golden. The double-fry creates a crispy exterior with a starchy, yielding interior that single-frying cannot achieve. Season immediately with salt and a squeeze of lime.
2
Maduros (sweet fried ripe plantain): The plantain must be fully ripe — black skinned, the flesh soft and beginning to caramelise internally. Slice diagonally 1cm thick. Fry in a small amount of oil at medium-low heat for 2–3 minutes per side until deeply caramelised and golden. The natural sugars of the ripe plantain caramelise and create a lacquered, sweet exterior. A pinch of sea salt on top immediately after frying amplifies the sweetness.
3
Plantain chips: Slice green plantain paper-thin on a mandoline. Fry immediately in oil at 170°C / 340°F for 90 seconds until crispy and slightly translucent. Season with salt and a pinch of cumin.
Three expressions of the same fruit: the tostones — starchy, slightly salty, with the crunch of the double fry and the lime brightness. The maduros — sweet, lacquered, caramelised, the natural fruit sugars dominant, the salt cutting through. The chips — thin, crispy, neutral starch with cumin warmth. Together they demonstrate why plantain is the most versatile starch in Caribbean cooking.

Method

1
Sauté onion, garlic, shadow beni, and thyme in oil until soft. Add callaloo leaves (tough stems removed) and okra. Add coconut milk and stock. Add smoked pig tail (pre-soaked in water 2 hours to reduce saltiness). Add whole scotch bonnet. Simmer 25 minutes until callaloo is completely wilted and the okra has released its natural thickener into the soup.
2
Remove and reserve the pig tail or crab. Blend the soup with an immersion blender until smooth — the okra creates a characteristically silky, slightly viscous texture that is unique to callaloo. Return the meat/crab. Adjust seasoning. The callaloo should be deeply green, slightly thick, coconut-rich and earthy from the dasheen leaves.
💡 Callaloo (or calaloo) is the Caribbean's most important stew/soup and the dish that defines national identity across Trinidad, Guyana, and other islands. The dasheen leaf (taro leaf) produces a distinctive earthy, slightly mineral flavour and natural viscosity from its mucilage content. If dasheen is unavailable: combine 60g taro leaf (frozen, widely available in Asian grocery stores) with 20g spinach — the combination approximates the flavour and texture of fresh dasheen bush.
The callaloo: deeply green, silky, warm with coconut milk richness and the earthy depth of dasheen. The smoked pig tail adds a smoky, slightly salty pork depth that permeates the entire broth. Okra's natural thickener gives the soup a body unlike any stock-based soup. The scotch bonnet: a warm heat that builds gradually as the bowl is consumed. This is the soup that Caribbean mothers make when someone is sick or coming home after a long time away.

Method

1
The bake: Not to be confused with baking in an oven — Trinidadian "bake" is fried bread dough. Mix flour (300g), baking powder (2 tsp), salt, butter (30g), enough water to form a soft, smooth dough. Rest 15 minutes. Divide into 120g pieces. Roll each to a disc approximately 1.5cm thick. Fry in oil at 165°C / 330°F for 3 minutes per side until golden and cooked through. The bake will puff slightly and develop a light, airy interior with a golden crust. Split through the middle horizontally — the bake is the vessel for the shark.
2
Marinate fish in green seasoning minimum 1 hour. Season with salt. Coat lightly in seasoned flour. Pan-fry or deep-fry at 180°C / 360°F until golden and cooked through — approximately 4 minutes per side for pan-frying. The fish should be flaky inside and crispy on the outside.
3
The assembly: Split bake open. Layer shredded cabbage, tomato, cucumber. Place fish fillet. Then: the guest chooses their sauces — at Maracas Beach, the ritual is "how you want it?" and the vendor adds each condiment on request. In the restaurant: serve all condiments in small individual cups alongside the bake. The experience of building the bake sandwich is part of the dish.
💡 At Maracas Beach in Trinidad, the bake and shark vendors have queues that stretch 40 people deep every weekend. The dish is so culturally significant that the location — Maracas Beach, a 45-minute drive from Port of Spain over the Northern Range — is itself part of the bake and shark mythology. Describe it as "the Trinidadian fish taco from the most famous beach in the Caribbean." American guests understand "fish taco" immediately; the comparison creates a reference point that makes the dish approachable.
The bake: soft, airy, slightly sweet from the butter — a gentle bread flavour that lets the fish and sauces dominate. The fish: crispy-edged, flaky, with the green seasoning's herb complexity permeating the flesh. Shredded cabbage for crunch. Then the sauces: chadon beni's intense herbiness, pepper sauce's fruity scotch bonnet heat, tamarind's sweet-sour depth, garlic sauce's richness. Every bite is different depending on the sauce combination. This is one of the most joyful, chaotic, delicious hand-held foods in the world.
