KitchenFounder Guide #44 · Chef Introduction
"Country has been feeding people for 65,000 years. I'm just paying attention."

I grew up in Queensland on a property where the bush was the back yard. My grandmother knew where the quandong trees grew before the rains came. She knew which season brought the finger limes and which mornings you'd find the bunya nuts on the ground. That knowledge — quiet, embodied, passed hand to hand — is what I came back to after a decade in European kitchens.

In Lyon, in Copenhagen, in London, I learned technique. Perfect caramel. Tempered chocolate. The geometry of tarts. But the ingredient that made every European chef lean forward and ask questions — that was always the tin of wattleseed in my bag, or the dried davidson plum I'd brought from home.

This guide is about marrying those two worlds without betraying either. Classic European pastry structure. Native Australian ingredients at the centre, not as novelty garnish but as the reason the dish exists. I've spent years building relationships with First Nations growers and suppliers who are doing extraordinary work — I'll share those sourcing leads throughout this guide because the ingredients are everything.

A note on respect: the knowledge embedded in these ingredients belongs to the peoples of this Country. Where I reference traditional use, I do so with care. I encourage every chef who picks up this guide to learn the Country context — not just the flavour profile — of every ingredient they use.

Cook with curiosity. Source with integrity. Plate with confidence.

— Chef, Guide #44

01
Wattleseed Crème Brûlée
Wattleseed-infused custard · native honey caramel crust · serves 4
$6.80
per serve AUD
GF NF

Ingredients

  • Full cream (thickened)500 ml
  • Egg yolks6 large (120 g)
  • Caster sugar100 g
  • Roasted wattleseed (ground)18 g
  • Pure vanilla extract1 tsp (5 ml)
  • Flaked sea saltpinch
  • Caster sugar (for top)40 g
  • Jarrah honey (drizzle)20 g
  • Native edible flowers (optional)to garnish

Method

  1. Combine cream and ground wattleseed in a saucepan. Heat over medium flame to just below simmer (80°C). Remove from heat, cover, steep 20 minutes.
  2. Whisk egg yolks and caster sugar in a bowl until pale and ribbon-stage — about 3 minutes by hand or 90 seconds electric.
  3. Strain wattleseed cream through a fine sieve, pressing pulp. Discard solids. Reheat cream to 75°C.
  4. Slowly ladle hot cream into yolk mixture while whisking — temper gradually to avoid scrambling. Add vanilla and salt.
  5. Pour custard into four 150 ml ramekins or shallow brûlée dishes. Place in a bain-marie (water halfway up sides). Bake 150°C fan-off for 38–42 minutes until set with a slight wobble in centre.
  6. Cool to room temperature. Refrigerate uncovered 4 hours or overnight until fully set.
  7. Drizzle a thin layer of Jarrah honey across surface before adding a thin, even layer of caster sugar (8–10 g per ramekin).
  8. Torch in a slow circular motion, working from outside in, until deep amber caramel forms. Allow 30 seconds to set hard.
  9. Garnish with edible native flowers. Serve immediately.
🍳 Technique Note

Wattleseed releases oils that can turn bitter if over-steeped. 20 minutes at 80°C is the sweet spot — you get the deep roasted coffee-chocolate note without astringency. Always strain well; fine particles will cloud the custard and create a grainy texture.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

If your brûlée cracks during torching it was too cold — always bring to room temperature before the final sugar stage. Use a thin, even sugar layer (not thick). Two thin passes with the torch beats one heavy scorch.

🎨 Plating Note

Serve in wide, shallow ceramic dishes to maximise the caramel surface area. A single finger lime pearl pressed into the caramel just before service adds a citrus pop that cuts the richness beautifully.

Source: Ground roasted wattleseed — Outback Pride (SA), Kakadu Trading Co., Herbie's Spices (Sydney/online). Buy in 200 g minimum for cost efficiency. Store airtight in the freezer; shelf life 18 months. Jarrah honey — Beechworth Honey, local WA producers via farmers markets.

Lemon Myrtle Tart
Lemon myrtle curd · Italian meringue · shortcrust pastry · serves 8
$5.40
per serve AUD
NF

Ingredients

  • Shortcrust Pastry
  • Plain flour200 g
  • Unsalted butter (cold, cubed)120 g
  • Icing sugar40 g
  • Egg yolk1 (20 g)
  • Ice water2 tbsp
  • Lemon Myrtle Curd
  • Eggs (whole)4 (200 g)
  • Caster sugar180 g
  • Unsalted butter (cubed)120 g
  • Lemon myrtle dried leaf powder6 g
  • Fresh lemon juice80 ml
  • Lemon zest2 lemons
  • Italian Meringue
  • Egg whites3 (90 g)
  • Caster sugar200 g
  • Water60 ml

Method

  1. Pastry: Pulse flour, butter, and icing sugar in food processor until sandy. Add yolk and ice water, pulse until dough just comes together. Wrap, rest 30 mins refrigerated.
  2. Roll pastry 3 mm on floured bench. Line a 23 cm tart tin with removable base. Blind bake 180°C for 18 minutes with weights, then 8 minutes uncovered until golden. Cool completely.
  3. Curd: Combine eggs, sugar, lemon juice, zest, and lemon myrtle powder in a double boiler over simmering water. Whisk constantly until thick enough to coat spoon (82°C), about 12–15 minutes.
  4. Remove from heat. Whisk in cold butter cubes one at a time until glossy and emulsified. Strain through fine sieve. Pour into cooled tart shell. Refrigerate 3 hours to set.
  5. Italian Meringue: Combine sugar and water in saucepan, heat to 118°C without stirring. Simultaneously beat whites to soft peaks. Pour hot syrup in thin steady stream onto whites while beating. Continue beating on high until glossy and cool (5–7 min).
  6. Transfer meringue to piping bag. Pipe tall kisses or swoops across tart surface.
  7. Torch meringue to golden brown. Serve within 2 hours.
🍳 Technique Note

Lemon myrtle leaf is 90% citral — far more intense than lemon. 6 g is the right dose for a tart; more and it becomes soapy. Add powder directly to the curd base rather than infusing separately so it integrates smoothly rather than speckling.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

Italian meringue needs the sugar syrup at exactly 118°C — use a digital thermometer. If whites are overbeaten before syrup is added, the meringue will be lumpy. Aim for soft peaks only when the syrup is ready to pour.

🎨 Plating Note

Dust finished tart with tiny amount of lemon myrtle powder through a fine sieve for colour contrast. Add 3–4 finger lime pearls squeezed at the last moment — the green-gold of finger lime against brûléed meringue is stunning.

Source: Lemon myrtle powder/leaf — Outback Spirit, The Essential Ingredient (Sydney), Australian Native Foods (online). Buy powder for curd work; whole dried leaves for infusions. Avoid synthetic lemon myrtle extract — the real thing is a completely different flavour profile.

Davidson Plum Pavlova
Davidson plum compote · finger lime caviar · chantilly cream · serves 6
$7.20
per serve AUD
GF NF DF*

Ingredients

  • Pavlova Base
  • Egg whites (room temp)6 (180 g)
  • Caster sugar300 g
  • White vinegar2 tsp (10 ml)
  • Cornflour2 tsp (6 g)
  • Pure vanilla extract1 tsp (5 ml)
  • Davidson Plum Compote
  • Davidson plum (frozen/dried)200 g
  • Caster sugar80 g
  • Water60 ml
  • Star anise1 piece
  • Chantilly & Garnish
  • Thickened cream (cold)400 ml
  • Icing sugar30 g
  • Vanilla bean paste1 tsp
  • Finger limes3 (pearls only)

Method

  1. Preheat oven to 130°C fan-off. Line baking tray with baking paper. Draw a 22 cm circle as guide.
  2. Beat egg whites on medium speed until frothy. Increase to high. Add sugar one tablespoon at a time, beating for 20 seconds between each addition. Continue until stiff, glossy peaks form and sugar is fully dissolved (rub a little between fingers — no graininess).
  3. Sift cornflour over meringue. Add vinegar and vanilla. Fold gently with spatula — 6–8 folds maximum, keeping the marshmallow texture.
  4. Mound onto circle, building high sides and a slight crater in centre. Bake 90 minutes. Turn oven off, door ajar — leave 2–3 hours or overnight to dry fully.
  5. Compote: Combine davidson plum, sugar, water, and star anise in saucepan. Simmer 12–15 minutes until jammy and deep purple-red. Remove star anise. Cool. Refrigerate until needed.
  6. Chantilly: Whip cold cream, icing sugar, and vanilla to soft peaks. Keep cold.
  7. Assembly: Spoon generous chantilly into pavlova crater. Drizzle compote over and around cream. Squeeze finger lime pearls across surface. Serve immediately.
🍳 Technique Note

Davidson plum has an extraordinary tartness — it reads like sour cherry meets blood plum with native bush notes. Use it sparingly as a compote rather than raw, as the raw flesh can be overwhelmingly astringent. The sugar ratio 80 g to 200 g fruit brings it into dessert balance beautifully.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

Humidity is the enemy of pavlova. Never make it on a wet day — the shell won't dry properly and you'll get weeping. If you're in a humid climate, add an extra 5 minutes to the oven drying time and keep the door slightly ajar during the cool-down phase.

🎨 Plating Note

The deep crimson-purple of davidson plum compote against white chantilly is the dish's hero visual. Keep the cream white and the compote bold — don't mix them. Finger lime pearls scattered across the top add textural interest and a bright citrus perfume on approach.

Source: Davidson plum — available frozen (Robins Foods, Coles/Woolworths selective stores) or dried from Outback Spirit, Kakadu Trading Co. Finger limes — native growers via Farm Direct Australia, seasonal May–August in NSW/QLD. Check Providore Direct for year-round supply.

Quandong + White Chocolate Mousse
Native peach compote · white chocolate mousse · vanilla · serves 6
$8.50
per serve AUD
GF NF

Ingredients

  • White Chocolate Mousse
  • White chocolate (35% cocoa butter)180 g
  • Thickened cream (cold)350 ml
  • Egg yolks3 (60 g)
  • Caster sugar40 g
  • Water30 ml
  • Vanilla bean paste1 tsp
  • Gold-strength gelatine leaf2 sheets (4 g)
  • Quandong Compote
  • Dried quandong halves150 g
  • Orange juice100 ml
  • Brown sugar60 g
  • Cinnamon stick1
  • Butter20 g
  • Toasted almond flakes (optional)20 g garnish

Method

  1. Mousse: Bloom gelatine in cold water 5 minutes. Whip 280 ml cold cream to soft peaks. Refrigerate.
  2. Melt chocolate over double boiler to 45°C. Keep warm.
  3. Cook sugar and water to 121°C (firm ball stage). Simultaneously beat yolks with vanilla until pale.
  4. Pour hot syrup slowly onto beating yolks (pâte à bombe). Beat until mixture has tripled in volume and is cool to touch.
  5. Squeeze gelatine dry. Heat remaining 70 ml cream to 60°C, dissolve gelatine in it. Add to melted chocolate and mix.
  6. Fold chocolate-gelatine mix into pâte à bombe. Then gently fold in whipped cream in two additions. Pour into serving glasses. Chill 3+ hours.
  7. Compote: Rehydrate quandong in orange juice 30 minutes. Transfer to pan with sugar, cinnamon, and 50 ml water. Simmer 15 minutes until quandong is tender and sauce coats spoon. Finish with butter. Cool.
  8. Spoon quandong compote atop set mousse. Garnish with almond flakes if nut-free tag not required.
🍳 Technique Note

Quandong (native peach) has a unique flavour: rhubarb meets apricot with a faint resinous note. Dried quandong needs rehydration — always use citrus juice rather than plain water to preserve the fruit's natural acidity and brighten the flavour. Don't over-sweeten; the slight tartness is its value.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

White chocolate mousse can be grainy if chocolate seizes or if gelatine isn't fully dissolved. Ensure chocolate is smooth at 45°C before folding and that gelatine is completely lump-free before adding. Work at room temperature — cold chocolate will seize on contact.

🎨 Plating Note

Serve in wide champagne coupes or individual terrines. The white mousse topped with the warm terracotta tones of quandong compote is visually arresting. A micro-thin quandong crisp (slice dried on silpat at 70°C overnight) adds height and a translucent jewel effect.

Source: Dried quandong — Outback Spirit (SA), Kakadu Trading Co. (NT), Australian Native Foods Co. (online). Fresh quandong is rare; dried is far more practical for a commercial kitchen. Season: Feb–May for fresh. Store dried quandong vacuum-sealed; shelf life 12 months.

Anise Myrtle Panna Cotta
Coconut milk base · finger lime caviar · toasted coconut · serves 6
$5.90
per serve AUD
GF VG* NF DF

Ingredients

  • Full-fat coconut milk600 ml
  • Coconut cream200 ml
  • Caster sugar70 g
  • Anise myrtle dried leaves (whole)10 g
  • Gold-strength gelatine leaves5 sheets (10 g)
  • Vanilla extract1 tsp (5 ml)
  • Finger limes4 (pearls)
  • Shredded coconut (toasted)40 g
  • Coconut sugar (optional garnish)15 g
  • Micro mint or native mint leavesto garnish

Method

  1. Bloom gelatine leaves in cold water for 5 minutes.
  2. Combine coconut milk, coconut cream, and caster sugar in a saucepan. Warm over medium heat to 78°C, stirring until sugar dissolves.
  3. Add anise myrtle leaves, reduce heat to lowest setting. Cover and steep 15 minutes. Do not boil — high heat destroys the delicate anise compounds.
  4. Strain through fine-mesh sieve, pressing leaves firmly. Discard leaves. Return liquid to 65°C.
  5. Squeeze gelatine leaves dry. Whisk into hot coconut liquid until completely dissolved. Add vanilla extract.
  6. Cool mixture to room temperature, stirring occasionally to prevent skin forming. Pour into six lightly oiled 120 ml dariole moulds or serving glasses.
  7. Refrigerate minimum 4 hours or overnight until firmly set.
  8. To unmould: run a thin knife around edge, briefly set base in warm water (5 sec), invert onto chilled plate.
  9. Toast shredded coconut in dry pan until golden. Cool. Scatter around base of panna cotta, add finger lime pearls and coconut sugar crystals. Garnish with native mint.
🍳 Technique Note

Anise myrtle delivers a clean, aromatic anise flavour that's softer and more complex than star anise. The key is temperature control — never boil the infusion or you'll volatilise the essential oils and lose the delicate perfume. This dish rewards restraint: 15 minutes at sub-boil, strain clean.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

For a fully vegan version, substitute gelatine with 3 g agar-agar powder. Note: agar sets firmer and won't have the silky wobble of gelatine. Bloom agar in cold liquid first, then bring to a full boil to activate — the opposite process to gelatine. Test set in fridge 30 minutes before filling moulds.

🎨 Plating Note

Unmoulded panna cotta sitting on a pool of finger lime gel (blend 60 g pearls with 20 ml simple syrup) creates a stunning green-against-white presentation. Toasted coconut forms a natural "halo" ring. Serve on slate or white ceramic — avoid coloured plates that compete with the finger lime's natural fluorescence.

Source: Anise myrtle dried leaves — Outback Spirit, Australian Native Foods, The Herbie's Spices range (whole leaves best for infusions; powder works but infusion time drops to 8 minutes). Finger limes — seasonally from Farm Direct Australia, Providore Direct. Out of season: frozen finger lime pearls from Sirromet or online native food suppliers.

Bush Tomato Caramel Slice
Bush tomato caramel · shortbread base · dark chocolate topping · serves 12
$3.80
per serve AUD
NF VG

Ingredients

  • Shortbread Base
  • Plain flour200 g
  • Desiccated coconut40 g
  • Brown sugar80 g
  • Unsalted butter (melted)150 g
  • Bush Tomato Caramel
  • Sweetened condensed milk400 ml (1 tin)
  • Brown sugar80 g
  • Unsalted butter60 g
  • Golden syrup2 tbsp (40 g)
  • Ground bush tomato (dried)8 g
  • Flaked sea salt1 tsp
  • Chocolate Top
  • Dark chocolate (70%)200 g
  • Coconut oil1 tbsp (15 ml)

Method

  1. Preheat oven to 180°C. Grease and line a 20×30 cm baking tray with baking paper, leaving overhang for easy removal.
  2. Base: Combine flour, coconut, brown sugar, and melted butter. Mix until crumbly. Press firmly and evenly into prepared tray. Bake 15 minutes until light golden. Cool completely.
  3. Caramel: Combine condensed milk, brown sugar, butter, and golden syrup in a heavy-based saucepan. Stir continuously over medium heat — this is critical, do not leave unattended.
  4. Cook caramel, stirring constantly, 12–15 minutes until it turns deep golden and pulls away from the sides of the pan. It should read 118°C on a sugar thermometer.
  5. Remove from heat. Quickly stir in ground bush tomato and sea salt. Pour immediately over cooled base. Spread evenly. Allow to cool 30 minutes at room temperature, then refrigerate 1 hour to firm.
  6. Chocolate: Melt chocolate with coconut oil over double boiler until smooth at 45°C. Pour over set caramel. Tip tray to coat evenly. Sprinkle with extra sea salt flakes if desired.
  7. Refrigerate 1 hour until chocolate sets hard. Use baking paper overhang to lift from tray. Cut with hot dry knife into 12 portions.
🍳 Technique Note

Bush tomato (akatjurra) has a deep, savoury-sweet complexity — sun-dried tomato meets tamarind meets caramel. Ground and added to a classic caramel, it creates a mysterious depth that guests will struggle to identify. Use 8 g maximum; higher quantities push it into savoury territory that breaks the dessert context.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

The caramel must be stirred constantly — condensed milk burns if left still. If your caramel seizes or goes grainy, you've let it reach too high a temperature too fast. Lower heat and stir more aggressively. If crystallisation occurs, add 1 tbsp of cream and stir vigorously off the heat.

🎨 Plating Note

Serve slices at room temperature, not cold — the caramel needs to be at room temp for full flavour. A quenelle of lightly whipped crème fraîche alongside cuts the richness. A dust of ground bush tomato on the plate gives a terracotta desert-earth aesthetic appropriate to the ingredient's origins.

Source: Ground dried bush tomato — Herbie's Spices, Outback Spirit, Native Foods Australia. Also found at specialty food suppliers. Buy whole dried bush tomatoes and grind in spice grinder for best freshness. Strong shelf life (18+ months) when stored in airtight container away from light.

Kakadu Plum Cheesecake
World's highest Vitamin C fruit · coconut biscuit base · no-bake · serves 10
$7.60
per serve AUD
GF* NF

Ingredients

  • Coconut Base
  • Coconut biscuits (GF if needed)200 g
  • Desiccated coconut40 g
  • Unsalted butter (melted)90 g
  • Cheesecake Filling
  • Cream cheese (room temp)500 g
  • Thickened cream250 ml
  • Icing sugar120 g
  • Kakadu plum powder12 g
  • Vanilla bean paste1 tsp
  • Gold gelatine leaves3 sheets (6 g)
  • Kakadu Plum Gel Topping
  • Kakadu plum powder6 g
  • Water200 ml
  • Caster sugar50 g
  • Agar-agar powder2 g

Method

  1. Process biscuits to fine crumbs. Add desiccated coconut and melted butter. Mix until combined. Press evenly into a 23 cm springform tin (lined base). Refrigerate 30 minutes.
  2. Bloom gelatine in cold water 5 minutes. Heat 50 ml cream to 60°C in small saucepan. Squeeze gelatine dry, dissolve in warm cream. Set aside.
  3. Beat cream cheese, icing sugar, and vanilla with electric mixer until smooth and lump-free, 3–4 minutes on medium speed.
  4. Add kakadu plum powder and mix 1 minute. The powder is intensely tart and will colour the mixture a pale gold-green — this is natural and correct.
  5. Whip remaining 200 ml cream to soft peaks. Fold into cream cheese mixture. Then fold in gelatine-cream mixture quickly before it sets.
  6. Pour filling over biscuit base. Smooth with palette knife. Refrigerate 4 hours minimum.
  7. Gel top: Combine water, kakadu plum powder, sugar, and agar in saucepan. Bring to full boil stirring. Remove from heat, cool to 50°C (still pourable). Pour carefully over set cheesecake. Return to fridge 1 hour.
  8. Remove springform ring. Slice with hot clean knife. Serve with a small bowl of fresh finger lime pearls on the side.
🍳 Technique Note

Kakadu plum (gubinge) contains up to 5,300 mg of Vitamin C per 100 g — 50× more than oranges. The powder is intensely tart with a subtle earthy, slightly sulphurous note that rounds beautifully with cream cheese fat. Don't overshoot 12 g in the filling; the tartness should be bright, not aggressive.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

Kakadu plum powder can oxidise and brown if exposed to air and heat. Buy in airtight packets, store in freezer, and only measure what you need. For the gel topping, work quickly once the agar cools below 50°C or it will set in the pan. Keep the pan over low heat while you pour.

🎨 Plating Note

The gel top creates a jewel-like, semi-translucent surface — gold-green over white cheesecake. Serve slices on chilled slate with a smear of extra kakadu plum gel across the plate. Micro herbs (native lemon thyme or river mint) pressed into the top add a wildflower meadow quality.

Source: Kakadu plum powder — Outback Spirit, Kakadu Trading Co. (NT — consider buying directly from Indigenous-owned enterprise), Harrington's Organics. Available online. This is arguably the most important ingredient in the guide; buy from ethical sources that support First Nations growers and pay fair prices.

Macadamia + Wattleseed Tart
Macadamia frangipane · wattleseed custard · serves 8
$9.20
per serve AUD
GF*

Ingredients

  • Sweet Shortcrust
  • Plain flour220 g
  • Icing sugar50 g
  • Cold unsalted butter130 g
  • Egg yolk1 + 1 tbsp water
  • Macadamia Frangipane
  • Raw macadamia nuts (blitzed fine)180 g
  • Unsalted butter (softened)100 g
  • Caster sugar100 g
  • Eggs2 whole (100 g)
  • Plain flour20 g
  • Ground wattleseed10 g
  • Wattleseed Custard
  • Full cream milk250 ml
  • Egg yolks4 (80 g)
  • Caster sugar60 g
  • Cornflour20 g
  • Ground wattleseed8 g
  • Butter30 g

Method

  1. Pastry: rub butter into flour and icing sugar to sandy breadcrumbs. Add yolk and water, bring together. Wrap, rest 30 min cold. Roll 3 mm, line 23 cm tart tin. Blind bake 175°C for 18 min (weights), then 8 min uncovered until pale gold.
  2. Macadamia frangipane: Beat soft butter and sugar until pale. Add eggs one at a time, beating well. Fold in blitzed macadamia, flour, and wattleseed. The mix should be thick and spreadable.
  3. Spread frangipane evenly into cooled tart shell (do not overfill — rises about 30%). Bake 170°C for 25–30 minutes until frangipane is set and golden on top. Cool fully.
  4. Wattleseed custard: Steep wattleseed in warm milk 15 minutes. Strain, pressing. Return milk to 80°C.
  5. Whisk yolks, sugar, and cornflour until pale. Temper hot milk into yolk mix. Return to saucepan. Cook over medium heat, whisking constantly, until thick and boiling (2 minutes bubbling to cook out starch).
  6. Remove from heat. Whisk in cold butter for gloss. Press plastic wrap directly onto surface. Cool to 40°C.
  7. Pipe or spoon wattleseed custard into a generous layer over cooled frangipane tart. Smooth surface. Refrigerate 2 hours.
  8. Finish with macadamia halves (toasted 160°C, 10 minutes), a light dusting of wattleseed powder, and gold leaf if occasion warrants.
🍳 Technique Note

Macadamia is Queensland's — and the world's — original tree nut. Blitz raw macadamia fine for frangipane but leave a few larger pieces in for textural contrast. Wattleseed in both the frangipane and custard layers creates a layered flavour experience — subtle in the baked layer, dominant in the custard.

✓ Fail-Proof Tip

Frangipane can dome and crack if oven is too hot. 170°C fan is ideal — it sets gently. If you see rapid browning at 15 minutes, cover loosely with foil. The custard top masks any surface imperfections so don't stress if the frangipane isn't picture-perfect.

🎨 Plating Note

The custard top should be mirror-smooth and deep chocolate-brown from the wattleseed. Serve each wedge with a side of lightly whipped cream. Decorate the cream with a single drop of Jarrah honey — it pools beautifully on the white surface and ties back to the dish's native ingredient story.

Source: Raw macadamia nuts — Queensland growers via Buderim Ginger & Macadamia, Suncoast Gold Macadamias, or Woolworths Select range. Always buy raw for baking — roasted will make frangipane oily. Ground wattleseed — Outback Spirit, Herbie's Spices. Macadamias: store vacuum in freezer to prevent rancidity from high oil content.

Saltbush Caramel Fondant
Warm molten centre · saltbush butterscotch sauce · crème fraîche · serves 6
$6.40
per serve AUD
NF

Ingredients

  • Fondant Batter
  • Dark chocolate (64%)150 g
  • Unsalted butter120 g
  • Caster sugar120 g
  • Eggs + extra yolks3 eggs + 3 yolks
  • Plain flour50 g
  • Ground dried saltbush leaves4 g
  • Flaked sea salt1 g
  • Saltbush Butterscotch Sauce
  • Brown sugar120 g
  • Unsalted butter80 g
  • Thickened cream150 ml
  • Ground dried saltbush3 g
  • Vanilla extract1 tsp
  • Crème fraîche180 g (6 quenelles)

Method

  1. Butter and flour-dust six 150 ml dariole moulds. Chill in freezer.

Wattleseed
Acacia species · pan-Australia
Seeds of native acacia (wattle) trees. Ground roasted wattleseed has a flavour of dark-roast coffee, hazelnut, and chocolate. Australia's most versatile native food ingredient in dessert contexts. Backhousia citriodora · SE Queensland rainforest
Native rainforest tree producing leaves with the world's highest concentration of citral — the key flavour compound in lemon. Far more lemony than lemon itself, with a sweet, clean citrus profile. Used fresh, dried, or powdered. Essential oil commercially extracted. In desserts, use sparingly — potent. Pairs beautifully with cream, custard, meringue. Available year-round dried. Antimicrobial properties; used traditionally for medicinal purposes.
Davidson Plum
Davidsonia pruriens · QLD/NSW rainforest
Deep blue-purple native plum from rainforest margins. Intensely sour raw — similar to very tart sour cherry crossed with plum and rhubarb. Extraordinary deep colour when cooked into compote. Very high in anthocyanins (antioxidants). Must be cooked with sugar to become palatable in dessert contexts. Native to the Daintree and surrounding QLD/NSW rainforest Country. Frozen and dried widely available. Season: January–April fresh.
Kakadu Plum
Terminalia ferdinandiana · Northern Australia
Small pale green/yellow fruit from NT and WA. Holds the world record for Vitamin C concentration — up to 5,300 mg per 100 g (50× oranges, 3,000× apple). Light, delicate flavour: slightly tart with subtle earthy notes. Powder used in desserts and drinks. Sacred to First Nations communities in the Top End. Traditionally eaten fresh or dried. A critically important export ingredient for Australian food culture globally. Source from Indigenous enterprises wherever possible.
Finger Lime
Microcitrus australasica · QLD/NSW rainforest
Finger-shaped native citrus containing tiny spherical juice vesicles (caviar). When squeezed, pearls pop out like caviar, releasing a burst of intense citrus juice — tropical lime, grapefruit, sometimes even passionfruit notes depending on variety. The "citrus caviar" of global fine dining. Five colour varieties: green, yellow, pink, black, red. Season peak: May–August. Frozen pearls available year-round. Mountain Pepper Berry
Tasmannia lanceolata · Tasmania and SE Australia
Native pepper from Tasmanian and SE Australian mountain forests. Twice the heat of black pepper with extraordinary aromatic complexity — floral, eucalyptus, berry sweetness, then delayed intense heat. Both berries (hotter) and leaves (milder, more herbal) are used. In desserts: micro-doses in chocolate ganache, meringue, or paired with dark fruits. Traditional First Nations use: seasoning, medicine, preservative. Commercially harvested in Tasmania. Available dried whole berries and ground.
Quandong (Native Peach)

Native peach of arid and semi-arid Australia. Flavour: tangy rhubarb meets apricot with a faint resinous note. Bright red skin when fresh; most available dried. Historically one of the most important foods of desert Country — eaten fresh, dried for storage, traded across trade routes. High in Vitamin C, calcium, magnesium. A partially parasitic plant that requires a host plant to grow — makes commercial cultivation complex. Valued in jams, compotes, pies. Use dried rehydrated in citrus juice.

Monofloral honey from the Jarrah eucalyptus forests of Western Australia. Deep amber, intensely flavoured with malt, honey, subtle medicinal notes. Appendix D
Menu Design & Cultural Responsibility
You are working with ingredients from the world's oldest living food culture. How you present, credit, and profit from these ingredients matters.
"The menu is your first conversation with a guest about Country."

When I write a menu item that features kakadu plum or wattleseed, I have a responsibility. These are not exotic novelty ingredients that I "discovered." They are part of a continuous food culture stretching back at least 65,000 years — a culture that was systematically disrupted by colonisation and is now, in many ways, reclaiming its culinary inheritance.

That reclamation should include chefs like me — but only if we do it with genuine respect, accurate attribution, and concrete support for First Nations growers and suppliers. Below are the principles I apply in my kitchen and that I'd encourage any chef using this guide to adopt.

The commercial opportunity here is real. The ethical obligation is also real. They are not in conflict.

Menu Copy Principles

Name the ingredient, not the exoticism.
Write "wattleseed" not "mysterious Australian seed." Your guests deserve accuracy.

Name the Country when you can.
"Kakadu plum (Larrakia Country, NT)" or "davidson plum (Yidinji Country, QLD)" is more honest than a vague "native Australian fruit."

Include a short cultural context line.
"Wattleseed — ground from seeds of the golden wattle, used by First Nations Australians for over 6,000 years." This is a 20-word investment that transforms a menu line into a conversation piece.

Don't claim too much.
If your recipe is a European-style tart with wattleseed in the custard, say so clearly. You're using a native ingredient in a contemporary fine dessert context — that's valid and interesting. It doesn't need to be dressed as a traditional First Nations recipe.

Supporting First Nations Enterprise

Supplier choice is a political choice.
Where First Nations-owned suppliers exist, use them. The price difference is often minimal; the impact is significant.

Acknowledge on the menu.
Many restaurants now include a simple acknowledgement of Country in their menus. At minimum, name the Country your primary native ingredients come from.

Collaborate, don't extract.
If you are building a business significantly on native ingredient storytelling, consider whether there's a way to formally collaborate with, support, or co-create with First Nations community organisations. This might be as simple as a supplier partnership agreement with a fair-trade clause, or as significant as a co-design process for your educational materials.

Long-term thinking.
The native food industry is still developing. Buying now, even at slightly higher costs, supports the infrastructure that will make these ingredients more available and more affordable for everyone in the future.

Cooking with Country

When you cook with Davidson plum or wattleseed, you are not simply working with interesting ingredients. You are handling something that has been known, tended, gathered, and understood for 65,000 years. That is the oldest continuous food tradition on earth. Modern technique is perhaps 300 years old. Country is 65,000. The responsibility ratio is clear.

The responsibility we carry in this kitchen is threefold. To the land itself — that these plants are sourced sustainably, that we do not exhaust what took millennia to develop. To the Traditional Custodians — that we name them, pay them fairly, and share their stories only with permission and gratitude, never as appropriation dressed up as appreciation. And to the guest — that what lands on the plate is genuine: a true encounter with something Australian, not a performance of it.

When a guest tastes finger lime for the first time, something small happens that I believe matters. They are, for a moment, connected to a place and a people they may never have thought about. Food does that. No other art form delivers the past directly into the body. We have been given the privilege of being the conduit for that moment. Do not take it lightly.

Cook with humility. Source with honesty. Tell the story with care. And remember always that the ingredient arrived at your bench long before you did, and will outlast you if you tend it well.

"Ngurra" — Country, home, the place where you belong.
