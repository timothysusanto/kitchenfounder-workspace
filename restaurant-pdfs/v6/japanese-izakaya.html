Chef's Lens
// What a Michelin-trained chef sees in Japanese izakaya cooking
Japanese cuisine operates on a philosophy of subtraction. Every ingredient that doesn't belong is removed. What remains is precise. Dashi — the foundational stock of Japanese cooking — is made from kombu and bonito, steeped at exactly 60°C, never boiled. Boiling makes it bitter. The difference between a great dashi and a mediocre one is 10 degrees. That level of precision runs through every technique in this guide. The food cost on edamame is 12%. The philosophy behind it took centuries to develop.
// What this guide does
The izakaya format works in the US because it solves a problem Americans didn't know they had: a place to drink well and eat exceptionally at the same time, without the formality of a restaurant. Sharing plates, high-margin sake and whisky, a kitchen that runs efficiently on small portions. When readers reach the financial model, the reaction is consistent: the numbers are better than expected.
This guide is why.

Method

1
Bring a large pot of heavily salted water to a rolling boil — salt level should taste noticeably salty, approximately 1 tablespoon per litre. This is not just seasoning the edamame; the salt actually penetrates the pod wall during cooking, seasoning the beans from the outside in. Under-salted water produces bland edamame no matter how much you finish them after.
2
Add frozen edamame directly to boiling water — do not thaw first. Frozen edamame dropped into boiling water experiences rapid surface blanching that sets a bright vibrant green colour before the interior has time to soften excessively. Boil exactly 4 minutes. Overcooking turns them grey and mushy; undercooked beans are starchy and chalky.
3
Drain immediately and toss while hot in a bowl with fine sea salt and togarashi. The residual heat opens the pores of the pod skin slightly, allowing the seasoning to adhere. The togarashi provides subtle heat and citrus from the yuzu peel component — a step above plain salted edamame.
4
Finish with a single small drizzle of sesame oil — less than 2ml. This is aromatic, not a coating. Serve immediately in a small wooden bowl or ceramic dish with an empty bowl beside it for discarded pods. This two-bowl presentation signals respect for the guest's comfort.
Chef's Tip: Edamame is your highest-margin item on the menu. It should land on every table within 3 minutes of seating — offered as a complimentary snack or charged as part of an izakaya cover charge. Never let a table sit for more than 5 minutes without something in front of them.

Method

1
Press silken tofu gently between paper towels for 20 minutes before service prep — silken tofu holds more water than firm, and surface moisture causes violent oil spattering and prevents the starch crust from adhering. The pressing must be gentle; silken tofu breaks easily.
2
Cut into 5cm cubes. Dust completely in katakuriko (potato starch) — not cornstarch, not flour. Potato starch creates an extremely thin, translucent, almost ethereal crust when fried correctly. It becomes slightly glossy in the broth rather than soggy, which is the signature characteristic of proper agedashi. Shake off excess starch.
3
Fry in oil at 180°C / 356°F for 2–3 minutes until the crust is set and just beginning to colour — pale golden, not deep golden. You are setting the crust, not browning the tofu. The interior must remain custardy and cool.
4
Broth (tsuyu): combine dashi, soy sauce, and mirin; bring to a gentle simmer. Pour warm broth into a deep bowl (~2cm depth), place tofu gently in broth, top with grated daikon, a dot of ginger, and sliced scallion. Serve immediately.
Chef's Tip: Train servers to deliver this dish immediately and tell guests: "Eat this one first — it's best right now." This creates urgency and demonstrates knowledge. That interaction builds loyalty.

Method

1
Cut chicken thigh into 2.5cm chunks — never use breast for yakitori. Thigh has higher fat content which self-bastes during grilling, prevents drying, and provides the characteristic juicy chew. Thread onto thin bamboo skewers alternating with 2cm scallion pieces for negi-ma style.
2
Tare (たれ) glaze: combine soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar in a small saucepan. Simmer over low heat for 8–10 minutes until reduced by a third and slightly syrupy. The tare is the heart of yakitori — make it fresh daily and keep warm. Never use bottled teriyaki sauce as a substitute.
3
Grill over high heat at 230°C / 446°F. Turn every 90 seconds. At the halfway point, brush with tare — it will caramelise immediately. Apply tare twice more. Total cook time: 6–8 minutes. Internal temp: 74°C / 165°F.
4
Serve immediately on a long rectangular plate with a small dish of extra tare for dipping. Offer the guest a choice: tare (sweet-soy glaze) or shio (sea salt + lemon). This choice is traditional and personalises the experience.
Chef's Tip: Your tare improves every day you use it. Add the grill drippings back into the pot each service — old yakitori chefs call this the "mother tare." Tell this story to guests. It creates fascination and differentiates your yakitori from the competition.

Method

1

Method

1
Cut chicken thigh into 4–5cm chunks, skin on. Marinate in soy sauce, sake, grated ginger, and garlic for minimum 2 hours (overnight is better). The sake begins to denature the chicken proteins gently before cooking, tenderising the interior. The ginger has proteolytic enzymes that further tenderise. This double enzymatic softening is what makes karaage's interior impossibly moist even after being fried twice.
2
Remove chicken from marinade; pat lightly with a paper towel — you want to remove pooled liquid but not the marinade coating. Toss in potato starch until each piece is evenly coated. Do not use flour for classical karaage. Potato starch creates a thinner, crispier, more delicate shell and stays crispy longer.
3
First fry: oil at 160°C / 320°F for 4 minutes. Remove and rest on a rack for 3 minutes. This lower-temperature first fry cooks the interior through without burning the crust. The rest period allows residual heat to finish the centre and lets surface moisture escape.
4
Second fry: oil at 185°C / 365°F for 90 seconds. This high-heat flash creates the shattering, deeply golden exterior. Internal temp: 74°C / 165°F. Drain briefly, serve immediately with kewpie mayo and lemon.
Chef's Tip: Kewpie mayo is not a substitute for regular mayo — it's made with egg yolks only, rice vinegar, and MSG. The result is richer, more umami, and tangier. It's the essential dipping companion for karaage. If you don't stock Kewpie, your karaage is missing its partner.

Method

1
Batter: whisk flour, dashi, egg, and soy sauce until smooth and lump-free. The batter should be very thin — thinner than pancake batter, almost the consistency of cream. The high dashi ratio gives savoury, deeply flavoured batter. Rest batter 10 minutes before using; this relaxes gluten and produces a lighter texture.
2
Heat a cast-iron takoyaki pan over medium-high. Brush generously with oil — every cavity. Fill each cavity with batter to just overflowing. Place one piece of octopus, a pinch of scallion, and beni shoga in each. The slightly overfilled cavity is intentional: the excess batter will be folded back in during turning to create the spherical shape.
3
After 2–3 minutes (when edges set), use a takoyaki pick to rotate each ball 90 degrees, tucking the overflowing batter underneath. Rotate again 90 degrees after another 2 minutes. The goal is a perfect sphere with a golden, evenly coloured surface. Total cook time: 8–10 minutes. The interior should be liquid-centre for authentic takoyaki.
4
Transfer immediately to a boat-shaped plate. Drizzle generously with takoyaki sauce, zigzag kewpie mayo, top with waving katsuobushi bonito flakes (they move in the heat — a dramatic, memorable effect), and finish with aonori. The bonito flakes "dancing" on the hot balls is one of the most photographed food moments in Japanese cuisine.
Chef's Tip: A live takoyaki station, visible from the dining room, is one of the best marketing tools you have. The smell, the turning technique, the dancing bonito flakes — it draws stares from every table. Consider positioning your takoyaki station where guests can watch. The performance is the brand.

Method

1
Tonkotsu broth (batch): blanch pork bones (trotters + neck bones) in boiling water 10 minutes, discard water. Transfer to fresh cold water, bring to a rolling boil, maintain that vigorous boil for 18 hours. The rolling boil is the secret: it emulsifies the collagen and fat into a creamy, opaque white broth. A simmer produces a clear, lighter broth. Tonkotsu requires violence. Final broth should coat a spoon.
2
Chashu pork belly (batch): roll pork belly tightly with butcher's twine. Sear all sides until golden. Braise in soy sauce, mirin, sake, sugar, ginger, garlic for 2.5 hours covered. Cool in braising liquid overnight — the liquid becomes tare. Slice 1cm thick per service, torch or pan-sear to order.
3
Ajitsuke tamago: soft-boil eggs exactly 6 minutes 30 seconds in boiling water, transfer immediately to ice bath. Peel and marinate in chashu braising liquid diluted 1:1 with water for minimum 4 hours. A 7-minute egg has a fully set yolk and loses its magic entirely.
4
Assembly: warm serving bowl with hot water (dump before use). Add tare to bowl. Ladle 400ml hot broth. Cook noodles in separate boiling water exactly 90 seconds. Drain, shake, add to bowl. Top with 2 chashu slices, halved soft egg, menma, nori pressed against bowl edge, scallion, sesame. Finish with sesame oil.
Chef's Tip: One batch (20 litres) serves approximately 50 bowls. Never run out of broth mid-service — make batches every 2 days, keep a minimum 24-hour buffer. Running out of tonkotsu mid-Saturday is a reputation-damaging event.

Method

1
Score the chicken thigh skin with a sharp knife in a crosshatch pattern — 3–4 cuts each direction, 3mm deep. This allows the fat underneath the skin to render out as the chicken cooks and allows the teriyaki glaze to penetrate and caramelise into the skin surface rather than just coating the top.
2
Pan-fry skin-side down in a dry pan over medium heat for 6–7 minutes. Starting in a barely warm pan allows the fat to render gradually before browning begins. Flip and cook 3 minutes more. Internal temp: 74°C / 165°F.
3
Pour teriyaki sauce (soy, mirin, sake, sugar) into the pan around the chicken — not over it. Allow to bubble and reduce, tilting the pan and spooning the sauce over the chicken repeatedly. Chef's Tip: Use Japanese-style short grain rice (Calrose or similar) cooked with a 1:1.1 water ratio. Never use long grain for Japanese rice bowls — the stickiness of short grain is what allows the sauce and protein to bind to it. Invest in a commercial rice cooker that holds rice for up to 4 hours without drying out.

Method

1
Salt the mackerel fillet generously (~1% salt by weight) 30–45 minutes before grilling. This short cure draws out excess surface moisture via osmosis and seasons the flesh throughout. If you salt immediately before cooking, the salt creates steam during cooking. If you salt more than 2 hours before, the texture becomes too firm. The 30–45 minute window is precise and important.

Method

1
Miso marinade: combine white miso, mirin, sake, and sugar. Whisk until completely smooth. White (shiro) miso is preferred over red miso because its lower salt content and higher sugar content caramelises more beautifully without burning. This is why Nobu's original recipe specifically calls for white miso.

5 Service Training

Izakaya service is warm, knowledgeable, and unhurried. Guests should feel like they're being guided by a friend who knows the food deeply — not sold to. These scripts are starting points. Personalise them to your voice.

Script 1 — The Izakaya Format Explanation

When: First table contact, after welcoming guests and presenting menus

"Welcome to [Name]. Before you dive into the menu — our food is izakaya style, which means everything comes as it's ready rather than in courses. It's meant for sharing, so we recommend ordering 2–3 dishes at a time and adding more as you go. If you'd like to start with something while you decide, our edamame and yakitori are usually the first things out of the kitchen. What can I bring you to drink first?"

Script 2 — The Yakitori Story

When: Guest asks about yakitori or shows interest in it

"Our yakitori is grilled over a very hot grill and glazed with our tare — that's the house sauce we simmer every day and build up over time. The longer we cook, the deeper it gets. You have two options: tare, which is the sweet-savoury glaze, or shio, which is just sea salt and lemon — that's actually the version yakitori purists prefer because you taste the chicken itself. First time here, I'd do half and half — try both and see which you prefer."

Script 3 — The Ramen Timing Script

When: Guest orders tonkotsu ramen or asks about it

"Excellent choice — the tonkotsu takes about 8 minutes because we cook the noodles fresh to order. Would you like me to put in some yakitori or gyoza while you wait? They'll land at your table in about 4–5 minutes, so you'll have something to eat while the ramen is coming. [Pause.] Also — one thing with our ramen: we recommend eating it right away when it arrives. The noodles continue cooking in the hot broth, so the sooner you start, the better the texture."

Script 4 — The Sake Introduction

When: Guest is undecided on drinks, or asks about sake

"If you've never tried sake with food before, this is a great place to start. Our house sake is a junmai — that means it's pure rice wine, nothing added, clean and slightly umami. We serve it in the traditional way: a small flask called a tokkuri, with two ceramic cups. You can choose warm or cold — warm sake is more aromatic and a little gentler; cold sake is crisper and pairs really well with the yakitori and karaage. Both are $9.99 for the flask. It pairs beautifully with everything we have."

Script 5 — The Dorayaki Pre-Sell

When: Taking food orders at the table

"One thing I'll mention before you finish ordering — our dorayaki takes about 10 minutes to make fresh. It's our Japanese pancake sandwich with sweet red bean paste — very traditional, really popular. If you think you might want dessert, it's worth putting in now so it's ready around the time you're finishing your mains. Shall I add it? [Pause.] If you change your mind it's easy to cancel."

6 Kitchen Prep Schedule — 7:00am to 3:00pm

The prep flow is the engine of the operation. A well-executed prep schedule means zero scrambling at service. Each role has specific ownership — Head Cook (HC), Line Cook (LC), and Steward (ST) each have defined lanes that do not overlap unless required.

Set up fryer — pre-heat to 160°C. Portion karaage and gyoza from fridge. Stage takoyaki batter and fillings. Portion edamame (weigh and bag per serve). Receive deliveries, check temps. Sanitise all surfaces. Fill side station with serving ware. Stock chopstick sets. Pre-fold paper napkins. Fill water pitchers.
8:00–9:30am

Cut and portion all proteins: mackerel (score, salt, refrigerate 30min), chicken thighs (teriyaki, yakitori, karaage). Julienne vegetables for yakisoba mise. Cut scallion, daikon, garnishes. Brew mugicha cold batch. Portion dipping sauces (ponzu, tare, kewpie). Fill condiment station. Set tables — place settings for first seating. Pre-heat serving bowls with hot water.
9:30–11:00am
Final Breakfast Prep		 Taste and adjust ramen broth — season with tare. Pre-heat miso soup base. Cook gyudon beef and onion batch (hold in bain marie). Assemble anmitsu components — check agar set. Prepare takoyaki batter — rest 10 min before service. Bread chicken katsu portions (flour → egg → panko), lay on tray. Stage yakisoba noodles — pre-portioned bags. Check fry oil temperature and quality. Polish glasses and ceramics. Fill sake service sets (tokkuri flasks + ochoko cups). Ice beer mugs in freezer. Pre-portion fukujinzuke pickles. Confirm table numbers and POS system.
11:00am–2:30pm
Lunch Service		 Expedite all mains — call tickets, plate controls. Grill yakitori to order (90 sec × 3 turns). Execute ramen bowls — noodle to plate under 90 seconds. Oversee protein temperatures across line. Fry station: karaage (double-fry), gyoza (fry-steam), katsu, takoyaki. Wok: yakisoba and stir-fry items. Monitor oil temperature and quality. Communicate timing with HC at all times. Run food from pass to tables. Clear dishes every 3–4 minutes. Replenish water and tea. Process bills, reset tables within 4 minutes of guests leaving. Restock condiments continuously.
2:30–3:00pm
Closedown		 Cool ramen broth safely — transfer to shallow containers in ice bath, refrigerate within 2 hours. Discard any chashu held over 4 hours. Label all prep containers (item + date + time). Drain and filter fry oil (check for quality — change if darkened or off-smell). Clean fryer. Wrap and refrigerate all opened proteins. Store leftover takoyaki batter (discard if older than 24h). Full sanitise all surfaces, equipment, and floor. Wash, dry, and stack all ceramics. Complete waste log and FIFO check of refrigerators. Report any stock shortages to HC before leaving.

8 Safety & Allergens

Food Safety Overview: All kitchen staff must be certified in food safety handling. Temperature logging is mandatory for all proteins and cooked items. Cross-contamination prevention protocols are in force at all stations. Review allergen information with FOH staff at every service briefing.

Critical Temperature Control

✓ SAFE SERVING TEMPS
Protein Safe Temperatures
  • Poultry (chicken thigh, breast): 74°C / 165°F
  • Pork belly, chashu: 74°C / 165°F
  • Ground pork (gyoza filling): 74°C / 165°F
  • Fish (mackerel, salmon): 63°C / 145°F
  • Miso salmon (medium preferred): 57°C / 135°F
  • Beef (yakiniku, thin-sliced): served to preference
  • Eggs (soft-boiled ramen egg): pasteurised
⚠ HOLD & DISCARD RULES
Time & Temperature Management
  • Hot hold (soups, broths): minimum 60°C / 140°F
  • Cold hold (all proteins): maximum 4°C / 40°F
  • Ramen broth: cool to 21°C within 2 hours, 4°C within 4 hours
  • Fried proteins (karaage, katsu): discard after 2 hours held
  • Chashu pork (sliced): discard after 4 hours held
  • Natto: never reheat above 50°C — serve cold or room temp
  • Takoyaki batter: discard after 24 hours refrigerated
