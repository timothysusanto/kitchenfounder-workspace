// What a Michelin-trained chef sees in Italian cooking
Italian cuisine is the most misunderstood in professional kitchens. Operators see simplicity and assume ease. They are wrong. The simplicity is the difficulty. Cacio e pepe is three ingredients — pasta, pecorino, black pepper — and it will expose every gap in your technique. The emulsification of starch water and cheese into a sauce without breaking requires temperature control, timing and practice. The food cost is $3.10. The technique is unforgiving. This guide gives you the method that makes it repeatable.
// What this guide does
What changes when you read this guide is your relationship with food cost. Italian cuisine has a reputation for being expensive to run. That reputation belongs to operators who don't know their numbers. House-made pasta costs $0.80 per portion. Sold at $19.99, that's one of the best margins in casual dining. The trattoria model works.
This guide shows you exactly how.

Dice Roma tomatoes into 8mm concassé — remove seeds and juice first. Seeds carry excess moisture which will quickly saturate the bread and make it soggy before it reaches the table. Salt the tomato lightly and let it sit 3 minutes, then drain again. This brief salt-draw removes residual liquid while seasoning the tomato from within.
2
Grill bread slices on a cast-iron griddle or over open flame at 240°C / 464°F for 90 seconds per side until deep char lines appear. The char is not cosmetic — the Maillard reaction creates complex bitter-smoky compounds that balance the acidity of the tomato. Never toast in a toaster; the direct flame contact is essential.
3
While the bread is still hot, rub one side firmly with a halved garlic clove. The rough bread surface acts as a microplane, releasing allicin directly into the bread. This is why brushing with garlic oil is an inferior substitution — the abrasion is what releases the flavour.
4
Toss tomato concassé with torn basil and olive oil immediately before plating. Basil oxidises and blackens within minutes of being cut or crushed. Mound generously on bread, drizzle with EVOO, finish with flaky salt. Serve within 90 seconds of assembly.
Chef's Tip: The quality of olive oil defines this dish more than anything else. Use a single-estate EVOO with a peppery finish — the polyphenols that create that throat-hit are also responsible for the complexity of flavour. Never use "light" or "pure" olive oil for finishing.

1
Remove burrata from refrigeration 20 minutes before service. Cold burrata straight from the fridge has a firm, rubbery stracciatella centre — allowing it to come to room temperature lets the cream filling loosen to its correct, rich, spoonable consistency. This single step dramatically changes the eating experience.
2
Place burrata whole in the centre of a chilled plate. Arrange folded Prosciutto di Parma ribbons loosely around — do not lay flat and stiff. The irregular fold creates visual drama and allows the salt-cured silk texture to be properly experienced when lifted with a fork. Never pre-cut prosciutto for this dish.
3
Scatter halved heirloom cherry tomatoes and torn basil. Season tomatoes only with flaky salt — the prosciutto provides all the saltiness the dish needs. Restraint is the entire philosophy of this plate.
4
Finish with a generous pour of EVOO over everything at the last moment. At the table, score the top of the burrata lightly so the cream begins to pour out as the guest receives the dish. This tableside moment of breaking the burrata is a genuine hospitality experience that drives memorable dining.
Chef's Tip: Burrata has a 3–4 day shelf life from production. Source from a local Italian deli or specialty importer who can guarantee 48-hour-fresh product. Burrata that is even slightly old develops an acidic, off flavour. At $6 per ball, every piece must be perfect.

1
Arancini must be made from cold risotto — never warm. Cold risotto has enough starch gel and protein structure to hold a ball shape without disintegrating during frying. Make risotto the day before service, spread thin on trays, refrigerate overnight.
2
Wet hands, scoop 80g cold risotto. Press a cube of mozzarella into the centre, then close the risotto around it completely with no gaps — any exposed mozzarella will leak during frying. Roll into a firm ball. The internal mozzarella must be completely sealed.
3
Standard breading: flour → egg wash → panko. The flour layer creates a protein surface for the egg to adhere to. Without flour, egg slides off the starchy risotto surface. Double-bread for an extra-crisp shell: flour → egg → panko → egg → panko again.
4
Deep fry at 180°C / 356°F for 4–5 minutes until deep golden-amber. The high heat creates a rigid, crackling shell while the interior reaches 74°C / 165°F, melting the mozzarella centre. Do not raise oil temperature to rush — the exterior will burn while the centre stays cold.
Chef's Tip: The risotto batch for arancini should be slightly over-seasoned and slightly over-cooked compared to table risotto — more salt and more starch ensures the arancini are flavourful after frying dilutes the seasoning, and firm enough to ball without falling apart.

1
Use only centre-cut beef tenderloin from a USDA-certified, sushi-grade or equivalent supplier. The beef must be fresh (not previously frozen), sliced to order or within 2 hours of service. Freeze the loin for 25 minutes before slicing — not to freeze solid, but to firm the muscle fibres so they slice cleanly and thinly without tearing.
2
Slice 1.5mm thin on a mandoline or with a sharp slicing knife. Lay slices between cling film and gently press flat with the back of a pan — "velo" technique. Arrange in a single overlapping layer on a chilled plate immediately.
3
Dress the carpaccio with lemon-Dijon emulsion immediately before service. The acid in the lemon begins to denature the beef proteins deliberately — dress less than 2 minutes before serving so the beef retains its vivid red colour and just-kissed-by-acid flavour.
4
Top with rocket, capers, and Parmesan shavings. The rocket's peppery bitterness cuts the richness of raw beef. Capers provide bursts of brine. Parmesan adds umami. These are not garnishes — each element serves a specific flavour and textural function. Never skip any of the three.
Chef's Tip: Carpaccio requires a raw meat advisory on your menu. It also requires impeccable supply chain hygiene. Build a relationship with a single trusted butcher for your tenderloin. Never use carpaccio as a vehicle for clearing old beef — guests can taste the difference, and the food safety implications are serious.

1
Pat squid rings completely dry with paper towels before dredging. Moisture is the enemy of crispy frying — any water on the squid surface will turn to steam in the hot oil, creating a soggy coating instead of a crispy shell. Dry the squid, then season lightly with salt before dredging so the seasoning penetrates the flesh.
2
Use a 50/50 mix of semolina and all-purpose flour. The semolina's coarser grain creates a textured, crunchy exterior with more surface area than plain flour alone. Do not use a wet batter — traditional Italian calamari fritti uses only dry flour, which allows the flavour of fresh squid to shine through.
3
Fry in batches — never crowd the fryer. Crowding drops oil temperature below 180°C / 356°F and causes the squid to steam rather than fry. Small batches (100g max) at a constant temperature produce consistently crispy results. Total fry time: 2–2.5 minutes only. Overcooked squid toughens irreversibly.
4
Drain immediately on a wire rack (not paper towel — the bottom will steam). Season with flaky salt the instant they leave the fryer while the surface is still tacky with oil. Serve within 3 minutes or the coating softens from residual moisture.
Chef's Tip: The secret to non-rubbery calamari is freshness and timing. Fresh squid (not frozen) has better moisture content and texture. The 2.5-minute fry time is non-negotiable — every extra 30 seconds adds noticeable toughness. Set a timer every single time.

1
Sweat diced onion and garlic in olive oil over medium heat for 12–15 minutes until fully translucent and beginning to caramelise. This low-and-slow cooking converts the harsh, sharp compounds in raw onion into sweet, complex sugars. A rushed 5-minute sweat produces a soup with a raw, sharp undertone that no seasoning can fix.
2
Add San Marzano tomatoes whole (crush by hand into the pot). San Marzanos are the critical ingredient — their low acidity, thick flesh, and natural sweetness produce a soup that needs minimal sugar correction. Standard canned tomatoes can be used in a pinch but require added sugar and longer cooking.
3
Simmer 20 minutes uncovered. The uncovered simmer allows moisture to evaporate and the tomato to concentrate. Add fresh basil in the last 5 minutes only — prolonged cooking destroys the volatile aromatic oils that make basil distinctive.
4
Blend until silky smooth, pass through a fine-mesh strainer. Serve at 74°C / 165°F. Swirl heavy cream decoratively on top — do not stir in. The cream swirl provides visual elegance and the guest experience of combining richness with acidity in each spoonful.
Chef's Tip: This soup is a profit leader — low food cost, batch-produced, holds well under refrigeration for 3 days. Make a large batch every two days. Keep it in a bain-marie at 78°C and test with a thermometer every service.
