The Holy Trinity of American BBQ: Brisket, ribs, and pulled pork are the pillars of serious American barbecue — a cuisine built on patience, fire management, and an almost obsessive relationship with smoke. The low-and-slow method (225–250°F / 107–121°C for 10–16 hours) transforms tough, collagen-rich cuts into fork-tender masterpieces by converting connective tissue (collagen) to gelatin over time. The smoke ring — that pink layer beneath the bark — is formed when nitric oxide from burning wood reacts with myoglobin in the meat. The bark is the Maillard reaction combined with smoke particulates, salt, pepper, and rendered fat binding together over 12+ hours. The stall (160–170°F internal temp) is evaporative cooling as intramuscular fat begins to render. Understanding these three phenomena separates a genuine pitmaster from someone with a smoker.
// What a Michelin-trained chef sees in American BBQ
American BBQ is the only major cuisine where the cook cannot see the food while it cooks. The brisket is in the smoker for 12–16 hours. The only feedback is the temperature probe, the smell of the smoke, and the feel of the bark when you press it. The stall — the plateau between 150–170°F where evaporative cooling arrests the temperature rise for hours — is the moment that separates experienced pitmasters from beginners. Beginners panic and raise the temperature. Experienced pitmasters wait. The smoke ring, the bark, the pull test: these are not aesthetic details. They are the technical evidence that the collagen has converted to gelatin, the fat has rendered, and the meat is done. Post oak is not a preference. It is the correct wood for Central Texas brisket.

Method

1
Source the point correctly: Burnt ends come from the point (deckle) of the brisket — the fatty, thicker end, not the flat. The point has more intramuscular fat and connective tissue, which means it survives double cooking without drying out. After the primary 12-hour smoke, separate point from flat by cutting through the natural fat seam. The point should be deeply barked and yielding. Internal temp should already be at 203°F / 95°C.
2
Cube and toss: Cut point into 1.5-inch cubes. Toss with brown sugar, a splash of apple cider vinegar, and half the BBQ sauce. The vinegar cuts richness; the sugar creates caramelisation in the second cook. Do not skip either.
3
Second smoke/oven: Place cubes on a wire rack or in a cast iron skillet. Return to smoker at 275°F / 135°C uncovered for 60–90 minutes, or finish in a 300°F convection oven. The goal is to render any remaining fat and create a sticky, lacquered bark on all surfaces. Stir once at 45 minutes and add remaining sauce + butter. The butter emulsifies with the rendered fat to create a glossy, clingy glaze.
4
Plate immediately: Burnt ends are best served the moment they come out of the second cook. 6 cubes per portion, sauce spooned over, pickles on the side. If prepping in advance, hold in a covered hotel pan with a thin layer of warm sauce at 140°F / 60°C for service. Do not hold longer than 90 minutes or the crust softens.
Chef's Tip: Burnt ends are a by-product of the brisket programme — you cannot make them without running a full brisket operation. They are however one of your highest-margin and most emotionally compelling items. When a table orders burnt ends as a starter, the pitmaster story is already being told before the main arrives. Never skip them from the menu.

Method

Method

1
Dry brine with rub — 4 hours minimum: Apply dry rub generously to wings, coating all surfaces. Refrigerate uncovered for at least 4 hours (overnight preferred). The salt draws moisture to the surface, which reabsorbs, seasoning the meat deeply. Uncovered refrigeration also dries the skin surface — essential for achieving crispy skin in a low-heat smoker environment.
2
Smoke at 225°F / 107°C for 90–120 minutes: Use a blend of hickory and fruit wood (apple or cherry). Wings should reach 165°F / 74°C internal at the thickest point of the drumette. The skin will not be crispy at this stage — that comes next.
3
Finish on high-heat grill for crispiness: Transfer smoked wings to a 450°F+ grill or broiler for 3–4 minutes per side. Only intense radiant heat renders the subcutaneous fat and crisps the skin. Without this step you have rubbery-skin smoked wings.
4
Hold and plate: Batch-smoke wings in the morning. Hold pre-grilled in a hotel pan at 140°F / 60°C. Fire the grill finish to order (3–4 min). Plate 6 wings, ranch on the side, celery, and blue cheese crumble.
Chef's Tip: The house dry rub ratio: 3 parts paprika, 2 parts brown sugar, 2 parts kosher salt, 1 part black pepper, 1 part garlic powder, ½ part cayenne, ½ part cumin, ½ part dry mustard. Make this in 1kg batches.

Method

1
Salt-wilt for crunch: Toss shredded cabbage with 1 tsp kosher salt, let stand 15 minutes, then squeeze out excess moisture. Salt-wilted cabbage retains its crunch for 4–6 hours dressed; unsalted cabbage turns limp within 30 minutes.
2
Dress with restraint: Combine mayonnaise, apple cider vinegar, celery seed, sugar, salt, and pepper. This should be a lightly dressed slaw — the vinegar acidity is what makes slaw work as a palate cleanser alongside fatty brisket and pork.

4
Batch management: Prep 2 batches daily — a morning batch (for lunch service) and an afternoon batch (for dinner). Do not carry slaw from lunch to dinner; dressed cabbage past 4 hours becomes limp and watery.

Method — The Complete 12-Hour Protocol

1
Trim and rub (night before, 8pm): Trim fat cap to ¼-inch. Apply SPG rub generously (salt + coarse black pepper, 50/50 by volume, optionally garlic powder). Let rest uncovered in walk-in overnight — this dries the surface slightly, aiding bark development. SMOKE SCIENCE The Maillard reaction requires a dry surface. A wet brisket entering a smoker will steam rather than crust.
2
Smoker at 225–250°F / 107–121°C, post oak, 5am start: Post oak is the canonical Texas brisket wood — it burns clean and long, producing a medium-weight smoke that penetrates without overwhelming. Maintain fire temperature at 225–250°F. Place brisket fat-side up. The smoke ring forms in the first 4 hours only — once internal temp exceeds 140°F / 60°C, the ring stops developing. SMOKE RING SCIENCE
3
Managing the stall (160–170°F internal, typically 10am–12pm): At approximately 160°F / 71°C internal temperature, the brisket will stop rising for 2–4 hours. This is evaporative cooling. New pitmasters panic and raise the temperature; experienced ones wait. Texas Crutch (optional): Wrap in pink butcher paper at 165°F to accelerate through the stall while allowing some steam to escape. Target final internal: 203°F / 95°C. STALL SCIENCE
4
Rest 1–2 hours before slicing: Wrap in butcher paper, place in an empty cooler, rest minimum 1 hour. Slice across the grain in ¼-inch slices. The flat and point have grains running different directions — separate and slice each correctly. Serve on butcher paper with white bread and pickles: Texas BBQ tradition.
Chef's Tip: The 203°F target is not about time — it is about collagen conversion, complete when the probe meets zero resistance (the "probe slide" or "butter test"). Some briskets hit this at 195°F; some need 210°F. The number is a guide; the feel is the truth. Invest in a good wireless meat thermometer.

Method

1
Membrane removal — non-negotiable: The silvery membrane on the bone-side of spare ribs must be removed. It is tough, inedible, and blocks smoke penetration. Use a butter knife to loosen one corner, grip with a paper towel, and peel in one piece. St. Louis cut means sternum bone, cartilage tips, and skirt flap have been removed. Apply dry rub to both sides and rest 2 hours minimum.
2
225°F for 3 hours, unwrapped: Place ribs bone-side down. Cherry wood adds sweetness and deep mahogany colour; post oak provides foundational smoke flavour. Spritz with 50/50 apple cider vinegar and water every 60–75 minutes.
3
The 3-2-1 decision: After 3 hours, assess bark development. For maximum bark, go unwrapped the full 5 hours at 225°F. Target internal temp: 195–203°F / 90–95°C — or use the bend test: if the rack bends 90° and the bark cracks slightly, it is done.
4
Rest 20 minutes, slice and serve: Rest wrapped 20 minutes. Slice between bones. Each bone should have a clean pull — meat detaches with slight resistance. BBQ sauce on the side. Plate on butcher paper with coleslaw and pickles.
Chef's Tip: St. Louis has more fat, more cartilage, and more flavour than baby backs. They take longer (5 hours vs 4) but produce a richer, more complex eating experience. For a BBQ joint, St. Louis is the professional's choice for standalone rib orders.

Method

Method

Understanding the cut — plate vs chuck: Plate short ribs (USDA 123 cut, bone-in) are what you want. These are thick-cut single bones (3–4 inch meat cap above the bone) with extreme intramuscular fat.
