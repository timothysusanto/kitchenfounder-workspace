💡 15 Tips for Pitching Investors
1. Lead with the customer, not the food. Investors fund businesses, not menus. Start with: "There are 2.3M people in [city] who want [X] and nobody is serving them well."
2. Know your numbers cold. Revenue, food cost %, labour %, break-even. If you hesitate on any of these, you lose credibility instantly.
3. Don't oversell the market. "The global restaurant industry is $3 trillion" means nothing. Show the opportunity in your specific city, neighbourhood, and format.
4. Show proof of concept. Pop-up, catering gig, supper club — anything that proves people will pay for your food. No track record = higher perceived risk.
5. Have a clear use of funds breakdown. Fit-out 60%, equipment 20%, working capital 13%, marketing 7%. Vague answers here kill deals.
6. Know your comparables. What did a similar concept in your city raise? What are they valued at? Show you've done the research.
7. Name your unfair advantage. Why you? Why this location? Why now? Could be: supplier relationships, a cult following, a format that doesn't exist locally, a team that's done it before.
8. Address the risk head-on. "The biggest risk is X, and here's how we've mitigated it." Investors respect founders who see clearly, not founders who pretend there are no risks.
9. Leave a one-pager behind. Every meeting, every time. They'll show it to their partner/spouse/accountant. Your pitch continues after you leave the room.
10. Follow up within 24 hours. Email recap, key figures, next step. Most founders don't do this. It sets you apart immediately.
11. Bank pitch vs angel pitch are different. Banks want security (lease, assets, personal guarantee). Angels want upside (equity, growth story, exit). Tailor each version.
12. SBA loan requirements. Personal credit 680+, 10-20% down, 2 years business plan, personal financial statement. Takes 60-90 days. Start early.
13. What investors actually worry about. In order: operator experience, location, competition, lease terms, your personal financial commitment. Address all five.
14. Red flags that kill deals. No experience, no skin in the game (you're investing nothing), no market research, unrealistic financial projections, bad location rationale.
15. The close. Don't ask for the money on first contact. Ask for a second meeting. "I'd love 30 minutes to walk you through the full numbers — when works for you this week?"
