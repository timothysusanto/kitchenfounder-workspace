The Nama Philosophy · The Yeast Science · Japanese Context02
What Is a Nama Donut — And Why Japan Changed Everything
In 2019, a small donut shop in Tokyo began selling donuts that disappeared from the counter before noon every day. They weren't spectacular looking — no towering cream rosettes, no rainbow glazes. They were pale, round, slightly undersized, and yielded to the fingers like cold butter. Inside: raw cream, barely set, pooling around the bite. They became the most sought-after donut in Japan within eighteen months.

The Japanese word nama (生) means raw, fresh, or unprocessed. Nama chocolate (生チョコ) is the truffle-textured ganache that Royce made famous. Nama beer (生ビール) is draft beer — unpasteurised, alive. Nama donut is a donut made with living yeast, cold-fermented over 12–18 hours, fried at the lowest temperature that still achieves colour, and filled with cream that is barely set — served within 4 hours of filling.

The result is a donut that tastes nothing like a Western donut. The crumb is so soft it compresses like foam and springs back. The filling is cold, alive, almost liquid at the centre. The glaze is thin and barely-there. The flavours are restrained — this is Japanese pastry philosophy: one clear flavour, executed at the highest possible level, served in the right window of time.

The Yeast — Why It's Different

The single most important ingredient in a nama donut is the yeast. This is not a metaphor. Standard commercial yeast produces a consistent, predictable rise with a neutral flavour. Japanese craft donut makers use a different approach entirely — and understanding it is the difference between a good donut and a transcendent one.

Sake Lees Yeast (酒粕酵母) — The Nama Donut Yeast

Sake kasu (sake lees) is the solid byproduct of sake brewing — the pressed rice mash left after the sake is drawn off. It contains living yeast cells (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same species as commercial yeast, but a different strain selected over centuries for sake fermentation), along with amino acids, enzymes, and small amounts of residual alcohol and sugars.

When used in dough, sake lees yeast contributes:

  • A faint floral, rice-based umami note in the finished dough — detectable as "something complex" even to those who don't know what it is
  • A slower, cooler fermentation that produces a finer, more even crumb bubble structure
  • Slight residual alcohol that evaporates during frying but contributes to crust development and browning at lower temperatures
  • Enzymes that break down some of the gluten and starch over the long cold proof, creating the characteristic tender, almost mochi-like bite

Where to source: Japanese grocery stores (Fuji Mart, Maruyu, Japan Centre) · online: Japan Centre UK, Umami Insider, Amazon Japan import · If unavailable: substitute 50g fresh yeast + 1 tsp sake for every 10g sake lees yeast called for.

The Tangzhong Method — The Other Japanese Secret

Many Japanese nama donut recipes combine sake lees yeast with the tangzhong (湯種) technique — cooking a small portion of the flour and milk together into a paste before adding it to the dough. At 65°C the starch in the flour gelatinises, absorbing up to 5× its weight in water. This pre-gelatinised starch is incorporated into the dough and creates extraordinary moisture retention and softness that doesn't go stale. The result is the "Hokkaido milk bread" texture — cotton-soft, feather-light, stays soft for 24+ hours rather than 4.

  • Tangzhong ratio: 5% of flour weight (25g flour) + 5× water weight (125g water) → cooked to 65°C → cooled completely before adding to dough
  • This is added in addition to, not instead of, the other milk in the recipe
  • Makes the dough slightly stickier to handle but the result is worth it

生ドーナツ生地 — Nama Donut Dough
Sake Lees Yeast · Tangzhong · Cold 15-Hour Ferment
Foundation Recipe · Yields 16–18 Donuts
Nama Donut Dough
生ドーナツ生地
Sake lees yeast · tangzhong starch paste · cold 15-hr ferment · fry at 160°C · cloud-soft crumb
Fry 160–165°C · 90 sec per side Active 50 min · Cold proof 14–16 hrs Yields 16–18 nama donuts
Ingredients
Tangzhong Paste (make first)
Bread flour (strong)25 g
Whole milk125 ml
Main Dough — Dry
Bread flour (strong)475 g
Caster sugar55 g
Fine sea salt8 g
Skimmed milk powder20 g
Main Dough — Wet
Whole eggs2 large (100 g)
Egg yolks3 (60 g)
Whole milk, warm (30°C)80 ml
Sake (dry junmai), room temp30 ml
Vanilla bean paste1 tsp
Yeast Activation
Sake lees (酒粕) paste40 g
Warm water (32°C)50 ml
Caster sugar (to activate)1 tsp
If No Sake Lees Available
Fresh compressed yeast15 g
Sake (for flavour)1 tsp
Butter — Cold, Cubed
Unsalted butter, cold150 g
Frying + Finishing
Neutral oil (rice bran)2 litres
Icing sugar, sifted (light dusting only)30 g
Method
  1. 1Tangzhong paste: whisk 25g flour into 125ml cold milk in a small saucepan until smooth — no lumps. Place over medium heat, whisking constantly. The paste will thicken suddenly between 60–65°C — continue whisking until it resembles thick wallpaper paste and leaves a brief trail when a spoon is dragged through. Remove from heat immediately. Transfer to a clean bowl. Press cling film directly on the surface. Cool completely to room temperature before using — at least 30 minutes. Do not rush; adding warm tangzhong to cold butter will melt it prematurely.
  2. 2Activate sake lees yeast: break 40g sake lees paste into 50ml warm water (32°C) in a small bowl. Add 1 tsp sugar. Whisk vigorously to dissolve — the sake lees will not fully dissolve and that is fine; the active yeast cells will disperse into the liquid. Leave 15–20 minutes until slightly foamy and you can detect a faint yeasty, floral fragrance. If using fresh yeast instead, crumble into warm water with sugar and leave 10 minutes until frothy.
  3. 3In the bowl of a stand mixer fitted with a dough hook, combine bread flour, sugar, salt, and milk powder. Add the cooled tangzhong paste, eggs, yolks, warm milk, sake, vanilla, and the activated sake lees yeast water (strain if using sake lees to remove undissolved solids). Mix on medium speed for 4–5 minutes until a cohesive dough forms. It will be sticky — this is correct.
  4. 4Cold butter incorporation: with the mixer on medium-low, add cold butter one cube at a time, waiting for each to be mostly incorporated before adding the next. This takes 12–14 minutes total — longer than standard brioche because the tangzhong creates more resistance. Be patient. After all butter is incorporated, increase to medium-high and knead 8 minutes until the dough is smooth, glossy, and passes the windowpane test. The dough will be softer and more extensible than standard brioche — this is the tangzhong effect.
  5. 5Shape into a smooth ball. Place in a lightly oiled bowl. Cover tightly with cling film. Refrigerate 14–16 hours (overnight). The sake lees yeast works slowly at 4°C, developing a complex fermentation flavour that commercial yeast cannot replicate. The dough will double slowly overnight.
  6. 6Remove dough from refrigerator. It will be cold, firm, and easy to work with — a benefit of the long cold proof. Turn onto a very lightly floured surface. Roll to exactly 2.5 cm thickness. Cut circles with a 7–8 cm round cutter (no hole — these are filled donuts). Place each circle on a 10×10 cm square of baking paper. Transfer to trays. Cover loosely with cling film.
  7. 7Final proof at room temperature: 1.5–2 hours at 22–24°C. The donuts will puff to about 3–3.5 cm tall and should jiggle gently when the tray is shaken — this is the "wobble test." Do not over-proof — an over-proofed nama donut collapses slightly during frying and loses the characteristic domed top.
  8. 8Fry at 160–165°C: this is lower than a standard donut (170°C). The tangzhong and sake lees create sugars that brown faster; lower temperature allows the interior to cook through without darkening the crust too quickly. Lower donuts (on their baking paper squares) into the oil. The paper releases naturally. Fry exactly 90 seconds per side. The colour should be a pale golden — not deep golden like an American donut. The nama aesthetic is deliberately restrained in colour.
  9. 9Remove. Drain on a wire rack 3 minutes. Do not sugar immediately — nama donuts are finished with a light dusting of icing sugar (not a heavy sugar roll) or glazed directly. Cool to room temperature before filling. This is non-negotiable — filling a warm nama donut melts the cold cream and destroys the textural contrast that defines the style.
The Critical Difference — Why Nama Frying Is Different Standard donuts fry at 170–180°C for 2 minutes per side and come out deep gold. Nama donuts fry at 160–165°C for 90 seconds per side and come out pale gold — almost cream-coloured in some versions. The visual restraint is deliberate: the pale crust is softer and more yielding to the bite, allowing the filling to be the textural and flavour statement rather than the crust. If your nama donuts come out dark gold, your oil is too hot. If they're pale and slightly greasy-feeling, the oil is too cool or the donuts were under-proofed. The sweet spot is narrow — use a reliable thermometer and clip it to the pot.

Sakura Double Cream
桜生クリーム — Sakura Nama Cream
Salted cherry blossom cream · sakura glaze · pickled sakura petal · freeze-dried strawberry dust
Cream whipped cold · Fill day of service Prep 20 min · Infuse 2 hrs Fills 6 donuts
Ingredients
Sakura Double Cream Filling
Double cream (48% fat, cold)300 ml
Mascarpone, cold80 g
Salted sakura blossoms (塩漬け桜)15 g
Icing sugar, sifted30 g
Sakura extract or rose water½ tsp
Pink gel food colouring1 tiny drop
Sakura Glaze
Icing sugar, sifted180 g
Sakura blossom infusion (see step 1)3 tbsp
Pink gel colouring2–3 drops
To Finish
Preserved sakura blossoms (desalted)1 per donut
Freeze-dried strawberry dustfine dusting
White edible pearl dustoptional shimmer
Method
  1. 1Sakura infusion: rinse salted sakura blossoms under cold water 3 times to remove excess salt — they should taste pleasantly salty, not overwhelmingly so. Soak 10 of them in 80ml cold water for 2 hours. Strain, pressing gently. Reserve the pale pink blossom-scented liquid for the glaze. Reserve remaining 6 blossoms for the finishing garnish.
  2. 2Sakura cream: place double cream, mascarpone, and icing sugar in a cold bowl (refrigerate bowl and whisk for 15 minutes first — cold equipment = more stable cream). Whip on medium speed until soft peaks form. Add sakura extract or rose water and one tiny drop of pink gel colouring. Continue whipping to firm but still glossy peaks. The cream must hold its shape when the donut is cut but still flow slightly at room temperature — this is the nama characteristic. Do not over-whip to butter.
  3. 3Fill: transfer cream to a piping bag with a 1.5 cm plain tip or star tip. Pierce the side of each donut with a skewer. Insert nozzle and pipe generously — 45–55g per donut. The donut should feel heavier and you should feel resistance. Alternatively, cut a shallow well in the top and pipe an open rosette (this is the modern Japanese café style — visible, abundant cream).
  4. 4Sakura glaze: mix sakura infusion water into sifted icing sugar. Add pink colouring to a soft blush. The glaze should be thick but flowing. Dip the top half of each filled donut (or just the rim if doing open-top style) into the glaze. Set on wire rack 5 minutes.
  5. 5Gently press one desalted sakura blossom into the glaze while still slightly wet — it should sit flat and visible. Dust the entire donut with a fine veil of freeze-dried strawberry powder using a fine sieve from 30cm height. Add a whisper of pearl dust if desired. Serve within 3 hours.
The Nama Moment

Nama #01 is the seasonal statement donut — available only during sakura season (late March to mid April in Japan; available year-round from preserved blossoms). Present on a pale grey ceramic plate with one fresh sakura branch if available, or a single preserved blossom on the side. The blush pink glaze, white cream, and cherry blossom petal create an image that is inseparable from the Japanese spring aesthetic. The flavour is delicate — barely floral, lightly salted, cold cream against the soft dough. There is nothing aggressive about it. That restraint is the luxury.

Diplomat set 4 hrs · Toffee 118°C Prep 35 min · Set 4 hrs Fills 6 donuts

Hojicha Diplomat Cream

Full-cream milk280 ml
Egg yolks3 large

Cornflour20 g

Caster sugar100 g

Hojicha Glaze
Hojicha tea liquid (strained, strong)40 ml
Icing sugar180 g
Brown food colouring (optional)1 drop
To Finish
Toasted rice puffs (see Donut 05 method)handful
Edible gold leafmicro fragment per donut
Hojicha powderfine dusting
Method
  1. 1Hojicha infusion: bring milk to a simmer. Add hojicha leaves. Remove from heat. Steep 12 minutes (hojicha can be steeped longer than green tea without bitterness — it's already roasted). Strain through a very fine sieve, pressing hard to extract all the roasted tea depth. You should have about 220ml of deep amber-brown, smoky-sweet milk.
  2. 2Soak gelatine in cold water. Whisk egg yolks, sugar, and cornflour until pale. Temper hot hojicha milk into egg mixture. Return to pan. Cook to 82°C, stirring constantly, until thick. Remove from heat. Squeeze gelatine and add. Add butter. Mix until smooth. The colour: warm caramel-brown, deeply inviting. Cool with cling film on surface. Refrigerate 4 hours until fully set.
  3. 3Salted toffee: dry caramel — cook sugar without stirring to a deep amber. Off heat, add warm cream slowly (it will bubble hard). Stir until smooth. Add butter and fleur de sel. Cool to room temperature. Transfer to a small piping bag or squeeze bottle. The toffee should be fluid enough to ribbon but not so thin it floods out — warm slightly if too thick.
  4. 4Whisk the set hojicha cream to loosen. Fold in whipped cream (diplomat technique). Fill donuts generously. For open-top style: cut a well, pipe diplomat cream in a dome, drizzle toffee ribbon over the top in a figure-eight or spiral before the cream sets fully.
  5. 5Dip the donut base or sides in hojicha glaze. Press toasted rice puffs into glaze while wet. Apply one micro-fragment of gold leaf to the toffee drizzle with tweezers. Dust with hojicha powder from a fine sieve. The visual: warm earth tones, gold shimmer, amber toffee gloss.
The Nama Moment

Hojicha's roasted, almost caramel warmth is the most approachable flavour in the Japanese tea family — guests who won't try matcha will almost always love hojicha. The salted toffee ribbon adds textural contrast and cuts the richness of the cream. Present on warm cedar wood or a brown ceramic plate. The aroma when the box is opened — toasted tea, warm caramel — sells the donut before the first bite.

Uji Matcha & White Chocolate
宇治抹茶 — Uji Matcha
Ceremonial Uji matcha cream · white chocolate ganache core · azuki red bean · matcha glaze · gold
Ganache 45°C · Cream cold Prep 30 min · Set 3 hrs Fills 6 donuts
Ingredients
Uji Matcha Cream
Double cream (48% fat), cold280 ml
Mascarpone, cold60 g
Uji ceremonial matcha (宇治抹茶)10 g
Icing sugar, sifted35 g
Fine salt1 pinch
White Chocolate Ganache Core
White chocolate (Valrhona Opalys 33%)100 g
Double cream50 ml
Matcha powder3 g (for slight colour)
Azuki Red Bean
Canned tsubuan azuki (coarse paste)120 g
Caster sugar (if adjusting sweetness)1 tsp optional
Matcha Glaze
White chocolate, melted120 g
Uji matcha powder8 g
Neutral oil1 tbsp
To Finish
Matcha powder (extra, fine sieve)dusting
Edible gold leaf1 fragment per donut
Azuki beans (whole, reserved)3–4 per donut
Method
  1. 1White chocolate ganache core (make first): heat cream until steaming. Pour over chopped white chocolate. Stir from centre. Add 3g matcha. Mix until smooth — the ganache will be a very pale jade. Pour into a small tray to a depth of 1.5 cm. Set in the refrigerator 3 hours until firm. Once set, cut into 2 cm × 2 cm squares. These ganache squares are placed inside the donut before the cream is piped — creating a double filling that the Japanese café style is known for.
  2. 2Uji matcha cream: sift matcha twice through a fine mesh strainer — lumps in matcha cream are unacceptable at this level. Combine cold double cream, mascarpone, sifted icing sugar, sifted matcha, and salt in a cold bowl. Whip on medium to firm peaks. The colour should be a vivid, pure green — not olive, not grey-green. Only ceremonial or premium culinary grade Uji matcha achieves this colour. Taste: it should be clearly matcha, slightly sweet, with no bitterness.
  3. 3Double-fill technique: make a well in the top of each donut (1.5 cm deep × 2.5 cm wide using a melon baller or small spoon). Place one azuki paste spoonful (15g) at the base of the well. Press one ganache square on top of the azuki. Pipe matcha cream generously in a dome shape above the surface of the donut — 50–60g cream total. The donut now has three layers: azuki bottom, ganache middle, matcha cream top.
  4. 4Matcha glaze: melt white chocolate over bain marie. Add matcha and oil. Whisk until smooth. Keep at 42°C. Drizzle over the base of the donut (the underside and sides) using a spoon — do not dip the top as this covers the cream rosette. Alternatively, drizzle in a spiral pattern across the cream.
  5. 5Dust the cream lightly with matcha through a fine sieve. Press 3–4 whole azuki beans into the cream for texture and visual authenticity. Apply gold leaf to the highest point of the cream dome with tweezers.
The Nama Moment

The triple-layer interior of this donut — earthy azuki, creamy ganache, grassy matcha — is a condensed version of wagashi (Japanese traditional confection) philosophy. When cut in half for display, the layers are visible and beautiful. The Uji prefix matters: Uji matcha comes from Uji City, Kyoto, and is the highest grade in Japan. Using the word "Uji" on your menu is a statement of quality that Japanese and matcha-literate guests will notice and pay for.

Milk or cream3 tbsp

To Finish

Method
  1. 1Shiro an preparation: shiro an (白あん) is a smooth white bean paste — the Japanese equivalent of white marzipan in texture but made from white kidney beans or lima beans. It is subtly sweet, faintly nutty, and extraordinarily smooth when well-made. Warm the canned paste in a small saucepan with butter and icing sugar over low heat, stirring until silky and loose enough to pipe. Season with a pinch of salt. Cool to room temperature. Transfer to a piping bag.
  2. 2Vanilla diplomat cream: heat milk with the split vanilla bean. Steep 15 minutes off heat — the milk should be deeply aromatic. Soak gelatine. Whisk egg yolks, sugar, and cornflour. Temper hot vanilla milk into egg mixture (strain out vanilla bean pod). Return to pan and cook to 80°C until thick and glossy. Add gelatine and butter. Mix until smooth. The cream should have visible black vanilla specks throughout — this is a quality signal. Cool with cling on surface. Refrigerate 4 hours until set. Then fold in whipped cream diplomat-style.
  3. 3Kinako coating: combine kinako, icing sugar, and salt in a wide shallow bowl. Kinako has a remarkable flavour — roasted, nutty, almost peanut-butter adjacent but distinctly Japanese. Rolling the base of the finished donut in kinako is a traditional wagashi technique that adds fragrance, a matte finish, and a grounding earthiness that contrasts beautifully with the floral vanilla.
  4. 4Assembly: make a top well in each donut. Pipe shiro an into the base of the well (20g). Pipe vanilla diplomat cream in a generous open rosette on top of the shiro an (40–50g). While the cream is set but not firm, lightly press the base of the donut into the kinako mix to coat the bottom third.
  5. 5Make vanilla bean glaze. Drizzle lightly over the top of the cream rosette. Microplane a very light grating of tonka bean (a small amount — tonka is intense and coumarin-rich). Press one small piece of dried vanilla bean pod into the cream at an angle for visual elegance.
The Nama Moment

This is the most Japanese donut in the collection — shiro an, kinako, and vanilla is a flavour combination that exists nowhere in Western pastry. To Japanese guests, the smell of kinako is immediate comfort memory — school lunch, childhood, Kyoto. To non-Japanese guests, it tastes like something familiar they can't quite identify, and that mystery is compelling. This donut should be explained by staff: "shiro an is white bean paste — it's like a very refined, less sweet marzipan. The kinako is roasted soybean flour. Together with the vanilla, it's our most Japanese flavour." When staff tell this story correctly, it's the donut that sells out first.

Yuzu Honey Mascarpone

Freeze-dried yuzu powder or slicesto garnish

Method
  1. 1Yuzu curd: whisk eggs, yolks, sugar, yuzu juice, and zest in a heatproof bowl. Place over a bain marie (barely simmering water — the bowl should not touch the water). Cook, whisking constantly, until the curd thickens enough to coat the back of a spoon and hold a clear trail — this takes 10–15 minutes and requires patience. The temperature should reach 82°C. Remove from heat. Whisk in cold butter cubes one at a time — emulsifying them into the warm curd creates a silky, rich texture. Strain through a fine sieve to remove zest.   2. 2Honey mascarpone cream: combine cold mascarpone, cold double cream, honey, and salt in a cold bowl. Whip on medium speed until firm peaks — 3–4 minutes. The honey adds a delicate floral sweetness and prevents the cream from tasting one-dimensional. Be careful not to over-whip — honey mascarpone turns grainy faster than plain cream.
  3. 3Assembly (dual filling): make a well in the top of each donut. Spoon yuzu curd into the base of the well (20g) — it should pool slightly, vivid yellow. Pipe honey mascarpone cream in a generous rosette over the yuzu curd, allowing the yellow to be visible around the edges of the cream. The colour contrast — pale ivory cream, vivid yellow curd — is the visual statement.
  4. 4Dip the side base of each donut in yuzu-honey glaze. Set on rack. Dust lightly with freeze-dried yuzu powder. Lay 2 strips of candied yuzu peel across the cream at a slight angle. At service, drizzle ½ tsp of acacia honey directly over the cream in a slow, deliberate arc — the honey drizzle catches the light and completes the visual.
The Nama Moment

Yuzu is the most distinctive citrus ingredient in Japanese cuisine — simultaneously lemon, mandarin, and grapefruit but none of them exactly. Non-Japanese guests invariably ask what the flavour is. That question is your most powerful selling moment: "This is yuzu — a Japanese citrus that doesn't have an English equivalent. You can't grow it outside Japan." The storytelling is built into the ingredient. Present on a white plate with a single fresh yuzu leaf (ornamental) if available, or a small pot of honey on the side for guests who want more sweetness.

Method
  1. 1Sesame tuile (make 1–2 days ahead): whisk egg whites lightly. Add icing sugar and mix until smooth. Add flour and melted butter. Fold in sesame seeds. Refrigerate batter 1 hour. Spread very thinly on a silpat-lined tray in rough oval shapes (10 cm long, 2 cm wide) using an offset spatula. Bake at 170°C for 8–10 minutes until golden and lace-like. Cool completely on the tray — do not move until fully crisp. Store in an airtight container with silica gel. These are the architectural garnish: a lace-thin sesame crisp that stands upright in the cream.
  2. 2Neri goma cream: neri goma (練りごま) is pure black sesame paste — the Japanese equivalent of tahini but richer, darker, and more intensely flavoured. Whip together cold double cream, mascarpone, neri goma, icing sugar, and salt. The cream will turn a deep slate grey — remarkable and unusual. Whip to firm peaks. Taste: it should be deeply sesame, slightly bitter at the back, rich, with the salt cutting through cleanly.
  3. 3Shiro miso caramel: dry caramel — cook 100g sugar to deep amber without stirring. Off heat, add warm cream carefully. Stir smooth. Cool to 60°C. Add miso and butter. The miso's salt and umami turn this caramel into something extraordinary — savoury, complex, layered. Add fleur de sel. Cool to room temperature. Transfer to piping bag or squeeze bottle.
  4. 4Assembly: cut a well in the top of each donut. Pipe miso caramel into the base of the well (15g). Pipe neri goma cream over the top in a generous open dome — 45–50g. The grey cream against the amber caramel visible at the base creates a dramatic visual.
  5. 5Drizzle a small amount of miso caramel over the cream in a deliberate arc. Press 3–4 fleur de sel crystals into the cream — they should be large enough to be visible. Add a pinch of loose sesame seeds. Insert one sesame tuile upright into the cream at a slight angle — it stands like a sail. Apply one micro-fragment of gold leaf to the tuile with tweezers. This is the most architecturally ambitious donut in the collection.
The Nama Moment

The combination of bitter black sesame, savoury miso, burnt sugar caramel, and fleur de sel is entirely without precedent in Western pastry — it is a purely Japanese flavour architecture built on umami as the primary note with sweetness as counterpoint rather than lead. This is the chef's donut: the one that pastry professionals and food writers will photograph and discuss. Place it at the centre of any display. When cut, the amber caramel bleeds through the grey cream — the cross-section is genuinely beautiful.

Staff Training — The Nama Donut Service Standard

The Nama Service Philosophy
A nama donut sells itself — visually, aromatically, texturally. But staff who understand what they're serving double the conversion rate from browsers to buyers. Every team member must be able to explain three things about any donut in the case without hesitation: what it tastes like, what makes it Japanese, and why it's made fresh daily. The guest who hears "this is made with sake lees yeast, cold-fermented overnight, and filled this morning" understands they are buying something rare. That understanding justifies ¥980–¥1,100 without a second thought.

Service Scripts — Exact Language for Every Donut

When a Guest Points at Sakura Cream (#01)

"That's our sakura nama donut. The filling is a cold double cream infused with salted cherry blossoms — real cherry blossoms, preserved in salt the way they've been for centuries in Japan. The donut itself is made overnight with sake lees yeast and filled this morning. The flavour is very delicate — lightly floral, barely sweet, cold cream in a very soft dough. It's best eaten today."

Key phrase: "filled this morning" — say this about every donut, every service.

When a Guest Asks "What Is Nama?"

"Nama means raw or fresh in Japanese. Our donuts are called nama because the dough is made fresh every morning — no freezing, no preservatives — and the cream inside is raw, lightly set, almost like a soft custard. They're best eaten within four hours. You'll notice it's softer and lighter than a standard donut — that's the Japanese technique. We use a yeast from sake brewing for the dough."

When a Guest Hesitates at the Price

"These are made from scratch every morning — the dough ferments overnight and we fill them fresh for each service. A nama donut at this level in Tokyo would be ¥900–1,200. Ours are in the same category. Once you try it, I think you'll understand why."

Never apologise for the price. State it confidently and let the product do the rest.

The Upsell — Drink Pairing Script

"If you're having the matcha donut, our Uji matcha latte is made with the same grade of matcha — it's a beautiful pairing. If you prefer something lighter, the yuzu donut actually goes really well with our hojicha. The roasted tea cuts through the richness of the cream nicely."

The Scarcity Principle — Why Selling Out Matters More Than Maximising Units

The most successful nama donut cafés in Japan (Floresta, Cream de la Cream, Mister Donut's Namu range) share one operational philosophy: make slightly fewer than you expect to sell and sell out before closing. A display case with 3 donuts remaining at 5:00 PM creates urgency in every customer who walks in. A display case that is full at 5:00 PM communicates that nobody wanted them. Cap daily production at 110% of your average daily sell-through, not more. The day you sell out at 3:00 PM is your best marketing day — guests who missed out will be back tomorrow when you open. This is the psychology behind Tokyo's most-queued food businesses.

A Michelin-starred restaurant is not defined by its price or its complexity. It is defined by its conviction. The conviction that one thing, done extraordinarily well, with the right ingredients, in the right window of time, served with knowledge and pride, is worth far more than ten things done adequately.

That is exactly what the nama donut represents. Six flavours. Made fresh. Sold within four hours. Explained with pride.
