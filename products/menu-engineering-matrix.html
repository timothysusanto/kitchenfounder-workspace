📖 The Menu Engineering Playbook

⭐ STARS — Protect & Feature

Stars are your best performers — high margin, high demand. Never mess with a star without careful thought. Feature them prominently on the menu (top-right of page, photo, callout box). Train staff to recommend them. Use them as anchors for upsell ("that goes great with our house cocktail").

🐄 PLOWHORSES — Reduce Cost or Upsell

Plowhorses pay the bills — guests love them but they don't make you rich. Your play: (1) Reduce portion size by 5–8% — most guests won't notice. (2) Find a cheaper supplier for a key ingredient. (3) Add a profitable side or upsell ("add a glass of wine for $12"). (4) Raise price by $1–2 and monitor demand — if it holds, you've turned a Plowhorse into a Star.

❓ PUZZLES — Promote or Reposition

Puzzles make great money per plate but nobody orders them. Either the name is confusing, the description is flat, or staff aren't selling it. Fix: (1) Rename it — make it evocative. (2) Rewrite the description — add texture, emotion, origin story. (3) Run it as a special or "Chef's Feature" for 2 weeks. (4) Staff incentive: whoever sells the most Puzzles this week wins $20. If none of that works, it might be a Dog in disguise.

🐕 DOGS — Remove or Radically Rework

Dogs drain your kitchen's time and cost you money. Be ruthless. (1) Remove it from the menu — most customers won't ask for it. (2) If it has sentimental value, keep it but stop promoting it. (3) Check if it shares ingredients with Stars — if yes, keep it for inventory efficiency. (4) Could it be a Bar Snack or Lunch Special at a lower price point where it finds an audience?

🧠 The 3 Golden Rules of Menu Engineering

  • Menu size matters: 7 ± 2 items per category is the sweet spot. More than that and guests can't decide. Decision fatigue = table time up = turns down.
  • Price anchoring works: Put your most expensive item at the top of each section. Everything else looks reasonable by comparison. Your real money-maker is the second-cheapest item — that's where guests' eyes land.
  • Run the matrix monthly: Seasons change, trends shift, suppliers reprice. A Star in January can become a Dog in July. This isn't a one-time exercise — it's a monthly discipline.
