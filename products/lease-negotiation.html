📋 KitchenFounder Lease Negotiation Cheat Sheet

Know what to fight for before you sign anything

⚠️ Watch for these 15 red flags before you sign. Each one can cost you tens of thousands — or trap you in a bad deal. Knowing them is your leverage.
1
HIGH RISK
Personal Guarantee with No Cap
Demand: Cap at 12 months rent max. Unlimited personal liability means your house, savings, and future are on the line if the business fails.
2
HIGH RISK
No Rent-Free Fit-Out Period
Standard: 3–6 months rent-free while you build out. If not offered, negotiate it. You can't open in week one — don't pay for a shell.
3
HIGH RISK
Triple Net (NNN) with No Cap
Demand: Cap CAM (Common Area Maintenance) increases at 5% per year max. Uncapped NNN = unpredictable costs that can spike 20–30% in one year.
4
HIGH RISK
Short Initial Term (Under 5 Years)
Minimum: 5 years to recoup fit-out investment. 5+5 option is ideal. A 2-year lease with no option means you can be removed after spending $200K on fit-out.
5
HIGH RISK
No Assignment Clause
Must have: Right to sell/transfer the lease when you sell the business. Without it, your business is worth far less — or unsellable.
6
MEDIUM RISK
No Exclusivity Clause
Add it: Prevent the landlord leasing adjacent space to a direct competitor. Defines your cuisine/concept category so a burger place can't open next door.
7
HIGH RISK
Demolition Clause
Walk away: This lets the landlord terminate with 6 months notice for redevelopment. You lose everything with minimal compensation. Non-negotiable dealbreaker.
8
MEDIUM RISK
Keep-Open Clause
Negotiate out: Forced to stay open certain hours or days. Bad for restaurants — you need flexibility to close for private events, slow periods, or staff emergencies.
9
MEDIUM RISK
Percentage Rent Above Natural Breakpoint
Check the math: Only fair if base rent is significantly below market rate. Above natural breakpoint means the landlord profits disproportionately when you succeed.
10
HIGH RISK
No Build-Out Allowance (TI)
Standard US: $50–$150 per sqft Tenant Improvement allowance. Fight for it. If they won't move, ask for rent abatement to offset your fit-out spend.
11
HIGH RISK
Restoration Clause
Remove it: Required to return to original condition = $50K+ demo cost at the end. Negotiate that your fit-out can remain — it adds value to the landlord's asset.
12
MEDIUM RISK
Below-Market Options, No Rent Control
Cap the increases: Option periods must have fixed rent increase caps — CPI or a fixed % (3–5%). Open-ended options let landlords set any price at renewal.
13
MEDIUM RISK
Joint and Several Liability (Partners)
Structure carefully: Each partner is liable for the full rent, not just their share. If a partner exits, you're exposed. Consider a holding entity (LLC) as the tenant.
14
LOW RISK
Insurance Requirements Too High
Standard is $1M GL. If they want $3M, push back — this triples your premium. If they insist, negotiate it into the rent-free period or TI allowance as offset.
15
MEDIUM RISK
No Co-Tenancy Clause
Add it: If the anchor tenant (e.g. grocery store, big box) leaves, your foot traffic drops 40%. A co-tenancy clause gives you rent reduction or exit rights if they leave.
💬 Use these scripts word-for-word or adapt them to your voice. The goal: stay professional, stay firm, always leave room for a counteroffer.
1
Opening Counter on Rent
Use when: You've received their asking rent and it's above your budget.
"Thank you for sending the lease terms. We're very interested in the space — it fits our concept well. That said, I've done the numbers, and to make this work at the level of investment we're both expecting, we need to come in at $[your number] per sqft/month rather than $[their number]. I want to be transparent: this isn't about being difficult. At $[their number], our break-even is [X covers/night], which carries more risk than either of us wants. At $[your number], we can invest confidently in the fit-out and build something that becomes a genuine anchor for this block. Can we get on a call to work through this together?"
💡 Always frame your counter around mutual benefit. The landlord wants a strong, stable tenant — use that.
2
Asking for Rent-Free Fit-Out Period
Use when: The lease has no rent-free period and you're building from scratch.
"We'd like to discuss the commencement date for rent. Our fit-out is projected to take [3–4] months, and we won't generate any revenue until we open. Industry standard for a full build-out of this type is 3–6 months rent-free from lease execution. We're asking for [X months] rent-free to align our cash flow with our ability to pay. We're committing to a [5+5 year] term — that's [X years] of reliable tenancy and a fully fitted space at our expense that adds value to your asset. The rent-free period de-risks the start for both of us. What can you do on this?"
💡 Emphasise the long-term commitment and your investment in the space as leverage.
3
Negotiating TI (Tenant Improvement) Allowance
Use when: There's no TI offered in a cold shell space.
"We've reviewed the space and our build-out is estimated at $[X]. For a restaurant in a cold shell, that's within the standard range of $150–$300/sqft for full commercial kitchen fit-out. We're asking for a TI allowance of $[X] — which is $[X]/sqft — to offset the cost of improvements that become permanent fixtures and add value to your property. Alternatively, if TI isn't feasible, we'd consider a rent abatement of $[Y/month] for the first [12] months to achieve a similar economic outcome. We want to make this work. What's the appetite here?"
💡 Always offer an alternative structure (rent abatement) so they can say yes in a different way.
4
Pushing Back on Personal Guarantee
Use when: They want a full-term personal guarantee with no cap.
"We understand you need security, and we're happy to provide a personal guarantee. However, an unlimited full-term guarantee isn't something we can sign — and frankly, it's not standard for a lease of this size. What we're proposing is a personal guarantee capped at 12 months' base rent. This is the industry norm and gives you meaningful security if things go wrong, while not exposing us to unlimited personal liability for a business venture. We're also happy to discuss a security deposit of [1–2 months] as additional comfort. Would a 12-month capped guarantee with a cash deposit work for your team?"
💡 Offering a cash deposit alongside a capped guarantee gives the landlord two layers of security and makes it easier to say yes.
5
Requesting Exclusivity Clause
Use when: There are other vacant units in the same centre/building.
"We'd like to include an exclusivity clause covering our concept category — specifically [full-service dining / casual Asian dining / etc.] — within the [centre/building/block]. We're investing heavily in fit-out and marketing to drive foot traffic to this location. That investment also benefits you and the other tenants. The least we'd ask in return is protection from a direct competitor opening next door. We're not asking for broad exclusivity — just protection within our core category for the term of the lease. This is standard in most commercial leases and costs nothing for landlords to grant."
💡 Keep exclusivity narrow and specific — "no other full-service Asian restaurant" is easier to get than "no food at all."
6
Asking for Assignment Rights
Use when: The lease is silent on assignment or requires landlord consent with no timeline.
"We need the lease to include a clear assignment clause that allows us to transfer the lease to a qualified buyer when we sell the business. We understand you want to approve the incoming tenant — that's reasonable. What we're asking for is that your consent cannot be unreasonably withheld, and that you respond within 30 days of a written request with a qualified buyer's financials. Without this, our business is effectively unsellable — it's a major issue for us and reduces the overall value of what we're building here. This is standard language and protects both parties."
💡 The key phrase is "cannot be unreasonably withheld" — landlords can still protect themselves without blocking your exit.
7
Negotiating Lease Term and Options
Use when: They're offering a short initial term or no option periods.
"We'd like to discuss the lease structure. Our fit-out investment requires a minimum 5-year initial term to achieve a return — anything shorter creates a financial mismatch we can't accept. We're proposing an initial term of 5 years with two further options of 5 years each — a 5+5+5 structure. Rent increases on options to be capped at CPI or 3%, whichever is lower. This gives you a 15-year anchor tenant commitment with a proven operator. That's an extremely valuable outcome for any commercial landlord and will be reflected in your property valuation. Can we get the 5+5+5 structure with capped option increases on the table?"
💡 Long terms are a genuine win for landlords — position it as a benefit, not just a demand.
8
Responding to "Take It or Leave It"
Use when: The landlord or agent claims the terms are non-negotiable.
"I appreciate the directness, and I want to be equally direct: we're serious about this space and want to make it work. But a lease at these terms as-is isn't something we can sign responsibly. We're not asking for the world — we're asking for [list your 2–3 key items]. These are industry-standard requests from any experienced operator. If these are genuinely immovable, I'd need to go back to our board/investors, and I'd also want to understand why comparable spaces in [area] are offering [X]. I don't want to walk away from this — but I also can't sign something I know is wrong. What's the one thing you have the most flexibility on? Let's start there."
💡 "Take it or leave it" is almost always a negotiating position, not a final answer. Stay calm, stay professional, and narrow to one issue at a time.
📖 20 key lease terms in plain English. Don't let jargon blindside you in the negotiation room.
NNN Triple Net Lease
You pay base rent PLUS your share of property taxes, building insurance, and maintenance. Costs can be unpredictable. Always cap annual increases.
CAM Common Area Maintenance
Your share of maintaining shared spaces — parking lots, lobbies, landscaping. Charged on top of base rent in NNN leases. Demand a cap on annual increases (5% max).
TI Tenant Improvement Allowance
Money the landlord gives you to build out the space. Standard in cold shell leases: $50–$150/sqft. Negotiate hard — every dollar they give saves you from borrowing it.
Base Rent
The core rent amount, before CAM, taxes, or insurance. Usually expressed as $/sqft/year or $/month. This is your anchor number in negotiations.
Percentage Rent
When you pay a % of gross revenue above a certain threshold on top of base rent. Can be fair if base rent is low. Always check the natural breakpoint before agreeing.
Natural Breakpoint
The revenue level at which percentage rent kicks in, calculated as: Base Rent ÷ Percentage Rate. If your breakpoint is $500K and you do $600K, you pay % on the extra $100K.
Option Period
Your right (not obligation) to extend the lease at the end of the initial term. Always negotiate renewal options with capped rent increases. Without them, the landlord can price you out.
Assignment
Transferring your lease obligations to a new tenant (usually when you sell the business). Landlords can require approval — but ensure consent cannot be unreasonably withheld.
Subletting
Renting part or all of your space to another party while you retain the lease. Different from assignment — you're still responsible if the subtenant defaults. Often restricted.
Personal Guarantee
You personally guarantee the lease obligations. If your LLC fails, the landlord can pursue your personal assets. Always cap this — 12 months rent is standard and defensible.
Certificate of Occupancy
A government document certifying your space is safe and legally approved for its intended use (food service). You can't legally open without it. Budget 4–8 weeks to obtain.
Permitted Use
What you're legally allowed to do in the space under the lease. Make sure it covers your concept fully — "restaurant and bar" vs "cafe only" matters enormously. Get it wide.
Exclusivity
A clause preventing the landlord from leasing nearby space to a direct competitor. Define your category carefully and narrowly so it's enforceable without being overbroad.
Co-Tenancy Clause
Protects you if a key anchor tenant (a major store that drives foot traffic) leaves the centre. Gives you rent reduction or early exit rights. Critical in shopping centres or malls.
Demolition Clause
Landlord's right to terminate the lease early for redevelopment. Typically 6 months notice. This is a dealbreaker — avoid it, or negotiate substantial compensation (2+ years rent).
Force Majeure
Suspends lease obligations during unforeseeable events (pandemics, natural disasters). COVID made this famous. Ensure it's in your lease and covers cessation of trading, not just property damage.
HVAC
Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning systems. Who maintains them? Who replaces them when they fail? Clarify in the lease — a failed HVAC unit can cost $15,000+. Negotiate landlord responsibility.
Gross Lease
You pay one fixed amount; the landlord covers all operating expenses. Predictable but typically higher base rent. Less common in commercial — more in office buildings.
Modified Gross Lease
You pay base rent plus some (not all) operating expenses. Landlord and tenant split the responsibilities. The most negotiable structure — define exactly what each party pays.
Net Lease
You pay base rent plus one or more of: property taxes, insurance, maintenance. Single Net = taxes. Double Net = taxes + insurance. Triple Net (NNN) = all three. Each adds cost and unpredictability.
